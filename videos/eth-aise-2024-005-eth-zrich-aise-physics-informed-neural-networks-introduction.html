First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Teaching Neural Networks to Solve Physics Equations

The question it answers: How can we use neural networks to solve the differential equations that describe everything from swinging springs to fluid flow, without needing massive datasets of pre-computed solutions?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you have a mass on a spring that bounces and slows down over time. You know the rule of physics that governs its motion (the damped harmonic oscillator equation), but solving that rule by hand is hard. What if a neural network could learn that rule and predict where the mass will be at any time?

Partial differential equations (PDEs) are the mathematical language scientists use to describe how things change in space and time. From how atoms vibrate (Schrödinger equation) to how weather patterns evolve (Navier-Stokes equations), these equations encode physical laws. Traditionally, we solve them with grid-based numerical methods like finite difference or finite element methods—we chop up space into a mesh and solve the equation at each grid point, one small step at a time.

Here's the key insight: a neural network is just a flexible mathematical function. Given some input (say, a time value), it can produce an output (the position of the mass at that time). So instead of using grids, why not ask a neural network to directly learn the solution function? The network takes in coordinates (like space and time) and outputs what the physical quantity is at that point.

But the trick is: how do we train the network? We can't just feed it thousands of labeled examples—that would require running expensive simulations first. Instead, we use the differential equation itself as our teacher. This is where 'physics-informed' comes in. We construct a loss function (what the network tries to minimize during training) with two parts. First, a 'boundary loss': the network must match our known starting conditions—if we know the mass starts at position one with zero velocity, the network must predict exactly that at time zero. Second, a 'physics loss': we check whether the network's prediction actually satisfies the differential equation at many random points throughout the domain.

To compute the physics loss, we need the derivatives of the network's output. This is where automatic differentiation becomes essential. Modern deep learning approaches like PyTorch can compute derivatives of neural networks through the chain rule automatically. For a damped oscillator, we need the first and second derivatives (acceleration and velocity). We take those derivatives of the network, plug them into the left-hand side of the differential equation, and see if we get zero—which is what the equation says should happen for any true solution.

The full loss is a weighted combination: boundary terms (ensuring correct starting conditions) and physics terms (ensuring the equation is satisfied everywhere). During training, we compute gradients of this combined loss with respect to the network's weights using backpropagation. With each training step, the network adjusts its parameters to better satisfy both the boundary conditions and the underlying physics. What emerges is a smooth, continuous approximation to the solution—not a grid of numbers, but an actual function you can query at any point in time.

Unlike finite difference methods, which give you the solution only at grid points, a physics-informed neural network gives you a functional approximation: you feed in any time value you want, and out comes the predicted displacement. This is 'mesh-free'—no grid required. The network learns to encode the entire dynamics of the system in its weights. The real cost comes from computing higher-order derivatives. Each derivative roughly doubles the computational cost, so training requires significant computation compared to forward passes alone.

The method generalizes: swap out the damped oscillator for Burgers' equation (fluid flow), or heat equations, or wave equations. Set up the boundary and initial conditions for your problem, define the physics loss by plugging the network's outputs into your PDE, and train. The network learns to satisfy your physics everywhere simultaneously, rather than marching through time step by step. This works because the loss function directly embeds the law of physics as an inductive bias—the network is incentivized to learn solutions that obey the rules.

Of course, limitations exist. The network still needs to be flexible enough to represent the true solution (approximation error), you need enough training points in the domain to constrain the physics everywhere (estimation error), and the optimization landscape may be complicated, so the training algorithm might not reach a true minimum (optimization error). But the power is clear: with just the differential equation and boundary conditions—no data—you can teach a neural network to solve it.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Neural networks are mathematical functions that can learn to approximate solutions to differential equations by training them to satisfy the physics equations at many points in the domain.
  • The physics loss: instead of matching data, we compute what the differential equation predicts and penalize solutions that don't obey it (make the residual zero).
  • Automatic differentiation (backpropagation through multiple layers) lets us compute the derivatives the network needs without hand-deriving them—we just apply the chain rule recursively.
  • Mesh-free: the network gives you a continuous function anywhere in space-time, not just at grid points, making it flexible for many downstream uses.
  • Boundary conditions fix uniqueness: without them, infinitely many functions satisfy the differential equation; boundary/initial conditions select the one physical solution.
  • Computational cost grows with derivative order: a second derivative (needed for many PDEs) costs roughly 2–2.5 times a forward pass, making training time a real bottleneck.

Worked intuition: Consider a damped harmonic oscillator: a 1 kg mass on a spring with spring constant 24 and friction coefficient 4, starting at position 1 with zero velocity. The exact solution is a cosine wave that decays exponentially. To train a physics-informed neural network: (1) Create a simple neural network with 3 hidden layers, 32 units each, that takes time as input and outputs position. (2) At the boundary (time = 0), compute the network output and its time-derivative. Loss term 1: penalize if output ≠ 1 or if derivative ≠ 0. (3) At 30 random time points across 0 to 1, compute first and second time derivatives of the network (using autograd twice). Loss term 3: compute m·(d²u/dt²) + c·(du/dt) + k·u (the residual) and penalize if it ≠ 0. (4) Combine losses as L_total = L_boundary + 0.01·L_physics. (5) Backpropagate and update weights. After a few hundred iterations, the network curves to match that exponential decay perfectly, without ever seeing the true solution formula—just the rule it obeys.

Where it breaks down: Physics-informed networks assume you know the differential equation and boundary conditions exactly. If your physics is wrong or incomplete, so is the solution. They struggle with sharp discontinuities (shocks) because neural networks are smooth. Very high-order PDEs (fifth derivative, sixth derivative) become computationally intractable due to exponential cost of nested differentiation. Extrapolation beyond the training domain is poor—the network has only learned within the region and collocation points it saw, not general physics intuition. Balancing the loss terms (boundary vs. physics) is an art; wrong weights can lead to satisfying boundaries but violating physics, or vice versa. And unlike traditional numerical methods, we don't have a priori convergence guarantees or error bounds.

Takeaway: Physics-informed neural networks flip the approach: instead of discretizing space and marching forward in time, embed the law of physics directly into what you're training the network to minimize. This lets you solve differential equations from first principles—just the equation and boundary conditions—with no data required, producing a smooth function anywhere you query it. It's elegant, but the real cost is computational: derivatives are expensive, and balancing terms requires tuning.
