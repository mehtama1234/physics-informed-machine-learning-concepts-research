First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: How AI Learns to Predict and Design Biology: From Protein Folding to Drug Discovery

The question it answers: What does a protein actually do once it's built, and how can machine learning help us design new proteins and drugs that work the way we want?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you have a long pearl necklace of 20 different bead types. The order of beads is written in your DNA. Once the necklace is made, it doesn't stay flat—it twists and folds into a specific 3D shape in your cell. That folded shape is everything: it determines whether the necklace becomes a machine that cuts things, a glue that sticks things together, or a lock that opens when the right key arrives. Making a new protein that folds the right way is like threading a new necklace in the dark, hoping it knots itself into exactly the shape you need. AI is learning to predict—and design—these knots.

Proteins are the machinery of life. They're built from a fixed alphabet of 20 amino acids—think of them as 20 distinct LEGO bricks. These bricks chain together in a precise order (that order comes straight from your DNA), and once assembled, the chain doesn't stay linear. It crumples, twists, and folds into a compact 3D structure. This structure is *everything*. The same 20 bricks assembled in different orders and folding differently will produce completely different jobs: one arrangement becomes an enzyme that cuts molecules, another becomes antibody that finds and marks invaders, a third becomes a signal that tells a cell to divide. The problem: predicting what shape a protein will fold into from just knowing the bead order is absurdly hard. A tiny 100-bead protein has 20^100 possible sequences—that's more possibilities than atoms in the universe.

For decades, getting protein structures meant months of lab work (X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance) to solve one protein at a time. Then in 2020, a system called AlphaFold 2—built by DeepMind using neural networks—won a major challenge by predicting protein structures so accurately that the 3D coordinates of atoms were off by less than 1 Angstrom (the width of a hydrogen atom). This wasn't a small incremental improvement; it was a breakthrough that let people predict structures instantly on computers. The secret wasn't inventing a new physics formula. Instead, AlphaFold learned from examples: it was trained on thousands of known protein structures and learned to recognize patterns in how amino acids at far distances in the sequence come close together in 3D space.

AlphaFold works by a clever trick: it borrows the idea from natural language—treating protein sequences like sentences. Just as 'the cat sat on the mat' has grammar and word order that constrains what comes next, protein sequences have hidden patterns. To train on this, the model takes a protein sequence and masks (hides) some amino acids with [MASK] tokens, the same trick used in language models like BERT. The model then tries to predict what the hidden amino acid should be. This forces the network to learn deep facts about proteins: it must understand geometry (what shapes can fit in a 3D pocket), chemistry (which atoms repel or attract), and evolution (which positions never change across species because they're critical). When a model can fill in the blanks correctly, it has absorbed genuine protein knowledge.

Once a neural network learns these patterns by predicting masked positions, the knowledge gets encoded in hidden layers—a compressed 3D map of the protein in the network's memory. This representation is then refined: you add a small neural network head that takes these learned representations and outputs predicted 3D atomic coordinates. This is called ESMFold. It runs 60 times faster than AlphaFold because it skips costly database searches. The tradeoff is modest: it's slightly less accurate on side chains (the flexible dangly parts of amino acids) but nearly as good overall. The point is that you don't need to reinvent the algorithm for each task. You learn a *foundation*—a general-purpose understanding of 'what proteins are'—and then plug task-specific heads on top.

Now that we can predict structures easily, the next frontier is *using* 3D structure for downstream predictions. The challenge: proteins don't live alone. A protein folds around a small molecule drug, and how tightly they hug each other determines whether the drug works. This is the binding affinity problem—predict the strength of interaction between a drug and its target protein. Early methods used grid-based 3D convolutions: divide space into tiny cubes (voxels), mark which atoms are where, and slide convolutional filters across like in image recognition. This works but is wasteful: many voxels are empty, and the number of parameters explodes cubically as you increase resolution. There's also the rotation problem: if you physically rotate the protein, the grid coordinates change, so the model must learn the same thing many times over.

The insight is to stop forcing irregular biology into a rectangular grid. Instead, represent biology as a *graph*—a network of connections. Atoms are nodes. Bonds between atoms are edges. Each node and edge can carry information (atom type, charge, bond type). This matches how biology actually is: unordered, irregular, but fundamentally relational. A graph neural network (GNN) processes this by message passing: each atom asks its neighbors 'what are you?' and updates itself based on the replies. Formally, a node gathers embeddings from its neighbors, aggregates them (usually by summing), passes through a learned neural network, and outputs an updated embedding. Do this for multiple layers, and a node eventually 'knows' about atoms several steps away in the graph structure. This is like convolution for non-Euclidean (non-grid) data. The beautiful part: graphs are automatically rotation-invariant because the network only cares about which atoms are bonded, not their spatial orientation.

A concrete example: discovering new antibiotics. A team trained a simple graph neural network on just 2,500 drug molecules, of which only 120 were known antibiotics. The model learned to recognize graph patterns that correlate with antibacterial activity. Then they scored every molecule in a large drug repurposing database. The top 99 predictions? 51 actually killed bacteria in the lab. The bottom 63? Only 2 did. Even more remarkable: the top hit, helicin, was structurally unlike any known antibiotic—the model didn't just copy patterns it saw; it generalized to novel chemistry. This is AI doing actual drug discovery, not just pattern matching.

The full picture joins these pieces: You use a foundation model (like ESM, trained on 250 million protein sequences via masked prediction) to embed protein sequences into learned representations that capture biological knowledge. When you need to predict binding affinity, you convert the protein and drug into a graph, initialize node features with these learned embeddings, run message passing through graph layers, pool the result, and predict. The elegance is reuse: the protein language model embeddings plug into many downstream tasks. And the whole chain—sequence to embedding to structure to graph to prediction—is differentiable and trainable end-to-end. The future models (AlphaFold3, RosettaFold AllAtom, OpenFold) extend this to predict not just single proteins but entire molecular assemblies (protein-protein, protein-DNA, protein-drug) by treating all molecules as a unified input.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Protein fold is destiny: The 3D shape a protein twists into is determined only by its amino acid sequence (via weak chemical attractions and repulsions). This shape controls all its function—but predicting which shape emerges from a sequence is like guessing which knot a long rope will tie in the dark.
  • Foundation models as universal translators: Training a model to fill in masked positions in protein sequences (or SMILES strings for drugs) forces it to learn deep structural and functional truths. The learned representations then transfer to dozens of downstream tasks (structure prediction, binding, function classification) without retraining from scratch.
  • Graphs beat grids for biology: Proteins and molecules are fundamentally graphs (atoms are nodes, bonds are edges). Graph neural networks process this naturally without wasting computation on empty space, and they're automatically invariant to rotation and translation—properties a grid representation must learn painfully.
  • Message passing as local reasoning: In graph neural networks, each atom updates its understanding by listening to its neighbors' embeddings. Multiple layers let information diffuse—a distant atom's influence reaches through the chain. This is how the network learns that an atom deep in the structure matters for the binding pocket at the surface.
  • Recycling refines predictions: AlphaFold doesn't predict once and stop. It loops: the predicted structure goes back into the core model, which refines the prediction again using the new geometric context. This iterative refinement is like sketching a drawing, looking at it, and erasing to improve.
  • Self-distillation scales learning: AlphaFold used a clever bootstrap: first train on known experimental structures, then use that partially trained model to predict structures for 350,000 unlabeled protein sequences, mix the real and predicted structures together, and retrain the model from scratch. This vastly expands the training set without human lab work.
  • Modular AI pipelines: Modern drug discovery chains multiple models: sequence → embedding (ESM), embedding → structure (AlphaFold/ESMFold), structure + drug → graph → binding score (GNN). Each module reuses outputs from earlier steps. This is how AI tackles the combinatorial explosion of the protein design space.

Worked intuition: Consider predicting whether a small-molecule drug (say, aspirin) will bind tightly to its target protein (cyclooxygenase, which causes inflammation). Step 1: Convert the protein sequence (say, 400 amino acids: MVLSPAD...KQEQEV) through a foundation model (ESM trained on 250M protein sequences). Each amino acid position gets a learned embedding—a vector that captures 'this position is usually hydrophobic,' 'this is near the active site in similar proteins,' etc. Step 2: Convert the 3D structure (atoms and how they're bonded) into a graph: nodes for each atom, edges for bonds. Attach learned embeddings to protein atom nodes. Step 3: Run graph neural network message passing: the oxygen atoms on aspirin message their neighbors 'we're polar, we want to hydrogen bond,' the carbon backbone atoms message 'we're hydrophobic.' This information flows through the protein graph—neighboring atoms learn context. After 3-4 message-passing layers, atoms in the binding pocket have absorbed information about the arriving drug. Step 4: Global pooling aggregates all node and edge information into a single representation. Step 5: A small neural network reads this and outputs a binding score (high = tight binding = good drug). The whole pipeline is differentiable, so the foundation model embeddings, message-passing weights, and scoring head all improve together during training on known protein-drug pairs.

Where it breaks down: Biological systems are messier than toy problems. First, proteins are dynamic—they vibrate and wiggle; AlphaFold predicts a static structure, missing conformational changes that matter for binding. Second, the training data is skewed: we have structures for proteins that fold easily in a lab, biasing models toward 'cooperative' proteins. Rare, intrinsically disordered proteins are underrepresented. Third, generalization is brittle: a model trained on antibacterial activity in E. coli might fail on Gram-positive bacteria or fungal targets because the biological context shifted. Fourth, for drug discovery, predicting binding affinity is one step; predicting whether the drug will reach the target organ, avoid liver metabolism, or not trigger immune toxicity requires a whole other chain of models. Finally, sequence-based methods (like ESM) cannot see 3D structure directly—the model infers it from patterns. For pathological cases where sequence diverges from structure (like intrinsically disordered proteins or assemblies with weak components), the foundation model must guess what the structure 'really does,' and that guess can be wrong. Graph neural networks on 3D structures are better but require expensive 3D data; sequence methods scale to billions but lose structural detail.

Takeaway: Modern AI doesn't solve biology by inventing new physics or rules. It learns emergent patterns from examples: a neural network trained to fill in masked amino acids absorbs enough structural knowledge to predict 3D folds and binding affinities. The trick is to represent biology faithfully (sequences for evolution, graphs for structure, embeddings as learned knowledge) and chain simple, reusable components (foundation models → structure → graphs → predictions) so that insights learned on one task transfer to many others. The frontier isn't a single 'AlphaFold' for everything; it's building modular, differentiable pipelines where each step—sequence embedding, structure prediction, interaction prediction, toxicity prediction—reuses and refines the knowledge from the step before.

Main Points Discussed

  • Chemical and biological objects often have structure that is not a simple table.
  • Graphs can represent atoms, bonds, residues, or interactions.
  • The useful prediction depends on preserving the structure tied to the measured property.

First-principles point: If the property depends on structure, the model must carry the structure.

Learner takeaway: Before learning model names, ask what object is being represented and what property is being predicted.

Do not worry about this yet: Do not try to learn all chemistry and biology at once. Track object, structure, property, and test.
