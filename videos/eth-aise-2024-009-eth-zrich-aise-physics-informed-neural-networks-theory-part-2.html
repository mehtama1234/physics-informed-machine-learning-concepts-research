First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: When Do Physics-Informed Neural Networks Work? And When Do They Fail?

The question it answers: Physics-informed neural networks promise to solve intricate scientific equations by training on the equations themselves rather than on labeled data. But when does this strategy succeed, and more importantly, what kinds of problems cause it to break down?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine trying to figure out how heat spreads through a metal rod just by knowing the physics law (heat flows from hot to cold), without having any direct measurements of the temperature at each point. That's what a physics-informed neural network does—it learns by enforcing that the physics law is satisfied everywhere, computing its own 'errors' from the equations rather than from labels.

Physics-informed neural networks, or PINNs, solve a fundamental challenge in science and engineering: many problems are governed by differential equations (mathematical rules describing how things change), but we lack enough measured data to train the network the traditional way. Instead of showing the network lots of examples of inputs and correct outputs, we give it the differential equation itself and ask the network to find a solution that obeys that equation. The network computes something called the PDE residual—basically, how badly the equation is violated at any point—and treats that violation like an error signal to learn from.

To make this work in practice, the lecturer lays out a approach with three objects we must track: the training error (how well the network satisfies the equation at points we evaluate), the residual (the mathematical mismatch between the network's solution and the true physics), and the total error (how far the network's answer is from the real solution). The key insight is that these three are connected through a chain: if the training error is small, then the residual will be small; and if the residual is small and the underlying physics has certain stability properties, then the total error will also be small. But each link in this chain depends on assumptions that may or may not hold.

For problems with nice, smooth solutions (like the heat equation, which models how temperature spreads), this chain holds beautifully. The lecturer proves a simple one-dimensional case step-by-step: if you have a perturbed solution (what the network predicts) and the only perturbation comes from a small residual, the difference from the true solution stays small over time. This works because the heat equation is stable—small changes in inputs produce small changes in outputs. This stability is used through an inequality called Grönwall's inequality, which essentially says 'nothing grows faster than an exponential.' Because of this, if the residual is small, error accumulation is controlled. The beauty of random sampling in high dimensions is that the residual can be estimated to shrink as the square root of the number of quadrature points (sampling locations), independent of dimension, which is why PINNs can tackle 100-dimensional problems that traditional methods choke on.

The lecturer shows concrete examples where PINNs succeed spectacularly. Radiative transfer problems—which involve radiation bouncing around in seven dimensions—can be solved to 2% accuracy in one hour, whereas a traditional finite element code took 15 days. The heat equation itself and the Black-Scholes equation from option pricing (which prices European options) both follow the same theoretical pattern and work reliably. These linear equations (or nearly linear) have small constants in the error bounds, so the chain holds tightly.

But then the lecturer shifts to the caveats: the Navier-Stokes equations of fluid dynamics. In principle, if the solutions are smooth, the same approach applies. However, in practice, the constant multiplying the small residual depends on the vorticity (the curl of the velocity field, measuring local spinning motion). When a fluid flow becomes turbulent, vorticity becomes very large and intricate, so even a tiny residual gets multiplied by a huge constant, making the bound useless. The lecturer shows examples: a simple vortex works fine, but as vorticity becomes increasingly complicated, the network's approximation gets blurry and fails. Critically, no PINN can yet handle water at realistic Reynolds numbers (10 to 100 million); they max out around 500.

The viscous Burgers equation illustrates another failure mode: it has both nonlinear transport (fast-moving features get compressed) and diffusion (smoothing). The network's error bound depends on the gradient of the velocity. When diffusion is very weak (viscosity near zero), the gradient blows up, and the network fails spectacularly to approximate solutions. When the same equation is run in a regime where diffusion dominates, the gradient stays bounded, and the network succeeds. This is not a flaw in the theory—it reflects real physics: when diffusion is weak, the true solution itself develops sharp features that are hard to approximate with a smooth neural network.

The third barrier, only partially addressed in the lecture, is the training error itself: can we actually find a good solution to the optimization problem? The lecturer notes that even a small network (4,000 parameters) is a 4,000-dimensional optimization problem. Gradient descent must start from a random initialization and navigate a landscape full of hills and valleys to reach a point where the equation is satisfied. For many problems shown (like the heat equation in 100 dimensions), this works—training errors reach 10^-3 or better. But there is no general guarantee, and this remains an open question in machine learning.

Key ideas in plain words

  • The PDE residual is an error signal built from the equation itself, not from labeled data. Small residuals are necessary but not sufficient; they must propagate to small solution errors through the stability properties of the equation.
  • Linear equations (heat, diffusion, radiative transfer) are much friendlier to PINNs than nonlinear ones because the error bound constants are independent of the solution; the chain holds tightly.
  • High-dimensional problems are not automatically hopeless because random sampling costs scale as the square root of the number of points, independent of dimension—a huge advantage over grid-based methods.
  • Nonlinear phenomena like large vorticity, shock formation, or sharp gradients can blow up the error constants in the theoretical bounds and cause the network to fail in practice. The mathematics correctly predicts where PINNs will struggle.
  • The optimization problem—actually finding network parameters that make training error small—is treated as a 'opaque model' in the first part of the lecture and analyzed more deeply in the second part using neural tangent kernel theory. Good local minima often exist, but reaching them is not guaranteed.
  • A physics-informed network is only as good as the physics it's given. If the true solution behaves in a way that violates the assumptions (smoothness, bounded derivatives, stability), the network will reflect that failure honestly.
  • PINNs are most reliable on moderate regimes: smooth, stable equations with reasonable solution behavior. They excel on linear or weakly nonlinear problems in high dimensions where traditional methods fail.

Worked intuition: Consider the one-dimensional heat equation: ∂u/∂t = ∂²u/∂x². The true solution u satisfies this exactly. Suppose a neural network u-hat satisfies the same initial and boundary conditions but has a tiny residual r everywhere: ∂u-hat/∂t = ∂²u-hat/∂x² + r. Define the difference w = u - u-hat. Then w satisfies ∂w/∂t = ∂²w/∂x² - r with zero initial and boundary conditions for w. Now multiply both sides by w and integrate over space: the left side measures how the squared error grows in time, the right side has two terms. The diffusion term (∂²w/∂x²) is negative after integration by parts (boundary terms vanish), which means it suppresses error growth. The residual term r is positive but small. Grönwall's inequality then says the cumulative effect is that the total squared error at time t is bounded by the integral of r² over all time and space. Since r is small, the error w stays small. This is the core argument, and it works only because the heat equation is stable—diffusion fights against error growth.

Where it breaks down: PINNs fail when: (1) the true solution itself has sharp features, discontinuities, or very steep gradients that a smooth neural network cannot represent well, even with infinite capacity; (2) nonlinear effects like vorticity or shock formation in the solution are large or increasing, which makes the error bound constant explode; (3) the equation is in a regime where smoothing (diffusion) is weak relative to sharpening (transport), allowing gradients to blow up; (4) the optimization landscape is such that gradient descent gets stuck in a poor local minimum far from any solution with small training error (this can happen but is not fully characterized). Additionally, there are problems with quadrature accuracy in very high dimensions if sampling is not done carefully, though random sampling helps. The approach is honest: where PINNs struggle, the theory predicts they will struggle.

Takeaway: Physics-informed neural networks are a effective tool when three conditions align: the underlying equation is stable or nearly linear, the true solution is smooth and well-behaved, and the optimization succeeds in finding a good training minimum. Under these conditions, they can solve high-dimensional problems where traditional methods fail. But they are not magic—they inherit the properties of both the physics (equations with large nonlinearities or instabilities transfer those difficulties to the network) and the learning problem (optimization is hard and not always successful). Knowing which problems are suitable requires understanding both the mathematics of the equation and the fundamentals of how neural networks learn.
