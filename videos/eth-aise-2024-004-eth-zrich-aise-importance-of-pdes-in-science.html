First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Why Partial Differential Equations are the Language of Physical Science—and What Breaks When We Try to Solve Them

The question it answers: From atoms to galaxies, how do we describe what happens in nature? And why is predicting even simple systems so impossibly expensive on a computer?

Something concrete to hold onto: Think of heat spreading through a room. Right now, temperature is different in different places and changing over time. There's no simple formula to tell you the temperature at any point and moment—but there is a rule (an equation) that governs how heat flows.

Start with a basic observation: the world works by rules. When you heat one side of a room, warmth spreads. When a star explodes, gravitational waves ripple outward. When a drug dissolves in water, concentration changes. All these phenomena follow laws—physical laws. Herbert Simon, a Nobel laureate who studied intricate systems, pointed out that science works by a simple method: given a description of a natural phenomenon, find the differential equations that govern it. A differential equation is a mathematical relationship that connects a quantity (like temperature) to how it changes—either over time or across space. A partial differential equation, or PDE, handles quantities that change in multiple ways at once: in time, and in different directions of space.

Here is the key insight: almost every physical system in nature is described by a PDE. Take something as simple as temperature in a room. At any point in space, at any moment in time, there is a temperature. The room is three-dimensional, so temperature varies as you move left or right, forward or backward, up or down. It also varies as time passes—warmer during the day, cooler at night. The physical law (Fourier's law, named after the mathematician Joseph Fourier) says that heat flows from hot to cold, and the rate of flow depends on how steep the temperature gradient is—how quickly temperature changes as you move through space. When you write down this law mathematically, you get a PDE relating the temperature and its spatial and temporal derivatives (rates of change). The same pattern appears everywhere: the Schrödinger equation, which describes electrons in atoms, is a PDE. The Einstein field equations of general relativity are PDEs. The Navier-Stokes equations, which govern fluid flow in the atmosphere, oceans, and aircraft wings, are PDEs. Reaction-diffusion equations describe how patterns form on animal wings. The scale ranges from subatomic particles to the entire universe—30 to 50 orders of magnitude in size—yet all are described by PDEs. This is the beauty and power of mathematics: one language captures vastly different phenomena.

Now comes the hard part: we cannot solve most of these equations by hand. There is no pen-and-paper formula. Even Einstein himself, who wrote down his equations in 1915, could only solve them in very special cases. For 175 years, from roughly 1757 (when Euler wrote his fluid equations) until the late 1920s, scientists tried to solve PDEs by finding clever tricks—Taylor expansions, asymptotic approximations, matching coefficients. Then everything changed. A physicist named Lewis Richardson realized that you could use a computer (even the analog computers of that era) to approximate solutions numerically. The idea is simple: instead of solving the equation everywhere and at every time, you pick a grid of discrete points in space and time. At each point, you approximate the derivatives (rates of change) using finite differences—simple arithmetic operations. You replace the continuous problem with a discrete one. For example, in a one-dimensional domain, instead of asking 'what is the temperature everywhere?', you ask 'what is the temperature at these 100 points?' The time derivative becomes a simple ratio: how much the temperature changes from one time step to the next. The spatial derivative becomes another ratio: how much temperature changes from one grid point to the next. You then march through time, using the old values to predict the new values. This method is 100 years old and works extraordinarily well. Modern computers using finite difference, finite element, or spectral methods can simulate supernova core collapse in 3D, cumulus cloud formation, tsunami generation, and airflow around rockets—all successfully tested against reality.

But there is a hidden cost that makes these classical methods break down. To get a small error in your answer, your mesh (grid spacing) has to be small, and your time steps have to be tiny. The time step must scale as the square of the mesh size (a stability requirement called the Courant condition). The error in your answer also scales as the square of the mesh size. This means the computational cost has a brutal scaling law: in one dimension, you need roughly 1/mesh_size mesh points; in two dimensions, you need (1/mesh_size)² points; in three dimensions, (1/mesh_size)³ points. The time cost scales similarly. Putting it together: the total computational cost grows as (1/mesh_size)^(5/2) in three dimensions. If you want a 1% error—that is, error of 1/100—you need 100^(5/2) = 100,000 times more computation. And that is before you account for the constant factors in the scaling law. In higher dimensions, the situation becomes absurd. For a 10-dimensional problem (common in quantum mechanics and some inverse problems), the scaling factor is dimension^6, or 10^6 = 1 million times more compute just for the extra dimensions. For 100 dimensions, it is 100 million times. This is the curse of dimensionality: traditional numerical methods break down in high dimensions.

Real-world costs illustrate the pain. Boeing spends approximately 2 billion dollars per year on computational budgets—much of it for simulating airflow around aircraft wings to optimize wing shape. This is a 'many-query problem': you start with a wing design, simulate the flow to compute lift and drag (solve the Navier-Stokes equations once), then adjust the shape slightly and repeat—hundreds or thousands of iterations. Each PDE solve is expensive; doing it hundreds of times is prohibitively expensive. Similarly, in seismic imaging, you have sensors on the ground detecting echoes from sound waves sent into the earth. From these echoes, you want to infer the geological structure (the speed at which waves travel through rock). This is an inverse problem: you know the wave equation and the sensor outputs, but you must infer the wave speed. The standard approach is iterative: guess a wave speed, solve the forward problem, check if it matches the data, then adjust your guess. This takes 100 to 10,000 iterations of the PDE solver. For uncertainty quantification in turbulent flows, you need statistics (mean, variance) over many independent samples, not just one solution. A high-resolution simulation might cost 100 node-hours on a supercomputer per sample, and if you need 1,000 samples to get stable statistics, that is 100,000 node-hours total—equivalent to roughly half a million Swiss francs if you had to pay out of pocket.

There is another, more subtle problem: missing physics. Yes, the Navier-Stokes equations perfectly describe fluid motion, but in climate modeling, you also need to account for vegetation, land absorption and reflection, ocean circulation, and radiation. The physics of how a forest affects climate, or how soil properties influence local weather, is either approximate or completely unknown. These are empirical relationships without first-principles derivations. Traffic flow in a city can be modeled roughly like fluid flow, but there is no fundamental theory. Flow in the brain involves biological circuits we do not understand. In all these cases, the underlying physics is either expensive to simulate, incompletely known, or both. You have lots of data but incomplete equations. This is the problem the course will address: can we use machine learning not to replace physics entirely, but to augment incomplete physics with data, or to dramatically speed up expensive simulations?

The core challenge distills to three issues. First: computational cost grows exponentially with problem dimension, making high-dimensional and many-query problems infeasible. Second: grid generation is hard for irregular domains, and the method requires re-discretizing if the domain changes. Third: when physics is missing or approximate, traditional numerical methods are useless—you cannot solve an equation you do not fully know. The entire course is built on the premise that we should combine classical physics (the PDEs we do know, because they are reliable) with modern machine learning (to learn the parts we do not know, or to accelerate the expensive parts). The next lecture will introduce physics-informed neural networks, which encode known PDEs directly into a neural network's loss function, so the network learns to be a solution to the PDE while also learning from data.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Partial differential equations (PDEs): equations relating a quantity and how it changes in space and time. They are the universal language of physics—atoms, weather, fluid flow, gravity all speak in PDEs
  • Discretization: breaking a continuous domain into a grid of discrete points and approximating derivatives using finite differences. This allows computers to solve PDEs, and it has worked for 100 years
  • Curse of dimensionality: the computational cost of traditional numerical methods scales exponentially with the dimension of the problem. A 10D problem costs roughly 1 million times more than a 1D problem for the same accuracy
  • Many-query and inverse problems: real applications often require solving the PDE hundreds or thousands of times (e.g., aircraft design, seismic imaging, optimization). Each solve is expensive, making the total cost prohibitive
  • Missing physics: some systems (climate, traffic, biological flows) are described by incomplete equations, or the equations are known but so expensive to solve that alternatives are needed
  • Scaling laws: error scales as (mesh_size)², time step must be proportional to (mesh_size)², and total compute cost grows as (1/mesh_size)^(d+1) in d dimensions. This is a hard mathematical fact, not a limitation of implementation
  • Why machine learning matters: when physics equations are expensive, incomplete, or require many evaluations, machine learning can learn surrogates from data, fill in missing pieces, or accelerate expensive solves

Worked intuition: Imagine you want to predict how temperature spreads in a one-meter-long rod after you heat one end. You discretize into 100 points. To get a 1% error in your answer, you need roughly 100 mesh points, which is fine. But now imagine a 3D room—a cube 10 meters on a side. If you use 100 points per side, you have 100³ = 1 million grid points total. The time step must be (1/100)² = 0.0001 relative time units. To simulate, say, one hour, you might need 100,000 time steps. Per time step, you do a handful of arithmetic operations per point, so 1 million × 100,000 = 100 billion operations. A modern CPU does ~10 billion operations per second, so this takes ~10 seconds—manageable. But now increase resolution to 1,000 points per side (more realistic for engineering). You have 1 billion grid points and 1 million time steps, or 10^15 operations—10,000 seconds, nearly 3 hours on a single processor. If you want 0.1% error instead of 1%, you need 10 times finer mesh, which is 10^4 times more operations: 100,000 seconds, nearly a day. This is why supercomputers exist, and why a single simulation can cost half a million dollars. Machine learning can learn a function that maps initial conditions directly to final temperature distribution, bypassing the expensive marching-in-time process, if it can learn from data.
