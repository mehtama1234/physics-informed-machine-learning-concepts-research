First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Learning operators with fixed bases and learnable basis networks

The question it answers: When you want to solve a physics problem of any size or resolution, how do you learn the operator that maps inputs to solutions without getting stuck to one particular grid size?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine predicting the weather at 100 sensor locations. You measure temperature at those points, predict the temperature six hours later at the same points, and keep doing this. You never care about the specific resolution—just the values at your fixed sensor network. But what if you want the learned pattern to work at different numbers of sensors, or different domains altogether?

The core challenge is that we live in an infinite-dimensional world—functions don't come as single numbers, they're defined everywhere in a continuous domain. But we can't store infinite information, so every numerical method and every neural network operator must project functions down to finite dimensions (basis coefficients), do math with those finite numbers, and project back up. The key insight is that if this projection-math-reconstruction loop is *representation equivalent*—meaning you get the same answer whether you work in continuous space directly or jump down to discrete coefficients and back—then you're learning a true operator, not just a map between grids. If you mess up this equivalence, you introduce *aliasing error*, which breaks your ability to generalize to new resolutions.

Traditional numerical methods like finite differences, finite elements, and spectral methods all follow this exact pattern. For finite differences, you pick a grid, write out a stencil like the heat equation discretized as (u-next-minus-u-now) equals some combination of u-left and u-right and u-center, then march forward in time. For spectral methods, you expand everything in sines and cosines (for periodic problems), multiply by learnable coefficients in Fourier space, and transform back. Finite elements do the same with polynomial basis functions. Every method is really saying: project down to basis coefficients, do work there, reconstruct.

So the question becomes: what if we replace the numerical scheme (which uses first-principles physics equations) with a *learnable* neural network? We still need to choose a basis to project onto. The simplest choice is a **fixed basis**—say, Fourier (sines and cosines). You take any input function from any grid, compute its 64 Fourier coefficients, feed those numbers into a neural network, get out 64 output Fourier coefficients, and transform back. Since you always work with 64 numbers, it doesn't matter if the input came from a fine grid or coarse grid. This is called a **spectral neural operator** and it is *representation equivalent* by construction—you avoid aliasing as long as your basis is chosen well.

But spectral neural operators have a tight constraint: you must supply the basis yourself. If your problem is periodic, use Fourier. If it's on a sphere, use spherical harmonics. This requires expert knowledge. What if instead, you could *learn the basis from data*? Enter **DeepONet** (from the 1990s, modernized recently). The idea is elegant: split learning into two parallel neural networks. One network, the **trunk net**, takes any point in your domain (say, x=0.5) and outputs one number per basis function (these are your learned basis functions). The other network, the **branch net**, takes your input coefficients (just point values at your sensor locations) and outputs coefficients to weight the basis functions. Then you sum: output = sum of (learned basis k) × (learned coefficient k). You're simultaneously learning both what the basis functions should look like and what weights they should have.

This sounds smart—like principal component analysis (PCA) but with neural networks instead of linear eigenvectors. And it does have a nice property: it's still representation equivalent in the sense that you always work with a fixed set of sensors, so different grid resolutions just get projected to the same finite set of sensors and both get the same treatment. But there's a subtle problem: unlike PCA, where you're guaranteed that with infinite data your learned functions will actually be an orthogonal basis, DeepONet learns basis functions and coefficients *simultaneously without any guarantee they'll form a true basis*. The theory says it's okay to work with *frames* (like bases but messier), and there's no mathematical reason to force orthogonality when you don't know the right inner product anyway.

To see the trouble, consider the Burgers equation—a simple nonlinear wave equation where smooth initial data (like random noise) evolves to develop sharp shocks (like sonic booms). Train DeepONet with a thousand examples. You get ~30% error. Train Fourier neural operator (FNO) on the same problem and you get ~4% error. More striking: theory proves that for a simple linear advection equation (just u(t,x-ct) shifting a bump to the left), DeepONet needs roughly *quadratic* difficulty in 1/error to hit a target accuracy, while FNO needs only *logarithmic*. To get 1% error, DeepONet might need 10,000 parameters while FNO needs just 10. This happens because DeepONet squashes all the problem structure into one fixed set of learned basis functions—you lose vital high-frequency information if your learned basis doesn't capture it. Fourier, by contrast, already has all the harmonic content available; you just need to weight it properly.

The deeper lesson is that a good basis must respect the physics of the problem. Fourier works beautifully for smooth, band-limited functions. But forcing everything through one learned basis, hoping the training data teaches the network to invent the right basis, turns out to be brittle. The spectral neural operator approach—where you supply structure-preserving bases like Fourier or wavelets—is more honest: you're saying 'I know my problem has this structure, let me exploit it.' The trade-off is real: fixed bases can't adapt to every problem, but learning bases from scratch with no constraints is even worse. The open challenge is designing operator architectures that preserve mathematical structure (like resolution independence, no aliasing) while remaining flexible and accurate in practice.

What unites all these methods is the humble diagram: function space → projection to basis → finite dimensional processing (whether numerical method or neural network) → reconstruction back to functions. Whether you use finite differences, spectral methods, Fourier neural operators, or DeepONet, you're all playing the same game—projecting to manageable numbers, manipulating them, projecting back. The question isn't whether to project (you must), but what basis to project onto and how to learn parameters that respect the physics.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Representation equivalence: A neural operator is truly resolution-independent only if the answer you get is the same whether you work in continuous space or project to discrete basis coefficients and back—if this diagram commutes, you have no aliasing error.
  • Spectral neural operators use a fixed, known basis (like Fourier) to squeeze functions to finite numbers, feed those to a neural network, and reconstruct. You sacrifice flexibility but gain guaranteed structure-preservation.
  • DeepONet learns both basis functions (trunk network) and coefficients (branch network) together from data, like a nonlinear version of PCA. Theoretically neat, but empirically fragile because learned bases may not capture problem structure.
  • For smooth problems, Fourier basis needs only logarithmic difficulty; DeepONet needs quadratic. This gap reveals that learning a basis from scratch often loses information faster than exploiting a good known basis.
  • All numerical methods (finite differences, finite elements, spectral) and neural operators share the same architecture: encode to basis → do work → decode from basis. The difference is whether the middle step uses physics equations or a learned network.
  • The open challenge: design operators that preserve the commutative diagram and generalize across resolutions while learning from data without forcing all structure through one fixed bottleneck of learned basis functions.

Worked intuition: Consider predicting one time step of the advection equation (a wave just shifting left at speed c). With 1000 training samples of bumps at different positions being transported, FNO learns: 'multiply high frequencies by a weight, low frequencies by a different weight, transform back.' Fourier already has all modes available, so it just needs to find two or three numbers per mode. DeepONet, by contrast, has to learn: 'here are my 64 basis functions (which the network invents from scratch), and here are the coefficients for each input.' If the 64 learned basis functions don't happen to include a representation of the high frequencies in the solution, training can't recover them from 1000 samples alone. FNO's basis is built-in and complete; DeepONet's basis is made-to-order but starved of data to learn from.

Where it breaks down: Fixed bases like Fourier fail when your problem has structures that don't fit the basis—like shock waves in Burgers equation, which are discontinuous and don't compress well in sine/cosine. DeepONet fails when your learned basis is too small to capture the problem's difficulty, which is exactly what happens with moderately sized training sets; you lose expressivity. Neither is a silver bullet. Resolution-dependence remains: even spectral operators may need more basis functions (more parameters) to represent finer solutions. And there's a hard trade-off—imposing strong structure (like orthogonality) on learned bases requires knowing the right metric/inner product ahead of time, which you don't for unknown operators.

Takeaway: When learning operators, always ask: what basis am I projecting onto, and does it match the problem's physics? A good known basis beats learning one from scratch when data is limited. But neither fixed nor learned bases fully escape the curse of dimensionality—the real frontier is architectures that grow their basis adaptively or work directly in function space without collapsing to discrete coefficients.
