First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Why Machine Learning Models Fail on Biology, and How Diffusion Generates New Proteins and Drugs

The question it answers: Machine learning has crushed records on proteins (AlphaFold), yet when we try to predict drug binding or design novel proteins, models hallucinate and fail. Why does generalization break, and how can we flip the problem—instead of predicting what's natural, generate what's new?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you have a huge library of protein binding measurements, but many are actually the same binding event measured twice (hidden duplicates). Your model trains on this redundant mess and reports 90% accuracy on test data that's already in the training set. You celebrate—but your drug fails in the lab because the model learned nothing about the *physics* of binding, just memorized the library. This is the honest crisis in biology today.

The first big shock is a simple one: biological datasets are built on *human contributions*. Researchers deposit protein structures into the Protein Data Bank (PDB), DNA sequences into UniProt, chemical measurements into public databases. This is amazing, but it creates hidden redundancy. The same protein-drug pair gets crystallized multiple times, sometimes with tiny variations. When you split training and test data *randomly*, you might accidentally put both versions of the same pair on opposite sides. The model learns the pair itself, not the underlying physics of how drugs bind proteins.

A real example: the PDBbind database is widely used to train models that predict binding affinity—how tightly a drug sticks to a protein. It has 20,000 labeled protein-drug assemblies. When researchers checked, they found **600 training assemblies were nearly identical to test assemblies** (same ligand atoms, overlapping protein structures, identical measured binding energy). When you remove this leakage—just filter out the redundant pairs—the best published models drop from state-of-the-art accuracy to barely better than a simple lookup table that says: "find the 5 training examples most similar to this test case and average their answers." This alone proves these models do not understand protein-drug physics; they memorized the library.

A second failure mode is blindness to biological preprocessing. Protein thermostability (how hot before it unfolds) is a valuable prediction task for designing industrial enzymes. The standard dataset comes from UniProt sequences. But UniProt stores the *full genetic sequence*—which includes signal peptides that get *cleaved off* after the protein is made. So the training data is noisy: your model sees structures that do not exist in cells. When the model learns "stability," it's learning stability of a fantasy protein, not the real one. The model confidently predicts nonsense because it never saw the basic biology.

A third failure: co-folding models (like AlphaFold 3, which predict protein-drug binding in one step) interpolate brilliantly on in-distribution examples but fail adversarially. When researchers mutated the binding pocket to glycine (unreactive amino acids that eliminate all interaction), the models still predicted the original binding pose—ignoring the fact that the pocket is now a dead void. When they packed the pocket with bulky phenylalanine side chains (physically blocking the drug), models still predicted the drug in its original position, causing steric clashes (atoms overlapping, impossible). The models learned global patterns in protein structure and sequences from the training data, not local physics of interactions.

The root issue: machine learning on biology conflates **pattern recognition** with **understanding**. Models can be very good at interpolating (filling in gaps within the training distribution) but fail to extrapolate (reason about novel scenarios). And they have no built-in cost for violating physics. A model that predicts impossible atom overlaps gets rewarded for matching the training target, not penalized for being unphysical. To fix this, we need better benchmarks that test adversarial reliability, we need to encode chemical and physical constraints *into* the model (not just the data), and we need curated datasets with explicit care to avoid leakage and redundancy.

But instead of just predicting what's natural, researchers flipped the problem: **generate new proteins and drugs from scratch**. This is where generative models—specifically diffusion models—enter. A diffusion model works by: (1) taking real data (protein structures, drug molecules), (2) gradually adding noise until the data is unrecognizable random points, (3) training a neural network to reverse this process step by step, (4) once trained, sampling random noise and letting the network "denoise" it into new realistic data. For proteins, this means starting with random orientations of amino-acid backbones and denoising them into plausible new folds never seen before.

RF Diffusion generates new protein backbones by representing each amino acid as a 3D frame (a translation and rotation describing the N, C-alpha, and C atoms). It adds Gaussian noise to the coordinates and rotations, gradually smearing structured protein geometry into random soup. The neural network learns to recover realistic protein backbones from this noise. The architecture is clever: it's based on RosettaFold (a structure predictor), fine-tuned for diffusion. At each denoising step, the network doesn't predict the next increment but directly predicts what it thinks the final ground-truth structure is—a prediction called x0-hat. It also uses "self-conditioning": feeding both the current noisy coordinates *and* the previous step's prediction to the next step. This improves performance. Generated proteins are diverse, most have no similarity to natural proteins (showing genuine novelty), and experiments show they are stable and expressible—they fold correctly in the lab.

Protein sequence design is the second half of the puzzle. RF Diffusion gives you a backbone (the carbon chain, no side chains, no amino-acid identity). To finish the design, you need to pick which amino acid goes at each position. ProteinMPNN solves this inverse-folding problem: given a fixed backbone structure, design a sequence that will fold into that structure. It represents the backbone as a graph: nodes are amino acids (C-alpha atoms), edges connect each node to its 32 nearest neighbors, encoding spatial locality. Node features start as zeros; edge features are initialized as all pairwise distances between backbone atoms. A graph neural network (message-passing) runs on this, allowing each amino acid's embedding to absorb information about its neighbors' spatial context. Then an autoregressive transformer goes through the sequence one amino acid at a time, sampling the best amino acid at each position given the structure and previously chosen amino acids. Training data is natural proteins with known sequences and structures—the model learns to rediscover the original sequence from just the backbone. It achieves ~50% sequence recovery, which sounds low until you realize many positions in a protein can tolerate multiple amino acids. The key test is *structure validation*: design a sequence, feed it to AlphaFold, and check if the predicted structure matches your target backbone. Real experiments show high fidelity.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Data leakage is invisible in biology. Datasets have hidden redundancies (same protein-drug pair measured twice, or similar pairs undetected because structure is intricate). Random train-test splits fail. Models appear accurate but are memorizing the library, not learning physics. Simple lookup tables beat deep neural networks on these compromised benchmarks.
  • Models learn patterns, not physics. Co-folding models fail adversarially (when you remove a binding pocket, they still predict the drug in the pocket; when you block it, they predict steric clashes). They interpolate brilliantly within the training distribution but extrapolate nowhere. No built-in cost for violating chemistry or physics.
  • Diffusion models flip the task: instead of predicting natural structures, *generate* new ones. Start with random noise, train a network to denoise step-by-step toward realistic structures. Once trained, sample random noise → denoise → novel proteins no one has seen before, yet stable and expressible.
  • Protein design is a two-step pipeline. RF Diffusion generates a realistic backbone (the carbon chain shape, unadorned). ProteinMPNN then designs a sequence of amino acids that will fold into that backbone (inverse folding). Structure validation uses AlphaFold to confirm the designed sequence actually folds into the target backbone.
  • Graph neural networks capture spatial locality. ProteinMPNN represents a backbone as a contact map (graph of nearby atoms) with edge features encoding distances. Message-passing spreads spatial context from neighbors. A transformer then autoregressively samples amino acids, choosing what fits each position given the structure.
  • Generalization via novelty, not memorization. Generated proteins are diverse, many share <50% structural similarity to natural proteins. Experiments confirm they fold and function. The model genuinely learned protein geometry, not just memorized the training set.
  • Conditioning lets you steer generation. RF Diffusion can be guided to match a desired topology (like a beta barrel) or incorporate a functional motif (e.g., the p53 binding helix, to design a drug that competes for MDM2). External potentials nudge the denoising trajectory toward design goals.

Worked intuition: Let's trace the ProteinMPNN story end-to-end. You start with an experimentally solved protein (say, MDM2, a cancer-related protein that binds p53). You strip off all side chains, leaving just the backbone—a bare skeleton. Now you run ProteinMPNN: it looks at the 3D backbone geometry, encodes nearby amino acids as graph neighbors, and asks: "what amino acids fit this scaffold?" It goes position by position, sampling amino acids one at a time. For a buried hydrophobic position deep inside the protein, it might predict "leucine"—a bulky, oily amino acid—because hydrophobic cores are what keep proteins stable. For an outer surface position, it might pick a polar, charged amino acid that tolerates water. Once it designs all ~100 positions, you have a new sequence that shares only ~40% identity with the original but should fold into the same 3D shape. To verify, you feed the sequence to AlphaFold. AlphaFold sees only letters, not the original backbone. It folds the sequence *ab initio* (from scratch). If the folded structure closely matches your target backbone, you win: you've designed a protein. If it diverges wildly, the design failed. The geometry-aware graph representation was crucial—it let ProteinMPNN learn that "at position 47, surrounded by these atoms at these distances, leucine is a good fit," instead of just memorizing "position 47 was leucine in the training set."

Where it breaks down: Diffusion-based design is effective but not a magic wand. Generated proteins are validated *in silico* (computer prediction with AlphaFold)—lab experiments are expensive and slow. Some designed proteins fail to fold, express, or remain stable when synthesized. The models handle *backbone* generation and *sequence* design separately, which is cleaner than end-to-end but misses cross-talk (amino acids and backbone shapes coevolve in nature). Conditioning on motifs works, but intricate design goals (enzyme with a new active site plus novel cofactor binding) are harder. Generative models are also data-hungry—they need large, clean datasets. With noisy or leaky data (the problem discussed at the start), even diffusion can fail. And generative models don't guarantee *functionality*—a designed protein might fold correctly and still do nothing useful biologically. You still need to test binding, activity, toxicity in the lab. The hype around AI protein design can overshadow this: the computers can generate beautiful structures; engineering useful function is the harder, longer slog.

Takeaway: Machine learning on biology fails not because the methods are weak but because hidden redundancy in datasets lets models memorize instead of learn physics. When we flip the task—from *predicting* natural structures to *generating* novel ones—diffusion models can learn the geometry of proteins deeply enough to design new ones that actually fold and work. But real-world design still requires careful data curation, physics-aware benchmarks, and wet-lab validation. The practical path forward is honest evaluation: avoid data leakage, test adversarial reliability, integrate chemical priors, and combine computational design with experiment.
