First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Why We Need AI to Solve the Laws of Nature: The Problem with Classical Methods

The question it answers: Scientists and engineers understand the laws governing everything from heat diffusion to aircraft flight through mathematical equations. But once you write the equations down, how do you actually solve them? And why does it become impossibly slow as problems get bigger or more intricate?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you want to design a faster airplane wing. You know the physics: the Navier-Stokes equations describe how air flows. A computer can solve these equations, but on a realistic 3D aircraft, it takes 500 node-hours of supercomputer time for just one simulation. If you need thousands of designs to optimize the shape, you are stuck. The calculation is correct but computationally impossible.

For 300 years, scientists have described natural phenomena using differential equations—starting with Newton. These are mathematical statements that say how things change over space and time. A tsunami? Modeled by fluid equations. A cloud formation? Conservation of mass, momentum, and energy. A cancer tumor spreading? Diffusion equations. Quantum mechanics, chemical reactions, financial options—all follow this pattern. The unifying principle is simple: write down the differential equations that capture the physical laws, then solve them.

But here is the catch: most of these equations are impossible to solve by hand. You cannot write down a formula for the answer. Even simple-looking equations like the heat equation (which Fourier derived to cool hot cannon barrels) require clever approximations. So since the 1920s, scientists have used numerical methods: finite differences, finite elements, spectral methods. These divide space and time into a grid of points, replace calculus derivatives with simple arithmetic, and let computers iterate step by step. This works beautifully—it is how we simulate weather, design planes, and understand the sun.

The fatal weakness emerges when you scale up. To get 1% accuracy (the engineering standard), the computational cost grows exponentially with dimensionality. In 1D (one direction), it might take 1,000 units of compute. In 2D (a flat surface), 10,000. In 3D (the real world), 100,000. Jump to 100 dimensions (common in finance or quantum systems), and the method collapses entirely. The cost is proportional to roughly 10^(dimension+2), which is insurmountable for realistic problems.

Even in three dimensions, the wall is immediate and practical. A high-resolution simulation of the Navier-Stokes equations (the physics of fluid flow around a car or aircraft) needs billions of grid points. Each time step takes minutes. Simulating it all the way to a steady state takes hours or days on a supercomputer. Now add the design problem: you want to tweak the shape to reduce drag. Each tiny design change requires a full re-simulation. An aircraft company needs thousands of simulations to find a good design. At 500 hours per simulation, it becomes years of computation. No designer can wait that long. They settle for conservative, sub-optimal designs because exploring the space is impossible.

A second, subtler problem emerges: the classical workflow assumes you know the equations. But in many real systems—biology, geology, medicine—you do not fully know the governing laws. You have data: observations, measurements, time series. But you must guess at the equations that explain them, then solve those guesses. If the guess is wrong, the simulations are wrong. And even if your equations are right, they might be so stiff (some parts change much faster than others) or so chaotic (tiny changes in initial conditions cascade into wildly different outcomes) that numerical methods become unreliable. You cannot confidently optimize a design or make a prediction when the uncertainty is baked into the method itself.

This is the gap the course addresses: instead of the slow, expensive cycle of write-equations → solve-numerically → iterate, what if you could learn a fast surrogate? A surrogate is a neural network or other learned function that acts like the equations but evaluates in milliseconds instead of hours. Train it once on a handful of expensive simulations, then use it to evaluate thousands of designs instantly. Or, if you do not know the exact equations, let the network infer them from data. This is the core of physics-informed machine learning: marry the physics you do know (or can measure) with the learning power of deep networks to solve the intractable and the uncertain.

But there is a catch to that too: a naive neural network trained only on data will interpolate between the simulations you fed it. Outside that range, it fails. A physics-informed network bakes physical laws (conservation of mass, energy, momentum) directly into the network architecture or loss function, so it respects the physics even where there is no training data. It becomes a learned equation-solver that is fast, accurate, and generalizable—if you build it right. The rest of this course is learning how to do exactly that.

The key insight is this: the classical world divides into domains. Physics and engineering live in the world of equations and numerical solvers. Machine learning lives in the world of data and learned patterns. For 300 years, scientists solved by hand and machine at the expense of time. For the last 20 years, they have tried pure learning, ignoring physics, and gotten brittle systems. The frontier—and the whole point of this course—is combining them: physics-informed learning, where symmetries, conservation laws, and differential equations guide the network, and data helps it learn the details and uncertainties the equations miss.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Differential equations (math statements of how things change) are the universal language of science and engineering—everything from stars to proteins follows them.
  • Numerical methods approximate the answer by replacing continuous space-time with a discrete grid and doing arithmetic instead of calculus—fast for simple 1D problems, impossibly slow in high dimensions.
  • The curse of dimensionality: as dimension grows, the cost to achieve fixed accuracy explodes exponentially; 3D fluid simulations take days, 100-dimensional finance problems become untractable.
  • Design and optimization amplify the cost: one simulation per shape × thousands of candidate shapes means years of supercomputer time, forcing engineers to be conservative and use heuristics instead of rigorous exploration.
  • Physics-informed machine learning: train a neural network on a small set of expensive simulations and let it learn to predict the outcome (or the dynamics) in milliseconds; embed the physics rules directly into the network so it respects them even outside the training region.
  • The frontier combines two worlds: the rigor and interpretability of physics-based equations with the flexibility and data-driven learning of neural networks, to solve problems that are too expensive classically and too uncertain for pure learning alone.

Worked intuition: Take the simplest case: the heat equation in one dimension (a metal rod, cold at the ends, with initial temperature distribution). Classically, you divide the rod into (say) 100 points, divide time into small steps, and update each point in parallel based on its neighbors. Forward 1,000 time steps takes a second on a laptop. Now, a neural network trained on 100 of these simulations (varied initial conditions) learns the operator: it takes the initial temperature distribution and predicts the temperature at any later time, in milliseconds. You can now cheaply answer "what if" questions: if I change the initial profile, what happens? If I want to find an initial condition that reaches a target state, optimization is now tractable. The network is fast because it amortizes the learning across many problems. But it also needs the physics baked in (e.g., temperature cannot violate maximum principles, no energy appears from nowhere) so it does not invent unphysical solutions when it extrapolates to untested regions.

Where it breaks down: Pure numerical methods fail on high-dimensional problems (100+ dimensions) where the grid becomes impossibly large; they also struggle with chaotic or stiff systems where tiny input changes cause huge output swings or where fast/slow timescales coexist. Pure neural networks trained only on data often fail when you extrapolate beyond the range they saw: they are brittle and unphysical outside the training envelope. Physics-informed networks are much better but still limited if the physics you bake in is incomplete or wrong, or if the data is sparse or noisy in critical regions. The method also assumes you can generate or afford sufficient training data; if a single simulation costs weeks, amassing 1,000 training samples may be impractical. Finally, even with fast surrogates, some inverse problems (inferring internal structure from boundary measurements) and uncertainty quantification (propagating noise and unknowns through a design space) remain genuinely hard and require careful probabilistic modeling.

Takeaway: The whole pipeline of science and engineering—observe nature, write equations, solve them, optimize designs—works brilliantly but breaks under scale and difficulty. Machine learning can patch this by learning fast surrogates from expensive simulations, but only if we teach the network the physics it must obey. This course is about how to do that: building neural networks that respect the laws of conservation and symmetry, that solve PDEs faster than classical methods, that infer missing physics from data, and that scale to real engineering design and discovery.

Plain title: What the course is trying to do

Watch for: The course is about using AI for science and engineering without losing the scientific question. Listen for the shift from ordinary prediction to claims about fields, equations, molecules, materials, and designs.

Main Points Discussed

  • AI is treated as a tool for scientific and engineering work, not as a magic answer box.
  • The course keeps returning to differential equations because many scientific systems are rules for quantities that change over space and time.
  • A learned model is useful only when the scientific quantity, the evidence, and the rejection test are named.

First-principles point: Start with the thing a scientist needs to know, then ask what evidence can carry that answer.

Learner takeaway: Do not start by memorizing method names. Start by asking what quantity is unknown and what would prove the answer is not ready.
