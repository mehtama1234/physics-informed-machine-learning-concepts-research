First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Hybrid Workflows: Putting Neural Networks Inside Your Algorithms

The question it answers: Instead of replacing your whole simulation with a neural network, what if you inserted a neural network into just the slow or uncertain part of your algorithm and kept the rest? How would you train it?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you have a cheap, fast approximation of an airplane wing's lift force (using a simple method) but it's not very accurate. Instead of throwing it away and training a neural network from scratch, you could keep the fast method and train a neural network to learn and fix its mistakes at each step.

We've seen two main ways to use neural networks for science so far. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) treat the network as the entire solution—it learns to satisfy the differential equation directly. Operator learning trains a network to map entire functions to other functions, like predicting pressure from permeability. Both work, but both have costs: PINNs are expensive to train because you have to satisfy the equations everywhere, and operators need a lot of labeled training data from expensive simulations.

The real question is: when should you actually use a neural network in your scientific work at all? Neural networks are fast to run once trained, and they can learn incredibly nonlinear patterns. But they need lots of training data, they're hard to optimize, they don't always generalize well, and they're opaque models—you can't easily understand what they learned. So the advice is simple: use neural networks only where your workflow is slow or where you don't understand the physics. Don't replace everything.

This leads to hybrid workflows. Instead of throwing away your traditional algorithm (like a finite element solver, a simulation, an inverse algorithm), embed a neural network inside it at the bottleneck. The network learns only the part you're unsure about or that's slowing you down. The simplest version is residual modeling: you have a physical model that makes a prediction, then you train a network to learn the difference between that prediction and the true answer. The final prediction is the physical model's output plus the neural network's correction.

Here's why residual modeling is easier than pure neural networks: if your physical model already gets you 90% of the way there, the network only has to learn the remaining 10%—a much simpler task than learning from scratch. This is also much more interpretable. You can look at what the network learned and ask: is it learning a small nonlinear correction? Is it capturing some physics I missed? Plus, you can pre-compute all the residuals (the differences) from your training data before training the network, so the training loop is just standard supervised learning.

But residual modeling is just the start. You can embed neural networks much deeper into your algorithm. For example, in a fluid flow solver that steps forward in time, the slowest part is usually solving a huge sparse matrix at each time step. Instead, you could put a network there that learns to approximately solve it faster. Or you could run a low-resolution (coarse) simulation quickly, then use a network to predict the high-frequency corrections the coarse version missed. The key move is: open up your algorithm, find where it struggles, and insert a learnable component there.

To train these embedded networks, you need to think about what data they see and what they should learn. If you're learning a residual correction at each time step, your training data is pairs of (low-fidelity step output, high-fidelity step output). But there's a subtle trap: when you run your trained algorithm in the future, the network will see low-fidelity outputs that have already been corrected by the network itself from previous time steps. That's different from its training data, which were pure low-fidelity outputs. This distribution shift can break the network. The fix is to unroll your algorithm over many time steps during training, so the network sees the full sequence including its own previous corrections, and the loss matches the final output to the true high-fidelity answer.

The big enabler that makes all this work is automatic differentiation (autodiff). This is a tool built into modern deep learning libraries that computes exact gradients of any program—not just neural networks, but any algorithm you write. If you define your hybrid algorithm in a differentiable way (using PyTorch or JAX), you can compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the network's weights all the way through the full algorithm, including matrix solves, loops, and every numerical step. This is remarkably effective: your traditional algorithm becomes like a recurrent neural network, where the network parameters are shared across time steps and you're unrolling it and backpropagating through it to train.

The practical result: you get a model that is as accurate as running a very fine, expensive simulation, but as fast as running a coarse, cheap one. Real experiments on fluid flow show this works—a neural network trained to correct a low-fidelity solver can achieve high-fidelity accuracy at low-fidelity speed. Of course it's not perfect. The network can't generalize to completely different problems or initial conditions it never saw. If you run it way outside its training regime, the errors compound and it fails. But for the regime you trained it on, it's a genuine hybrid that keeps the best of both worlds.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Hybrid workflow: Instead of replacing your algorithm with a neural network, embed a network at the bottleneck—wherever your algorithm is slow or uncertain—and keep the rest intact.
  • Residual modeling: Train a network to learn the difference between an approximate model and the true answer, so it only learns the correction, not the whole complicated function.
  • Distribution shift problem: A network trained on single time steps sees different inputs than it will during deployment after it's made previous corrections; fix by unrolling and training end-to-end.
  • Automatic differentiation: A tool that lets you compute exact gradients through arbitrary code including loops, matrix solves, and traditional numerical algorithms, not just neural network layers.
  • Unrolling as training: Unroll your hybrid algorithm over many time steps, backpropagate through all of them, and train the embedded network to correct the full multi-step trajectory, not just individual steps.
  • Physical domain insight: Embedding known physics keeps the model stable and interpretable, lets you work with limited data, and reduces the network's job to learning what you don't understand or what's expensive.

Worked intuition: Imagine predicting the force on an airplane wing. You have a fast Heuristic Smith panel method that's cheap but gives rough answers. You run a few expensive full CFD (fluid dynamics) simulations to get ground truth. For each CFD run, you also run the panel method on the same input. You compute the residual (CFD result minus panel result) for thousands of time steps or wing designs. Now train a neural network: input is the panel method's prediction plus the wing shape and flying conditions, output is the residual to add back. Once trained, your hybrid model evaluates the panel method (fast), feeds it to the network (fast), and returns panel result plus network output. On test cases, it's more accurate than panel alone and faster than full CFD. The network isn't solving aerodynamics from scratch—it's learning the correction that the cheaper method misses.

Where it breaks down: The network only works well when inputs are similar to what it saw during training. If you train on aircraft with 10-degree angle of attack and later test at 40 degrees, the network will perform poorly because it's outside its training distribution. Also, if you use it far into a simulation beyond what you trained on, errors compound: a bad correction at step 1 becomes input to step 2, amplifying mistakes. You also can't easily change the problem's resolution or parameters—if you trained on a 32x32 mesh and test on 128x128, it will fail. The method works in a fixed regime, not as a general solution.

Takeaway: Automatic differentiation lets you learn inside your existing algorithms by inserting neural networks at the bottlenecks and backpropagating through the whole thing. You get hybrid systems that are as fast as coarse methods but as accurate as expensive ones—but only for the regime and variations you trained on.
