First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Designing Entirely New Proteins from Scratch Using Generative AI

The question it answers: How can we use AI to design novel protein backbones and amino acid sequences that have never existed in nature, rather than just tweaking existing ones?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you have a LEGO structure you want to build, but instead of following an instruction manual from LEGO, you want to invent an entirely new structure that has never been made before. First you'd design the overall shape (backbone), then you'd figure out what specific colored bricks (amino acids) would lock together correctly to hold that shape. That's what RF Diffusion and ProteinMPNN do for proteins.

Proteins are chains made of 20 different amino acid building blocks, and they fold into intricate 3D shapes that determine what they can do. Last week you learned about protein engineering—modifying existing natural proteins to improve them. But today we flip the question: what if evolution has only explored a tiny corner of all possible proteins? If you consider all the ways you could arrange amino acids, nature has only tried a minuscule fraction. So the big opportunity is designing completely novel proteins from scratch, ones that have never been seen in nature, to do specific jobs we care about. This is called de novo (from scratch) protein design, and it's fundamentally different from engineering because you're not starting from a real protein—you're creating something entirely new.

To understand how to generate new proteins, we need a clever trick borrowed from image generation. Think about making images using diffusion models: you take a real image, gradually add random noise to it over many steps until it's completely unrecognizable (pure static), then train a neural network to reverse the process—to undo the noise step by step and recover real images. Once you learn this 'denoising' process, you can start with pure random noise and run the reversal process to create a brand new image that looks realistic but was never in your training data. The same idea works for proteins: take real protein structures, gradually scramble them with noise, train a network to unscramble them, then use the reverse process to generate new protein backbones.

But proteins have a special structure we can exploit. Every amino acid's backbone (the part common to all 20 types) consists of three atoms: N, C-alpha, and C, arranged in a rigid triangle. A whole protein backbone is just a chain of these triangles strung together. RF Diffusion represents a protein backbone as a series of translations (shifts in position) and rotations for each triangle. To add noise, you perturb the C-alpha coordinates randomly in 3D space, and you apply Brownian motion (random wiggling) to the rotation matrices. This gradually turns a neatly aligned protein backbone into a jumbled mess of randomly positioned and oriented triangles. Then you train a network to reverse this—to predict the original clean structure from the noisy version.

RF Diffusion doesn't start from scratch; it inherits knowledge from a structure prediction model called Rosetta Fold (similar to AlphaFold). Rosetta Fold learns how to predict structures from sequences by studying real proteins, and it gets very good at understanding protein geometry. RF Diffusion fine-tunes Rosetta Fold's architecture for the denoising task. Here's the clever part: RF Diffusion uses 'self-conditioning.' At each denoising step, the model takes the current noisy coordinates and predicts what the final clean structure should be. It then blends the current noisy version with this prediction and feeds that blend to the next step, along with the prediction itself. This helps the model learn better because each step has more information—both the current state and a 'peek' at where it should go. The result is that RF Diffusion can generate highly diverse protein structures completely unconstrained, and experiments show many of them fold stably into soluble proteins in the real world.

But generating a backbone is only half the problem. A backbone is just the skeleton with no amino acid identities—it's like having a 3D shape with no information about which of the 20 amino acid types sits at each position. To complete the design, you need a second model called ProteinMPNN that solves the inverse folding problem: given a fixed 3D backbone structure, predict which amino acid sequence will fold into it. ProteinMPNN represents the backbone as a graph where each node is a C-alpha position (an amino acid location) and edges connect nearby amino acids (each amino acid links to its 32 nearest neighbors). Node features start as zeros, but edge features encode geometric distances between the triangles of nearby amino acids. A message-passing neural network runs graph convolutions (information flows between connected nodes and edges) three times, allowing each amino acid to 'learn' about its neighborhood. This generates embeddings—learned representations—that encode the spatial context each position.

The second part of ProteinMPNN is an autoregressive decoder. It predicts amino acids one at a time, moving through the sequence, asking at each position: 'Given the structure and the amino acids already predicted, what should come next?' For each position, it computes a probability distribution over all 20 amino acids and samples one. Training uses proteins from the Protein Data Bank (experimentally determined structures): remove the side chains (keeping only the backbone), then train the model to rediscover the original amino acids. The model achieves about 50% sequence recovery—it gets the amino acid right half the time. You might think that's low, but it's actually impressive because many positions have multiple valid amino acids that fit the structure; the real test is whether the predicted sequence folds back into the intended structure.

The workflow is: design a backbone with RF Diffusion (conditioning on any motifs you want to keep), use ProteinMPNN to find an amino acid sequence that fits it, then validate with AlphaFold to check that the sequence actually folds into your designed backbone. In the lecture's demo, when they took the MDM2 protein, removed its side chains, and asked ProteinMPNN to rediscover the sequence, the model generated sequences with only 44-47% identity to the original. But when AlphaFold folded those novel sequences, they matched the input structure almost perfectly, showing that very different sequences can encode the same 3D shape. This is effective for protein engineering applications like cancer therapy: they designed proteins that hold a specific binding motif (a helix from p-53) in a new backbone, making them candidates to interfere with MDM2 in cancer cells.

Both models work because they're built on deep understanding of protein physics embedded in the networks they're derived from. RF Diffusion inherits from Rosetta Fold's knowledge of how structures work. ProteinMPNN uses graph neural networks because proteins are fundamentally geometric objects—which amino acids interact depends on proximity in 3D space, not sequence order. By using graphs, the model learns that spatial neighborhoods matter more than sequence position. The approaches are also controllable: RF Diffusion can be guided at each denoising step by adding forces that push coordinates toward desired topologies (helices, beta sheets, overall folds). ProteinMPNN can keep parts of a sequence fixed while redesigning the rest. Together, they provide a practical recipe for de novo protein design that's creative but constrained by real biophysics.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Diffusion models generate new structures by learning to reverse a noising process: train on adding noise to real structures, then sample new ones by starting with pure noise and denoising (step-by-step cleaning).
  • Protein backbones can be represented compactly as translations and rotations of rigid triangles (N, C-alpha, C atoms), which makes noising and denoising geometrically natural.
  • RF Diffusion generates novel protein backbones by inheriting knowledge from Rosetta Fold (a structure prediction model), fine-tuning it to denoise noisy protein coordinates.
  • The inverse folding problem (predicting amino acid sequence from a 3D backbone) is solved by ProteinMPNN using graph neural networks, where nodes are amino acid positions and edges encode geometric distances; message passing lets each amino acid learn about its neighborhood.
  • Autoregressive sequence generation means predicting amino acids one at a time, conditioning on the structure and previous predictions, allowing the model to build sequences that fit the fixed shape.
  • Validation uses AlphaFold as an oracle: if the ProteinMPNN-designed sequence folds back to the original backbone, the design worked, even if the sequence is very different from natural proteins.
  • Both models can be conditioned on constraints: RF Diffusion can be steered toward specific topologies by adding forces; ProteinMPNN can keep parts of a sequence fixed and redesign the rest, enabling rational engineering for specific applications.

Worked intuition: Picture designing a new protein that binds to a cancer target. First, RF Diffusion generates a novel backbone structure that you condition to include a specific helix (a spiral shape) borrowed from p-53 protein, because that helix naturally binds to the target MDM2. This design produces a 3D scaffold that holds this helix. Second, you run ProteinMPNN on this backbone, which treats it as a graph and predicts amino acids position by position. Maybe the original p-53 was: met-arg-gly-ser... but ProteinMPNN predicts: leu-lys-ala-thr... (completely different). These two sequences share only ~44% amino acids in common. Yet when you feed this new sequence to AlphaFold, it folds into the exact backbone you designed. Why? Because the network learned that these different sequences can produce the same 3D shape when folded. The new sequence is actually better suited to the supporting structure RF Diffusion created, so the designed protein might be more stable or active than if you'd kept the natural sequence.

Where it breaks down: De novo protein design is still constrained by what's learnable. ProteinMPNN achieves only 50% sequence recovery, so it sometimes predicts sequences that don't fold correctly—that's why AlphaFold validation is mandatory, not optional. RF Diffusion generates only backbones, not full proteins; you must pair it with sequence design. Both models are trained on known proteins, so they extrapolate from the training distribution; unconditional designs (starting from pure noise) can be novel but sometimes less reliable than conditioning on natural structural motifs. Computational cost is another limit: validating hundreds of designed sequences with AlphaFold is slow. Finally, structure prediction (AlphaFold) is a proxy for real-world function—a sequence might fold correctly in silico but be unstable, insoluble, or inactive in the lab. Experimental validation is always the final arbiter.

Takeaway: De novo protein design works by splitting the problem into two learned steps: RF Diffusion generates diverse 3D backbones starting from noise (inheriting understanding from structure prediction models), and ProteinMPNN finds sequences that will fold into those backbones using graph neural networks to encode spatial structure. Both are graph-aware, both exploit deep learning trained on real proteins, and both can be steered toward specific objectives, making it possible to design proteins for real applications—but validation remains essential because models capture structure, not all aspects of real-world function.
