First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: When neural networks fail at turbulence: why generative models are the answer

The question it answers: Why do standard neural operators completely fail at predicting turbulent flows even with thousands of training examples, and what fundamentally different approach actually works?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you're trying to predict exactly where a leaf will land as it falls through turbulent air. Two tiny changes to where you release it lead to completely different paths—the tiniest variations explode into massive differences. That is what makes turbulent flows chaotic, and it is why a neural network trained to predict individual outcomes will fail catastrophically.

For most of this course, neural operators have been wildly successful—they learn to predict smooth solutions to physics equations from data, catching intricate behaviors that humans would struggle to code by hand. But we hit a hard wall: turbulent fluid flows. These are dominated by two properties that break everything we built. First, multiscale structure—the solution contains eddies and vortices at wildly different sizes, from large swirling patterns down to tiny threads. Second, chaos—if you change the initial condition by less than one percent, the solution diverges by nearly one hundred percent. These two properties are linked: chaos creates the multiscale chaos, which feeds back into chaos, creating an inescapable tangle.

The problem is not architectural or a lack of data. The lecturer trained a hybrid neural operator on Navier-Stokes equations starting from simple initial conditions (Taylor-Green vortex), using thousands of high-resolution simulations. The result: order-of-one errors, and worse, the network predicts only the average, washing out every small-scale structure. When you give it slightly perturbed initial conditions that should produce wildly different outputs, it outputs almost the same thing. The network is blind to the perturbations because it has learned to be insensitive to tiny changes—and that is a fundamental feature, not a bug.

Why is neural insensitivity inevitable? During training, gradient descent requires bounded gradients to move smoothly through weight space. If gradients exploded, training would fail. This forces the network to be Lipschitz continuous—meaning the output cannot change too much when the input changes slightly. Formally, the Lipschitz constant bounds how much the output can change per unit input change. For a trained neural network, this constant is necessarily of order one or modest (maybe 10, maybe 0.5), never a million. In a chaotic system, the ground truth solution map has unbounded Lipschitz constant—tiny perturbations grow to huge outputs. The neural network physically cannot learn this behavior.

A clever mathematical decomposition makes this crystal clear. When you perturb the input by a tiny amount and take the expectation of the output error, you can split it into two pieces: the bias term (what the network outputs when it cannot distinguish the perturbation) and the variance term (the actual variation in the ground truth output given different perturbations). The variance term is independent of the network—it is pure physics—and for chaos it is order one. The bias term, no matter how well trained, can only shrink to zero in the best case. Even zero bias is not enough: the ground truth variance dominates, so the network's prediction error floors at order one. In short, the network can only learn the mean, never the spreading caused by sensitivity.

The mean is not a useless target—it is what you should compute next. Because deterministic pointwise prediction is mathematically impossible for chaotic systems, the insight is to stop trying. Instead, give up on matching every vortex, every eddy, every filament. Instead, learn the probability distribution of solutions. This is statistical solution theory: you cannot guarantee where every eddy will be, but you can characterize what states are likely, what the average field looks like, what the variance looks like. If you have a probability distribution of initial conditions (even a narrow one from measurement uncertainty), you can evolve that distribution forward in time and ask the neural network to learn the conditional distribution—the distribution of outputs given inputs, not the deterministic map.

Generative models do exactly this. Instead of learning a function, learn a way to sample from a distribution. The key idea comes from diffusion: start with a known, simple distribution (standard Gaussian noise) and learn how to smoothly transform it into your target distribution (solutions of the PDE). During training, you go forward: take a real solution, add noise iteratively until it becomes pure Gaussian. During inference, you reverse: start with Gaussian noise, iteratively remove noise while conditioning on the initial conditions, and out pops a sample from the learned distribution of solutions. The noising and denoising are reversals of each other, linked by a stochastic differential equation that can be inverted mathematically.

The training algorithm is simple: given clean samples from your data, add Gaussian noise at various noise levels, then train a neural network (the denoiser) to predict the clean sample from the noisy version. This is just regression—standard supervised learning. Once trained, the denoiser is plugged into a formula (Tweedies formula, from probability theory) that approximates the score function—the log-gradient of the probability density. Solving the reverse stochastic differential equation with the score function iteratively at inference time creates samples from the target distribution. The beauty is that diffusion models work precisely because they do not need insensitive networks to learn chaos; they need the network to be a good denoiser at different noise levels, and denoising is learnable.

In practice for fluids, the architecture is a hybrid of convolutions (for local structure) and attention layers (for global relationships), trained to denoise velocity fields conditioned on initial conditions and prediction time. At inference, you sample Gaussian noise, feed it through the reverse SDE with the trained denoiser, and get out a plausible velocity field. Each sample is different (capturing uncertainty), but statistically correct (the ensemble of samples has the right statistics). This trades pointwise precision for distributional honesty—you cannot predict which microeddy will form where, but you can predict the probability that there will be a microeddy and its typical strength.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Chaos kills networks: tiny input changes explode to huge output changes, but trained networks must keep gradients bounded, making them insensitive to small perturbations. This is not fixable by architecture or data.
  • The Lipschitz trap: any network learned via gradient descent has a bounded Lipschitz constant, so it cannot match maps with unbounded sensitivity. The ground truth has unbounded sensitivity; the neural network cannot.
  • Multiscale structure lives in the variance: the mean of a chaotic ensemble washes out small scales; only the variance (the spread from tiny perturbations) contains the small eddies and filaments. Networks learn only the mean, never the variance.
  • Statistical solutions are the only honest target: instead of predicting where each vortex will be, predict the probability distribution of vortex positions and strengths. This trades deterministic precision for statistical correctness—the honest answer for chaos.
  • Diffusion models learn distributions by noising and denoising: iteratively add noise to data (forward process) then train a network to reverse it (backward process). The backward process is conditioned on the initial state, producing ensemble predictions with proper uncertainty.
  • Denoising is learnable, chaos is not: while chaotic dynamics are unlearnable in the pointwise sense, denoising noisy data at each noise level is straightforward supervised learning. This is why diffusion succeeds where standard neural operators fail.
  • Conditionality comes free: the initial conditions can be built into the denoiser as an additional input. At inference, samples from Gaussian noise conditioned on initial state automatically produce physically plausible solutions.

Worked intuition: Consider the Taylor-Green vortex (smooth sinusoid initial conditions). At time t=2, a standard neural operator trained on thousands of high-resolution simulations outputs something almost homogeneous—all the detail is gone. But if you run the true equations with a tiny 1% perturbation to the initial condition, you get a wildly different state (100% pointwise difference). The network saw this difference in training and learned to ignore it (because keeping it would require unbounded gradients). The true system is sensitive; the network learned to be insensitive. Now imagine instead of asking the network for one prediction, you ask it to generate random samples from the conditional distribution of solutions given initial conditions. Use diffusion: add noise to the initial condition, train a vision transformer to denoise it at different noise levels, then reverse the process at inference. Each sample is different (capturing chaos), but the ensemble of many samples has the right mean, variance, and small-scale structure. You have traded the impossible (one correct prediction) for the possible (statistically honest ensemble).

Where it breaks down: Diffusion models are computationally expensive—you must run the denoiser many times per sample (typically 50-1000 steps), making inference slow compared to a single forward pass through a standard neural operator. They also require matching the learned ensemble statistics to ground truth, which needs many samples and careful validation. For very short times (under one convective time scale), the chaos has not yet grown, so standard neural operators work fine; diffusion is overkill. The method assumes you can generate or access training data in the form of solution samples at various times—it does not work for entirely new physics without retraining.

Takeaway: Chaotic systems with multiscale structure are unlearnable as deterministic maps because the neural network must have bounded gradients but the truth has unbounded sensitivity. The solution is to stop trying to predict individual outcomes and instead learn to sample from the distribution of outcomes using diffusion models. Train a denoiser, reverse the noising process at inference, and you get ensemble predictions with the right statistics—honest uncertainty quantification for a fundamentally unpredictable system.
