First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Building Reusable AI Models for Equations That Describe Nature

The question it answers: Can a single AI model trained on diverse physics equations learn patterns general enough to work on completely new equations it has never seen? And if so, could it dramatically reduce how much data we need to train physics models?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine a chef who has learned to cook from many national cuisines—Italian, French, Chinese, Indian. When asked to cook a dish from a cuisine they've never formally studied, they don't start from zero. They recognize flavors, techniques, and patterns that overlap. A physics foundation model works the same way: it learns patterns from many equations so that when it meets a brand-new equation, it already has useful instincts.

The core problem is simple to state but hard to solve. Physics research constantly needs AI models that can quickly predict how systems evolve in time. Whether it's fluid flowing around an aircraft, heat spreading through a material, or a shock wave moving through air, we want neural networks to learn these solution operators—functions that take initial conditions and parameters and output the future state. Early in this course, you learned that neural networks can do this reasonably well if we feed them enough training data. But here's the catch: generating that training data is extremely expensive. One example from the lecture: a single fluid dynamics simulation took 61,000 compute node hours. To collect 10,000 such samples (which modern models typically need), the cost becomes prohibitive—billions of dollars for many real applications.

Earlier approaches tried to work around this data hunger in two main directions. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) encoded the equation itself into the loss function, so the model learned from the equations' structure rather than data. But that approach hit a wall: for infinite-dimensional problems like PDEs with function inputs and outputs, we still couldn't reliably make it work. The alternative was pure data-driven learning—just throw enough data at effective architectures like convolutional neural networks (CNOs) and Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs). These worked beautifully when data was available, but they required thousands of training examples per task. The fundamental obstacle remained: how do you get those thousands of examples when each one costs thousands of dollars to compute?

The key insight comes from watching how successful AI works in other domains. Language models like GPT-3 and GPT-4 don't learn English in isolation. Instead, they see text from every domain—fiction, code, math, poetry, science papers—all mixed together. Through exposure to this diversity, they learn patterns and relationships that transfer to new tasks. Vision models work the same way: trained on millions of diverse images, they capture visual patterns that generalize. The same principle could work for physics. Instead of training a model on just Navier-Stokes equations (fluid flow), or just the Laplace equation (steady-state problems), what if you trained on many equations all at once? The model would see shock waves in compressible flow, vortices in incompressible flow, diffusion patterns in heat equations, and wave propagation in acoustic equations. Perhaps these diverse experiences would reveal common underlying structures—shared mathematical patterns that show up in different equations.

This is what foundation models do. They operate in two stages. In the first stage (pre-training), you gather data from many physical systems and equations, mix it together, and train a large neural network—typically a transformer, the same architecture that powers language models. The network isn't trained for one specific task; instead, it learns general representations of how physics works across this broad landscape. In the Poseidon model described in the lecture, researchers pre-trained on fluid dynamics data: incompressible flows (Navier-Stokes with sine-wave initial conditions and Gaussian initial conditions) and compressible flows (Euler equations with different initial conditions including shock-capturing test cases). Together, this amounted to about 80,000 trajectories, generating roughly 5 million image pairs when each trajectory was broken into input-output pairs at different times. They trained three model sizes—tiny (20 million parameters), base (160 million), and large (650 million)—and it took about a week on eight high-end GPUs.

The second stage (fine-tuning) is where the magic happens. Once pre-trained, you take this general model and adapt it to a specific downstream task. If that task involves the same types of variables (like density, velocity, and pressure from fluid dynamics), you simply initialize the training from the pre-trained weights instead of starting from random initialization. But if the task is completely different—say, a reaction-diffusion equation where the inputs are concentration fields instead of velocity fields—you take a different approach: you freeze most of the pre-trained weights (especially the core processor, which lives in latent space) and only retrain the embedding layers (which translate raw inputs into the latent representation) and the recovery layers (which translate the latent representation back to physical outputs). This way, you're borrowing the learned physics from the pre-training stage while allowing the model to learn how to encode and decode the new problem's specific variables.

When tested on 15 diverse downstream tasks arranged in a hierarchy, the results were striking. At the bottom were tasks close to the pre-training distribution (incompressible and compressible flows with variations in initial conditions). Moving up, tasks gradually became more alien: adding new physics like transport, forcing, or gravity; then completely different equations like the wave equation (acoustic waves, not fluids) and the Allen-Cahn equation (reaction-diffusion, not waves); finally, steady-state problems like the Poisson equation and flows past an airfoil on unstructured grids. On average, the foundation model achieved a 50-fold gain in sample efficiency: achieving the same error as FNO with 50 times fewer training samples. For example, FNO needed around 1,000 samples to reach 2% error on one test case; the Poseidon foundation model reached the same accuracy with just 40 samples. Beyond sample efficiency, the absolute error also improved by roughly an order of magnitude across tasks.

The mechanism behind this success appears to be two-fold. First, the model learns from data diversity. When researchers ablated the model by removing one of the pre-training datasets (the curved Riemann problems), keeping the total data volume the same but reducing diversity, performance degraded noticeably, especially on capturing small-scale features like small vortices. Second, the model learns universal patterns. In one striking example, when fine-tuned on a Poisson equation (a completely different class of problems—steady-state, not time-dependent), the pre-trained model could still generalize effectively. The researchers hypothesized that the model had learned diffusion-like behavior from pre-training, and when asked to solve a steady-state problem by diffusing Gaussian forcing terms, it recognized and applied that pattern. They tested this extreme case by freezing the learned latent space and retraining only the embedding and recovery layers (about 0.2 million parameters out of 158 million). Even with only those tiny layers trainable, the model learned the Poisson problem reasonably well—achieving 3% error with 32 samples, while FNO needed around 100 samples.

Why transformers rather than other architectures? When researchers compared foundation models built on top of the same data but with different backbones (convolutional neural networks versus Swin transformers), the transformer-based model was 2.5 times better than the CNN-based one, even though they had similar model sizes and trained on identical data. This mirrors what's seen across language, vision, and robotics: transformers excel at learning from diverse, multi-domain data because their attention mechanism can flexibly learn which features matter in which contexts. For single, narrow tasks, CNNs and transformers perform similarly. But when the data is diverse and you need broad transferable knowledge, transformers pull ahead. The transformer's flexibility in learning attention patterns across the entire input, rather than relying on local convolutions, seems crucial for capturing the varied patterns in different physical phenomena.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Expensive data is the barrier: Modern physics simulations can take thousands of compute hours per sample, making traditional 'collect 10,000 examples' infeasible for most researchers.
  • Foundation models borrow the language-model approach: Pre-train on large, diverse data from many equations and systems, then fine-tune on specific tasks—just like GPT learns English on diverse text then adapts to specific prompts.
  • Diversity drives generalization: Training on many physical equations (not just one) helps the model learn underlying patterns that transfer to unseen equations, even very different ones.
  • Transformers are the enabling architecture: Unlike CNNs, transformers can flexibly learn which features matter in different contexts, making them far better suited for learning across diverse physics domains.
  • Transfer reduces sample difficulty by 50-100x: A foundation model can solve downstream tasks with 50 times fewer training samples than task-specific models, and with lower absolute error.
  • Latent space encodes transferable physics: The learned 'latent' representation (the middle layers of the network) seems to capture universal mathematical patterns that appear across different equations, allowing transfer to occur.
  • Fine-tuning strategy matters: For similar physics, transfer all weights; for different physics, freeze the latent space and retrain only how inputs/outputs map to that shared latent space.

Worked intuition: Let's walk through what happens when the Poseidon foundation model encounters a Riemann problem in compressible flow it has never explicitly trained on, but which is similar to pre-training data. With zero examples shown, the model makes a guess by averaging patterns it learned—essentially, it returns something that looks like typical fluid flow but captures no specific features. At one sample (66 input-output pairs, because the model has access to different time points in a single trajectory), it suddenly recognizes shock waves and large vortices. These features existed in the pre-training data—shock waves appeared in other Riemann problems, large vortices in Kelvin-Helmholtz problems. The model doesn't relearn these features from scratch; it retrieves learned patterns and applies them. With four samples, it captures shocks and large vortices correctly. With 32 samples, it also catches the small vortices and fine details. By 128 samples, it approaches near-perfect reconstruction. The model is not forgetting and relearning with each sample; rather, it's iteratively refining its understanding of 'what this specific problem's initial conditions and boundary conditions map to' while reusing the deep physics knowledge it already possesses from pre-training.

Where it breaks down: Foundation models for PDEs are effective but not magic. First, they only work at the scale and diversity of pre-training. The largest gain (50x sample efficiency) came when downstream tasks were somewhat related to pre-training (other fluid dynamics problems). When you go completely out of distribution (e.g., solving a Poisson equation or wave equation when trained only on fluids), gains shrink—still helpful, but modest. Second, the approach is limited by pre-training diversity and scale. Poseidon trained on two types of fluid equations in 2D. Extending to 3D increases compute costs by roughly a factor of 100. The model has never seen integral equations, systems with unknown physics, or regimes far outside its pre-training distribution. Third, the model still cannot be truly 'zero-shot'—giving it no examples and expecting it to solve a new equation perfectly. It can produce reasonable guesses for in-distribution tasks, but for out-of-distribution tasks, you need at least a few samples to retrain the embedding and recovery layers. Fourth, current models don't incorporate symbolic equation information. The model is purely data-driven and doesn't exploit the actual form of the PDE if you know it. This is a design choice that keeps the approach general but leaves potential performance on the table.

Takeaway: The big lesson is that physics, despite appearing fractured into separate domains (fluid dynamics, heat diffusion, wave propagation, electromagnetism), likely shares deep mathematical structures. A transformer trained to recognize patterns across many equations can learn those shared structures, then transfer that knowledge to solve new equations with a tiny fraction of the data that a task-specific model would need. This means that expensive, hard-to-generate training data becomes less of a barrier. The curve from 'no data' to 'reasonable solution' is now steep and short instead of long and flat. For practitioners, it means the path to deploying AI in physics shifts from 'spend months generating data' to 'pre-train once on diverse public datasets, then fine-tune on your specific problem with whatever samples you can afford to generate.' This is how foundation models changed the game in language and vision; the work here shows it's possible in physics too, though we're still in the early stages of scaling and expanding the approach.

Video Study Note

Plain title: Foundation models for PDEs

Watch for: This lecture introduces foundation models in the PDE setting. Listen for pretraining, many PDE tasks, reuse, and the danger of claiming too much from broad training.

Main Points Discussed

  • A foundation model is trained broadly and then adapted or tested on specific tasks.
  • For PDEs, the hope is reuse across equations, grids, parameters, or settings.
  • The hard question is which new PDE case is actually inside the learned range.

First-principles point: Broad training is useful only when the new scientific task shares the structure the model actually learned.

Learner takeaway: Ask what was varied during training and what is genuinely new at test time.

Do not worry about this yet: Do not accept scale as proof. Look for the held-out PDE family and the failure case.
