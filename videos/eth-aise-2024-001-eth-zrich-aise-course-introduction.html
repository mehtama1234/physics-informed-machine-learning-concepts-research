First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Why AI is transforming science: from data to discovery

The question it answers: How can we teach machines to solve the hard scientific problems that humans have struggled with for centuries—not by replacing our understanding, but by combining it with learning?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine a weather forecaster running a climate simulation that takes weeks on thousands of computers. Then imagine a neural network doing it in seconds. That shift—from brute-force calculation to learned pattern—is what this course is really about.

Deep learning has taken over AI in the last decade. It works by training a mathematical function with tunable parameters (a neural network) to match patterns in data. You give it input examples and their correct outputs, adjust the parameters to minimize the difference between predicted and actual outputs, and suddenly you have a function that can predict new inputs it has never seen. This is simple in principle but extraordinarily effective. Three things enabled this explosion: exponential growth in available data, cheap GPU computing power, and mature approaches like PyTorch that let you write neural networks in a few lines of code.

Science has concrete, recurring problems that show up across disciplines. Ben walks through five: (1) Simulation—given a physical system and input conditions, predict the resulting behavior. This usually relies on solving differential equations (PDEs), but the equations are often too expensive to solve numerically, requiring millions of core hours. (2) Inverse problems—given observations of a system's behavior, infer what the input conditions were. This is ill-posed (multiple solutions fit the data), noisy, and requires repeatedly solving the forward problem, making it even more expensive than simulation. (3) Design and control—use optimization to find system parameters that maximize some outcome (like lift on an aircraft wing). (4) Data assimilation—merge noisy measurements with a physical model to estimate hidden state or forecast. (5) Equation discovery—observe data, infer what the underlying differential equations are.

Naive deep learning fails at all of these. When a neural network trained on simple, layered Earth velocity models encounters a curved or multi-fault velocity structure, its predictions collapse. Neural networks generalize terribly outside their training distribution. They also lack interpretability—you cannot explain why they made a prediction—and they require enormous amounts of training data, which is often unavailable in science. Most dangerously, they do not make novel predictions or satisfy physical laws, so they cannot unlock new science the way a good theory does.

Scientific machine learning flips the script. Instead of throwing neural networks at data and hoping they work, SciML tightly combines centuries of scientific understanding (conserved quantities, symmetries, differential equations) with learned functions. The goal is models that are more effective, reliable, and interpretable than either pure learning or pure physics alone. Ben introduces three broad strategies: (1) Soft constraints via loss function—train the network to match data AND obey physical laws simultaneously by adding constraint terms to the objective. This is how physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) work: you encode that the network's outputs must satisfy the wave equation, energy conservation, etc. (2) Hard constraints via architecture—build physical laws into the network's structure itself, so certain symmetries or invariances are automatic, not learned. (3) Hybrid methods—don't replace traditional simulators; embed neural networks inside them. For instance, use a learned function to improve gradient steps in an optimization loop, so the physics solver stays in control but learns to do its job faster.

To make this concrete, Ben shows three examples. For simulation speedup, FourCastNet learns to predict weather patterns by doing convolutions in the Fourier (frequency) domain, automatically suppressing high frequencies that are often noise. It is four to five orders of magnitude faster than traditional climate models and accurate enough to forecast hurricanes. For inverse problems, a neural network can learn to filter the noisy gradient estimates in a gradient-descent loop, removing ill-posedness artifacts and converging faster. For equation discovery, fit a neural network to observational data, compute its analytical derivatives (via autodiff) to build a library of candidate terms, then use sparse regression to find which terms and coefficients appear in the true differential equation—essentially letting the data reveal the underlying physics.

The real insight is that science already has a approach for integrating new techniques: the theory-experiment cycle. Propose a theory (or in this case, a constrained neural network), make a prediction, test it, refine. SciML methods sit somewhere on a spectrum between pure data-driven learning (no physics) and traditional simulators (no learning). Moving toward physics increases reliability and interpretability but requires fewer data and fewer learned parameters. Moving toward learning increases flexibility and speed but demands more data and harder interpretation. The right balance depends on the problem.

This course will teach the major SciML techniques: physics-informed neural networks (soft constraints), operator learning (architectures designed for differential equations, like Fourier neural operators), state-of-the-art models (transformers, graph networks, diffusion models) with scientific tweaks, and hybrid methods (neural differential equations, differentiable physics engines, symbolic regression). The unifying theme is that all of them solve real scientific tasks—simulation, inversion, design, equation discovery—by fusing learning with understanding. The result is not a opaque model predicting numbers, but an interpretable, generalizable, scientifically grounded model that can accelerate discovery.

Ben emphasizes that this is not hype. Weather and climate forecasting, nuclear fusion control, protein structure prediction, exoplanet detection, medical imaging, particle physics—AI is already delivering breakthroughs. But the breakthroughs came when people stopped trying to replace science with AI and started combining them. That combination—learned patterns guided by physical law—is the north star of scientific machine learning.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Deep learning = training a mathematical function with tunable parameters on data; three forces (data, compute, approaches) made it dominate AI in the last decade
  • Science has five recurring hard problems: expensive simulation, ill-posed inverse problems, design/optimization, data assimilation, and equation discovery from noisy observations
  • Naive neural networks fail at all five because they don't generalize outside training data, lack interpretability, need enormous datasets, and violate physical laws
  • Scientific machine learning solves this by encoding physical knowledge three ways: as soft constraints in the loss function, as hard constraints in the architecture, or as scaffolding in a hybrid algorithm
  • Three concrete wins: FourCastNet (weather in milliseconds), learned inverse solvers (faster convergence despite noise), sparse regression on neural network derivatives (discovers equations from data)
  • The spectrum from pure learning to pure physics—the right point depends on your data, your understanding, and your cost tolerance
  • Integration of AI and science works because it mirrors the scientific method: propose constrained model, predict, test, refine

Worked intuition: You have a seismic inverse problem: geophones on the surface record pressure waves from an earthquake, and you want to infer the Earth's velocity structure below. Traditional gradient descent refines a velocity model by repeatedly solving the wave equation forward and comparing predictions to observations. But the gradient pointing toward the true solution is extremely noisy because the problem is ill-posed (multiple velocity structures fit the same data). A physics-informed neural network can learn to take a better step: instead of following the noisy gradient directly, you train a small network to receive the noisy gradient and output a cleaned-up step direction. You train it by differentiating through the entire optimization loop on many synthetic inverse problems, so it learns to filter ill-posedness noise. Result: convergence is faster and artifacts (false velocity anomalies) vanish. The neural network is not replacing physics; it is helping the physics solver do its job better, which is why it generalizes to real problems it was never trained on.

Where it breaks down: Physics-informed neural networks and SciML in general have honest limits. First, they only work if you know which physics constraints matter. In an entirely new domain, you might encode the wrong conservation law or miss an interaction term, and the whole approach fails—you are not doing discovery, just learning subject to your guesses. Second, they train slower than naive networks because adding physics constraints to the loss function is computationally expensive (you have to solve PDEs during training). Third, architecture-based hard constraints are brittle: if your symmetry assumptions are wrong, the model can actually perform worse than one with no constraints. Fourth, sparse regression for equation discovery only finds linear combinations of known terms; if the true equation involves a term you did not include in your library, you miss it. Fifth, hybrid methods only help if the traditional solver is what you want to accelerate; they don't discover entirely new algorithms. Finally, SciML methods still need validation—a learned model that conserves energy on training data might violate energy conservation in a new regime, and unlike traditional simulators, you have no guarantee.

Takeaway: AI is not replacing science; it is amplifying the science-theory cycle. By encoding what you already know (physics, symmetries, conservation laws) into neural networks, you teach machines to learn the gaps in that knowledge faster and more reliably than pure statistics or pure first-principles simulation alone can manage. This is the heart of scientific machine learning.
