First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: How to Solve Physics Equations Using Neural Networks

The question it answers: Can we teach neural networks to solve physics equations (like the heat equation or fluid flow) without needing lots of solved examples?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you want to find the temperature everywhere in a room over time. The traditional way (finite difference method) requires a huge grid of points and becomes impossible in many dimensions. But what if instead you trained a neural network to 'know' the physics law directly?

Let's start with the gap that inspired this whole approach. Standard machine learning works like image classification: you show a neural network millions of pictures labeled 'dog' or 'cat', and it learns to recognize. But physics problems are different. When you want to solve the heat equation (which governs how temperature spreads), you only know three things: (1) the initial temperature everywhere, (2) the boundary conditions (like what happens at the walls), and (3) the physical law itself—the equation that temperature satisfies. You do NOT have interior measurements showing what the temperature is at arbitrary points in the room. So there's a mismatch: standard machine learning demands labeled data at sample points, but physics-based problems only give you the equation and boundary values.

Here's the key insight that solves this puzzle: we can use the PDE (partial differential equation) itself as training signal. A partial differential equation is a mathematical rule that the true solution must satisfy everywhere. For instance, the heat equation says 'the time-derivative of temperature equals the space-derivative of temperature.' If we have a neural network that predicts temperature at any location and time, we can check: does this network actually satisfy the heat equation? To check, we compute the time-derivative of the network's output (using automatic differentiation—the backprop machinery), compute the spatial derivatives (also using backprop), and see if they match the equation. When they don't match, that mismatch is an error signal we can minimize.

Let's unpack what 'residual' means, because it's the engine of the whole method. A residual is the amount by which the equation is violated. If the true solution satisfies 'ut = uxx' perfectly, then substituting the neural network in gives some leftover term—that's the residual. We compute residuals at many random points scattered throughout the space-time domain. We also compute how badly the network fails to match the initial conditions (the temporal boundary) and spatial boundary conditions. These three types of mismatch—interior residual (PDE violation), initial condition error, and boundary condition error—are packaged into one loss function.

To make this practical, we face an immediate problem: the loss function involves integrals (summing error over the entire domain), and you can't compute integrals by hand. The solution is quadrature rules—numerical integration tricks that replace an integral with a weighted sum over sample points. The simplest is Monte Carlo: pick random points in the space-time domain and at the boundaries, evaluate the residuals and boundary errors at those points, then average them. More sophisticated quadrature (Gauss-Legendre, quasi-Monte Carlo) can do better, but the principle is the same: replace the integral with a finite weighted sum.

Now the algorithm is simple: (1) Choose some random collocation points (sampling points) throughout space-time and on boundaries. (2) Set up a neural network with random weights (just like for any neural network). (3) At each collocation point, compute the residual of the PDE using automatic differentiation. (4) At each boundary point, compute how far the network is from satisfying the boundary condition. (5) Sum all these errors into a loss function. (6) Use gradient descent (Adam, LBFGS, or any optimizer) to minimize this loss. If training succeeds, the network weights adjust until the residuals vanish—meaning the network has learned a function that satisfies the PDE and boundary conditions.

Why is this remarkable? First, you don't need any ground-truth solution samples. The PDE itself is your training signal. Second, the method is almost trivial to code: once you have automatic differentiation (which any deep learning approach provides), computing derivatives with respect to space/time is no harder than computing derivatives with respect to network weights. Computing the residual of a nonlinear PDE is just composing automatic differentiation calls. Third, the method scales to high dimensions much better than traditional grid-based methods. In grid methods, if you need 100 grid points per spatial dimension, then in 5D you need 100^5 = 10 billion points—intractable. With neural networks and random collocation points, you just pick more points as dimension increases; there's no exponential grid explosion.

Let's see how far this reaches. For linear PDEs like the heat equation, it works beautifully: even in 100 dimensions, you get about 2.5% error (and about 1.5% at 50 dimensions). For nonlinear PDEs like Burgers equation (which combines transport with diffusion), it works too—the network learns to capture the sharp fronts and smooth regions. For the Navier-Stokes equations (which govern fluid flow), the network outputs a vector (velocity field) instead of a scalar, and the same residual logic applies: check that the momentum equations and continuity equation are satisfied. Even for higher-order PDEs with third or fifth derivatives (like the KdV equation with solitons), automatic differentiation just keeps applying the chain rule. The method generalizes because it's fundamentally agnostic about which equation you're solving—just give it the equation form, and it builds the residual.

But there are real limitations. When you have many higher-order derivatives, automatic differentiation becomes expensive and accumulates numerical error. The loss landscape becomes nonconvex, so gradient descent can get stuck in local minima and fail to find a good solution. Enforcing boundary and initial conditions is soft (via the loss term), not hard, so unless the loss term has high weight, the learned solution can violate them slightly. The method assumes the solution is smooth; if shocks (true discontinuities) arise, neural networks smooth over them. And for inverse problems (inferring the equation from noisy data), the method becomes sensitive to noise. These aren't dealbreakers, but they explain why PINNs succeed in some regimes and struggle in others.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs): A neural network trained to satisfy a PDE by minimizing the residual (the equation violation) rather than matching data samples.
  • Residual as training signal: Instead of labeled ground truth, the PDE itself becomes the loss function—measure how badly the equation is violated at sample points, and minimize that.
  • Automatic differentiation for both inputs and parameters: The same backprop machinery that computes weight gradients can compute spatial/temporal derivatives of the network output, making it cheap to evaluate residuals.
  • Collocation points and quadrature: Replace integrals with weighted sums over randomly sampled points in space-time, turning continuous optimization into a finite sum.
  • Dimension advantage: Unlike grid-based methods that explode exponentially with dimensions, PINNs use scattered points, so high-dimensional PDEs (50D, 100D) become tractable.
  • Generality across equation types: The method works for linear, nonlinear, scalar, vector, first-order, second-order, and higher-order PDEs—just change the equation form, same code.
  • Hard vs soft constraints: Boundary and initial conditions are enforced softly in the loss (can be violated slightly) rather than hard-coded, which is simpler but less rigid.

Worked intuition: Consider the heat equation ut = uxx in 1D space with zero boundary conditions. Start with a fully-connected neural network u_theta(x,t) with random weights theta. Evaluate it at, say, 1000 random (x,t) points inside the domain and 100 points on the boundary x=0 and x=1. At each interior point, use PyTorch autodiff to compute du/dt and d²u/dx², then form residual = du/dt - d²u/dx². At each boundary point, compute u_theta(0,t) and u_theta(1,t)—these should be zero. At t=0, compute u_theta(x,0) and compare to the initial condition. Now form loss = mean(residual²) + 10*mean(boundary_error²) + 10*mean(ic_error²). Backprop this scalar loss to get gradients of theta, then one step of Adam or SGD. Repeat for thousands of iterations. As the loss shrinks, the network gradually learns weights that make du/dt ≈ d²u/dx², boundaries ≈ 0, and initial condition ≈ given. At convergence, evaluating u_theta at any new (x,t) gives an approximation to the true heat equation solution.

Where it breaks down: PINNs struggle when solutions are truly discontinuous (shocks in fluid dynamics)—neural networks smooth over discontinuities, so you get 5-10% error instead of 1%. Computing many high-order derivatives via autodiff becomes numerically unstable and expensive. If the PDE is stiff or has widely different timescales, gradient descent gets slow or trapped in local minima. Inverse problems (inferring equation parameters from noisy measurement data) are sensitive: noise in the data propagates through the residual and corrupts learned solutions. For very intricate geometries with intricate boundaries, enforcing boundary conditions softly via loss weighting is crude compared to hard-constraint methods. The method also assumes smoothness; it cannot easily encode conservation laws as hard constraints the way structured discretizations can.

Takeaway: Neural networks can solve PDEs without solving examples—by treating the equation violation (the residual) as a loss function and letting automatic differentiation compute derivatives. This flips the usual machine learning approach from data-driven to physics-driven, and it scales to high dimensions where traditional numerical methods fail. But it trades rigor for flexibility: boundary conditions are soft, discontinuities are smeared, and you're always finding a local optimum of the residual, not necessarily the true global solution.

Main Points Discussed

  • A PINN represents the unknown solution with a neural network.
  • The loss includes data mismatch plus equation mismatch plus boundary or starting-condition mismatch.
  • The method matters when data are sparse but a physical rule is known.

First-principles point: If you know a physics rule, the learned answer should be penalized when it breaks that rule.

Learner takeaway: A PINN is not just a neural network for physics data. It is a neural network whose output is checked against a known equation.
