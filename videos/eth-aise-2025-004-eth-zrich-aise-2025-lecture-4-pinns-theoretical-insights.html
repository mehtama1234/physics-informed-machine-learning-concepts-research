First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Why Physics-Informed Neural Networks Work: Connecting Training Error to Real Solutions

The question it answers: When we train a neural network to satisfy a physics equation (a PDE) instead of matching data, how do we know the network is actually solving the equation correctly? What hidden mechanisms ensure that minimizing training error leads to the true solution?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you're fitting a curve through invisible data by checking whether the curve obeys a known physical law (like the heat equation) at sampled points. You can't see the true answer, only whether your curve follows the rules. How much must you follow the rules before you're actually right?

The core problem is simple but deep: in physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), we don't have access to the true solution of a PDE. Instead, we train by forcing the neural network to satisfy the PDE itself at randomly chosen points in space-time. We measure how well it satisfies the equation—this is called the residual—and minimize that. But the residual is an integral over the whole domain, which we can't actually compute. In practice, we approximate it using a sum over a finite number of sample points (a technique from numerical analysis called quadrature). The leap of faith is that if we get the residual small enough via training, and if we sample enough points, then the network will actually be close to the true solution.

To understand this, the lecturer breaks it into three connected questions, working backwards from what we want. Question 1: If the residual is small (meaning the network nearly satisfies the PDE), does that guarantee the total error—the difference between the network and the true solution—is also small? The answer depends entirely on properties of the PDE itself, not the network. The key property is called coercivity, which is a mathematician's way of saying the PDE is stable: small changes in the inputs lead to proportionally small changes in the solution. Formally, if two different right-hand sides differ by a tiny amount, their solutions differ by at most a constant times that amount.

Here's the crucial insight for coercivity: take the original PDE (D times u equals f, where D is some differential operator) and imagine a slightly perturbed version where u-bar satisfies the same equation but with a different right-hand side. The coercivity property says that the difference between u and u-bar in some norm is bounded by a constant times the difference in the right-hand sides. Now plug in your neural network approximation as u-bar. By the coercivity bound, the error is proportional to how much the network fails to satisfy the PDE—exactly the residual. Therefore, if coercivity holds and the residual is small, the total error must be small too. This transforms an intractable problem (directly matching the true solution, which we don't know) into a tractable one (making the PDE residual small).

But there's a catch: coercivity is not automatic. The lecturer gives the heat equation as an example of a PDE that is coercive—the constant doesn't blow up—so pins work well there. Nonlinear equations like Navier-Stokes are trickier. For Navier-Stokes, the constant in the coercivity bound depends on the solution's vorticity (how much the fluid is spinning). If the vorticity explodes, the constant blows up and the bound becomes useless: a small residual no longer guarantees small error. This is the first failure mode: coercivity can fail or become so pessimistic that it ceases to be useful.

Moving to Question 2: Does small training error guarantee small residual? Here's where quadrature approximation matters. The true residual is an integral, but we compute it as a sum over n sample points. Classical numerical analysis tells us that the error in this approximation shrinks as n grows larger. With Monte Carlo sampling, the error goes down like 1/√n. With smoother functions and better quadrature rules, it can decay much faster. So if you make the training error (the sum) small enough and take enough sample points, the true residual (the integral) will be close to the training error and hence also small. Again, the constant here depends on how smooth the residual function is, but for smooth PDEs this is usually reasonable.

Question 3—can we actually make the training error small in practice?—is the hardest. Neural networks are universal approximators in theory, meaning they can represent any continuous function given enough parameters. But in practice, training is nonconvex and getting stuck in bad local minima is a real problem. The lecturer shows numerical experiments on the heat equation where they can indeed achieve small training error, and moreover, the total error tracks the training error beautifully. For every test case shown (heat, Black-Scholes option pricing, Navier-Stokes with low vorticity), small training error led to small total error in practice. However, this isn't guaranteed—it works when the architecture and initialization align with the problem's structure.

The deeper story is that PINNs succeed when two things align: the PDE itself has the right mathematical structure (coercivity with reasonable constants), and the optimization landscape allows the network to find a solution. When the PDE is linear (like the heat equation or Black-Scholes), coercivity is automatic and the constant is explicitly related to the inverse of the differential operator. When the PDE is nonlinear (like full Navier-Stokes), coercivity can still hold, but the constant depends on features of the solution (its norm, its vorticity, its regularity). If those features blow up, PINNs fail. The examples in the lecture show cases where the vorticity stays bounded and everything works, but the lecturer hints that there are regimes where high vorticity or other nonlinear phenomena can derail training.

Putting it together: minimize training error → residual stays small (quadrature approximation, requires many sample points and smooth functions) → total error stays small (coercivity, requires PDE to be stable and its constants to be reasonable). This three-step chain is the complete mechanism. It's neither magic nor automatic. It depends on the specific PDE, the choice of sample points, the network architecture, and the quality of optimization. The lecture provides computable bounds showing these relationships rigorously, validates them numerically, and identifies where they break down.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Residual-based learning: Instead of comparing to unknown true solutions, minimize how much the network violates the PDE equation itself (the residual). This flips the problem from impossible to solvable.
  • Coercivity as the stability condition: A PDE is coercive if small perturbations in inputs lead to bounded perturbations in outputs. This is the mathematical glue that ties small residuals to small errors. Without it, the whole approach collapses.
  • Quadrature approximation: The integral residual is intractable; approximate it as a sum over sample points. More points → better approximation. This is classical numerical analysis, not machine learning.
  • Three-step verification chain: For a given PDE, you must verify (1) does coercivity hold and is its constant reasonable, (2) does the quadrature approximation error vanish as n grows, (3) can optimization actually minimize training error. All three must be yes.
  • Nonlinearity complicates coercivity: Linear PDEs (heat, Schrodinger) have coercivity constants independent of the solution. Nonlinear PDEs (Navier-Stokes, many others) have constants that depend on solution features like vorticity. If those features blow up, the approach fails.
  • The bound is loose but not vacuous: Computable bounds from theory are typically 2-3 orders of magnitude away from practice, which is considered excellent in machine learning. Conventional classification theory bounds are 30 orders of magnitude away, so PINNs theory is surprisingly tight.

Worked intuition: Consider the heat equation, the simplest case. You parameterize a solution u(x,t) as a neural network. At 1,000 randomly chosen spacetime points (x_i, t_i), you compute how much the network violates the equation: residual_i = ∂u/∂t - ∇²u (evaluated at each point). You minimize the average of these residuals (the training error). Now, because the heat equation is linear and has a bounded inverse, coercivity theory guarantees that if the average residual is small (say 0.01), then the maximum pointwise error |u_net - u_true| is also at most some constant times 0.01 (that constant is the norm of the inverse Laplacian). Furthermore, because you're sampling 1,000 points and the heat equation is smooth, the quadrature error (difference between the integral residual and the sum of sampled residuals) decays like 1/√1000 ≈ 0.03. So if training error is 0.01, the true residual is at most 0.01 + 0.03 = 0.04, and by coercivity, the total error is bounded by (constant) × 0.04. For the heat equation, that constant is order-one, so you get honest error bounds. This is what the numerical experiments confirm: training error and total error track each other with a small gap.

Where it breaks down: The approach breaks in multiple ways. First, if the PDE is not coercive (some nonlinear systems lack this property), or if coercivity holds but the constant blows up (as in Navier-Stokes with high vorticity), then small residuals no longer guarantee small errors. Second, if the residual function is not smooth, the quadrature error can be large and you'd need impractically many sample points. Third, if the optimization landscape is pathological—many local minima, saddle points that trap training—you may never achieve small training error even if a global optimum exists. The examples shown all avoid these pitfalls, but the lecturer hints that intricate fluids, turbulent flows, and other regimes can trigger failure modes. Finally, boundary conditions and initial conditions (hidden in the abstract notation) can introduce additional coercivity constants that blow up, making the bound useless.

Takeaway: Physics-informed neural networks work when three conditions align: the PDE is stable (coercive) with reasonable constants, the residual is smooth enough that sampled quadrature approximates the integral well, and optimization finds a solution with low training error. This is not magic—it's a careful chain of mathematical arguments grounded in PDE theory and numerical analysis. Verify coercivity, understand the constants, sample densely enough, and trust that small training error implies small true error. When any condition fails, PINNs fail too, and you need a different method or a redesigned network.

Video Study Note

Plain title: Why PINNs can work and where they struggle

Watch for: This lecture asks what can be said about PINNs beyond examples. Listen for approximation, optimization, stability, and why a small training loss may not settle the scientific question.

Main Points Discussed

  • A PINN has to represent the solution, find useful weights, and keep the equation error under control.
  • The theory separates what the network could represent from what training actually finds.
  • Trust still depends on checking changed regions, boundaries, sharp features, and held-out cases.

First-principles point: A method is not trusted because it has a physics loss; it is trusted only when that loss controls the quantity the scientist needs.

Learner takeaway: Separate three questions: can the network represent the answer, can training find it, and does the result survive the right test?

Do not worry about this yet: Do not memorize proof details first. Learn what each proof burden is trying to protect.
