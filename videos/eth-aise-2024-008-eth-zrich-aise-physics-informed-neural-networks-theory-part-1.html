First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Why Physics-Informed Neural Networks Work (and When They Don't)

The question it answers: We can train neural networks to solve equations from physics without any examples of correct answers. How is this even possible, and what mathematical conditions have to be true for it to work?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you have a rule that heat spreads through materials. You know the rule perfectly (the math equation). You don't know what the answer looks like, but you can check whether any guessed answer obeys the rule. A neural network can learn by repeatedly guessing answers, checking how badly they break the rule, and fixing itself—like a student improving a solution by checking their work against a known constraint.

To understand how this works, we need to see the problem clearly. When you have a physics equation (a partial differential equation, or PDE), you're really asking: given some inputs F, find the solution U that satisfies the equation. The equation itself is written as D of U equals F, where D is a differential operator—basically a recipe for transforming U using calculus. For instance, the heat equation says that how temperature changes over time, plus how it spreads in space, equals the heat source. The solution U (the temperature field) has to satisfy this relationship everywhere in the domain.

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) take a completely different approach from typical machine learning. They don't need labeled examples of correct answers. Instead, they use the equation itself as a teacher. Here's the mechanism: you train a neural network to output a guess for U at any location. Then, you automatically differentiate this neural network to compute what D of U would be at those locations. You compare D of U (from the network's guess) against F (which you know), and measure how far apart they are. This mismatch is called the residual—it tells you how badly the network's guess violates the equation. The training goal is to make the residual as small as possible everywhere. When the residual is near zero, the network's output approximately satisfies the physics.

But here's a tricky question: does minimizing the residual actually guarantee that your neural network's output is close to the true solution? Not automatically. This is where we need a bridge—a mathematical argument that connects 'the equation is nearly satisfied' to 'the answer is nearly correct.' The bridge is built on a property of PDEs called coercivity (or stability). This property says something intuitive: if two different inputs to a PDE are very close to each other, then their solutions must also be close. More formally, coercivity gives you a bound: the difference between two solutions is proportional to the difference between the inputs that created them. In other words, the PDE doesn't amplify small perturbations wildly—it behaves in a stable, controlled way.

Now apply this to your PINN. Your network solves a perturbed equation: instead of D of U equals F, it actually solves D of U_network equals F plus the residual error. The residual is like a small change to the input F. By the stability property, if the residual is small, then the output U_network must be close to the true solution U. This is the key mathematical link: small residual implies small error. This works not just for simple linear equations but also for nonlinear ones, though nonlinear cases introduce extra constants that depend on the specific solution.

But there's another gap. In practice, you can't compute the integral of the residual over the entire domain by hand. You sample the equation at random points in the domain, compute the residual at those points, and average them. This is called a quadrature—a numerical approximation of an integral. Here's the good news: if the underlying residual function is smooth (which it is, because the PDE solution is smooth), then as you add more quadrature points, the approximation converges to the true integral. So, making your training loss small (which is based on sampled points) pushes the true integral of the residual toward zero, which then pushes the actual error toward zero through the stability property.

The whole argument rests on whether neural networks can actually approximate smooth solutions well enough. This is where the universal approximation theorem comes in—a fundamental result saying that neural networks can approximate any continuous or integrable function arbitrarily well. But PINNs need more than that: they need the network and all its derivatives to approximate the solution and its derivatives well, because the residual involves derivatives. Fortunately, there are related theorems (including results by the lecturer) proving that neural networks can do this—they can approximate smooth functions along with their first, second, and higher-order derivatives. This completes the theoretical foundation: neural networks exist that can make both the training loss and the residual small simultaneously.

Finally, we have to ensure the problem is even solvable in practice—that we're not searching for a needle in a haystack. The existence results above prove that neural networks have the capacity to work, but they don't guarantee that gradient descent starting from a random initialization will find them. That's a separate, harder question about optimization. However, what we can say is: if there were a good solution out there (mathematically speaking, it should exist), then the loss landscape has a good point to find. This means the problem is 'well-posed' in the sense that we're not trying to accomplish something impossible.

In summary, PINNs work through a chain of logic: (1) Neural networks can approximate any smooth function and its derivatives arbitrarily well. (2) If a neural network approximates the true solution well, then the residual (the degree to which it violates the equation) becomes small. (3) If the residual is small and you have enough quadrature points, the training loss becomes small. (4) If the training loss is small and the PDE is stable (coercive), then the network's output must be close to the true solution. This creates a cycle: training to minimize a computable quantity (training loss) is equivalent to minimizing an uncomputable quantity (true error in solution space). But this chain can break at any link—if the PDE lacks stability, if the network can't be trained well, or if there aren't enough quadrature points, then the whole apparatus fails.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Residual: the mismatch between what a network predicts when you apply the equation to it, versus what the equation says should happen. Making residuals small is the entire goal of PINN training.
  • Coercivity (stability): a mathematical property of PDEs that says small changes in inputs produce small changes in outputs. This property is the bridge linking 'residual is small' to 'solution is accurate.'
  • Universal approximation: neural networks can approximate any smooth function arbitrarily well, and with the right structure, they can approximate derivatives too. This ensures solutions exist in theory.
  • Quadrature approximation: since you can't integrate over the whole domain by hand, you sample at points and average—as you add points, this approximation converges to the true integral, so training loss becomes reliable.
  • Well-posedness: the PINN problem is mathematically legitimate; there exists at least one neural network that makes the residual small. Whether you find it via optimization is a different question.
  • The chain of implications: good network → small residual → small training loss (with enough points) → small true error (if coercivity holds).
  • Failure modes: PINNs break if you can't train the network well, if you don't have enough quadrature points, if the PDE lacks stability, or if stability constants are huge.

Worked intuition: Take the one-dimensional heat equation: how temperature changes over time equals how much it spreads in space. The true solution is some temperature field u(x,t) that satisfies this. A PINN guesses a solution using a neural network u_net(x,t). To check the guess, it computes: (∂u_net/∂t) − (∂²u_net/∂x²) at several random points in space-time. Call this residual r. When r is near zero, the network's guess obeys the heat equation. The network learns by reducing |r| at those sample points. Now, because the heat equation is stable (small perturbations to the forcing term cause small changes in temperature), if r stays small everywhere, then u_net must be close to the true solution u. The training loss is just the average of |r|² at sample points. Make the training loss tiny, add more sample points so the average converges to the true integral, and the network's error shrinks. That's the whole mechanism.

Where it breaks down: This approach fails in several ways. First, if you can't train the network well—if gradient descent gets stuck in a bad local minimum—the training loss stays large, so the residual stays large, so the solution is wrong. Second, if you don't have enough quadrature points, the training loss becomes a poor proxy for the true residual; you might think the residual is small when it's actually not. Third, if the PDE itself lacks coercivity—if small input perturbations can produce huge solution changes—then even a small residual doesn't guarantee a small error. Fourth, for nonlinear PDEs, the coercivity constant can depend on the solution itself in complicated ways, making it hard to predict when training will succeed. Finally, in high dimensions, the curse of dimensionality can make sampling impractical: you might need exponentially many quadrature points to cover the domain well.

Takeaway: Physics-informed neural networks work because the PDE equation itself acts as a teacher: by making the network's output approximately satisfy the equation (small residual), and by using mathematical stability properties of well-posed PDEs, you indirectly force the output to approximate the true solution—without ever needing ground-truth examples. This beautiful idea rests on universality of neural networks and stability of PDEs, but it's fragile: it fails whenever training is hard, sampling is sparse, or the PDE is inherently unstable.
