First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: How AI reads the molecular world: from protein sequences to 3D structures

The question it answers: How can machine learning models learn to predict how proteins fold into 3D shapes and what molecules can do, starting only from text-like sequences of amino acids?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine if you had a recipe written in just letters (A, B, C, ... for each ingredient), and a neural network could learn from millions of recipes to guess not just what the final dish looks like, but also how it will taste and what it can be used for—that's what these models do with proteins.

The core problem that kicks off this whole field is a massive mismatch: proteins are built from just 20 naturally-occurring amino acids (the building blocks), strung together in long chains. But those chains fold into intricate 3D shapes that determine what the protein actually does—whether it speeds up a chemical reaction, holds your cells together, or carries oxygen in your blood. The challenge is that the sequence alone (the order of amino acids) doesn't obviously tell you the shape. Predicting this shape from sequence is like guessing the final form of origami from just a list of the color order of the paper—the spatial rules are hidden in the chemical properties of those amino acids, and you have to learn them from data.

For a long time, biologists had to use expensive experimental methods like X-ray crystallography or nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) to physically measure protein structures, which took months or years. But in 2020–2021, two breakthrough models emerged: AlphaFold and ESM. Both are transformer neural networks (the same architecture that powers modern language models). The insight is that protein sequences and natural language are analogous: both have a fixed vocabulary (20 amino acids vs. thousands of words), grammar-like rules, and long-range dependencies (amino acids far apart in the sequence can influence each other when folded). This analogy opened the door to applying deep learning methods from NLP to biology.

AlphaFold works by doing something clever: first, it searches genetic databases for similar sequences in other organisms and builds a multiple sequence alignment—a side-by-side comparison showing which positions are conserved (shared by many proteins) and which vary. Conserved positions hint at what's important for the protein to function. Alongside this, it generates a rough distance map between amino acids using known protein structures as templates. These two representations (the sequence alignment summary and the pairwise distance prediction) are fed into AlphaFold's core engine, which is a transformer with special 'attention' mechanisms that update both representations iteratively. Finally, a structure module converts the refined representations into actual 3D coordinates—distances between residues and bond angles. The model even recycles its own predictions, feeding the 3D output back as input to refine itself, typically three times. When AlphaFold2 competed in the 14th CASP (Critical Assessment of protein Structure Prediction) challenge, it achieved astonishing accuracy: predicting atom positions with a root mean square deviation of only 0.96 ångströms (a carbon atom is 1.4 ångströms wide).

ESM (Evolutionary Scale Modeling) took a different route. Meta's team trained a transformer on 250 million protein sequences using a self-supervised objective: they randomly masked some amino acids and asked the model to predict what was hidden. This is conceptually simple, but the model must learn deep biological knowledge to do it well. Remarkably, the hidden layer representations that ESM generates encode not just sequence information but also structural details. Researchers proved this by taking ESM's embeddings and training a simple logistic regression classifier to predict secondary structure elements (alpha helices and beta sheets) from the protein structure—achieving high accuracy. The key point: the model never saw 3D coordinates, only sequences, yet it learned structure-like patterns. Later, ESM was extended with a folding head (ESM-Fold), which uses the same recycling strategy as AlphaFold. ESM-Fold is about 60 times faster than AlphaFold because it doesn't need to search databases or compute multiple sequence alignments—it just takes a sequence and outputs a structure, working entirely standalone.

However, both AlphaFold and ESM-Fold have an important limitation: they predict a single, static 3D structure—typically the lowest-energy conformation. Real proteins are dynamic; they wiggle and bend. If you predict a structure and find that a known substrate doesn't fit in the enzyme's active site, it might be because the protein can actually flex open in reality but the model shows it closed. This limitation matters for drug discovery and enzyme engineering, where you need to understand not just the typical shape but the range of shapes a protein can adopt.

Beyond sequence-only models, the lecture introduces structure-based approaches. One method is 3D convolutional neural networks (3D-CNNs). These work by converting the 3D atomic coordinates into a 3D grid of voxels (imagine a 3D checkerboard). Each voxel gets assigned channel values based on what atoms are nearby: carbon atoms in one channel, nitrogen in another, oxygen in a third. Then standard convolutional operations scan this grid to extract features. This works well for small prediction tasks (like predicting whether a molecule will bind strongly to a protein), but has drawbacks: the model size grows cubically with grid resolution, much of the grid is empty space (sparse), and the model must be trained on multiple rotations of the protein to be rotation-invariant, making training expensive.

To handle protein structures more efficiently, the field is turning to graph neural networks (GNNs). A graph is a natural representation: atoms become nodes, chemical bonds become edges, and you can store properties on both. Unlike 3D grids, graphs don't waste memory on empty space. The model learns by letting information flow along edges—each atom learns features by aggregating information from its bonded neighbors, then passes refined information back. Transformers and message-passing GNNs use attention to learn which connections matter most. This approach is more memory-efficient, computationally scalable, and naturally equivariant to rotations, making it ideal for processing large numbers of molecular structures.

The lecture closes with the insight that multiple input modalities work: text-based (just sequences), structure-based (3D coordinates), and hybrid (structure + sequence together). For small molecules, chemists use SMILES—a linear text notation where atoms are chemical symbols, bonds are drawn with special characters, and rings are numbered. These can be tokenized and fed to transformers just like protein sequences. The field is discovering that different architectures and input types are useful for different questions. Self-supervised pretraining on massive unlabeled datasets (250 million sequences for ESM, vast SMILES corpora) followed by fine-tuning on smaller labeled datasets is becoming standard. This is how AI learns the molecular world: by finding patterns in billions of sequences and structures, learning a latent geometry of proteins and molecules, and using that knowledge to predict properties and design new ones.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Amino acid sequences alone don't reveal 3D protein shape; deep learning models learn this mapping from data by exploiting patterns (like conserved positions) and analogies to natural language (grammar, vocabulary, long-range dependencies).
  • AlphaFold combines sequence alignments, structural templates, attention mechanisms, and iterative refinement (recycling) to predict protein 3D coordinates with near-experimental accuracy; it's the most accurate but requires database searches, so it's slower.
  • ESM-Fold achieves similar accuracy 60× faster by using only sequence input and self-supervised pretraining on 250 million sequences, proving that structure information is encoded in sequence patterns even without seeing coordinates.
  • 3D-CNNs can process protein structures as voxel grids (like 3D images) but are computationally expensive and struggle with sparsity and rotation invariance; graph neural networks are emerging as a more efficient alternative.
  • Graph neural networks represent proteins as atoms (nodes) and bonds (edges), avoiding grid sparsity and naturally handling rotations; they scale better for large datasets and are becoming the favored structure-based architecture.
  • Both text-based approaches (using sequence alone) and structure-based approaches (using 3D coordinates) are valuable; sequence methods are simpler and more data-rich, while structure methods capture spatial information crucial for tasks like drug binding prediction.
  • Self-supervised pretraining on unlabeled sequences (e.g., masked language modeling for ESM) learns deep biological knowledge; fine-tuning on small labeled datasets then enables specific predictions like function or stability.

Worked intuition: Consider a small enzyme that naturally binds and processes molecule X. You want to engineer it to accept molecule Y instead. If you naively use only the amino acid sequence, you might miss the spatial constraints of the active site pocket. But if you predict the 3D structure with AlphaFold, you can visualize the pocket's shape and see that Y is too bulky. You might change one amino acid to make more room. Here's the AI part: instead of manually trying hundreds of mutations, you could use a GNN trained on many enzyme–substrate pairs to score which mutations are most likely to maintain protein stability while opening the pocket. The GNN learns 'which atoms talk to which' (the graph connectivity) and 'how changes propagate' (message passing), letting it predict the effect of a change without running expensive molecular simulations. This combines sequence knowledge (from pretraining like ESM), structure knowledge (from AlphaFold or a GNN encoder), and learned rules (from the training data).

Where it breaks down: Both AlphaFold and ESM-Fold predict a single static structure, not the dynamic conformational ensemble proteins actually sample; so they fail if you need to know about flexible regions, transient binding pockets, or allosteric motion. They're also less reliable for very small proteins (< 50 residues) and multimeric assemblies (multiple subunits). Structure-based models like 3D-CNNs and GNNs require known 3D coordinates, which are far scarcer than sequences—so sequence-only methods scale better to unexplored proteins. Graph neural networks still require careful edge definition (which atoms count as bonded?), and they haven't yet matched the experimental accuracy of AlphaFold for backbone atoms. Finally, all these models are trained on experimentally solved structures, which are biased toward proteins that crystallize well or are easy to measure, not necessarily representative of the full proteome.

Takeaway: Neural networks learn to predict protein shape and molecular properties by finding patterns in massive amounts of sequence and structure data, using the insight that sequences encode structure just like natural language encodes meaning. The best modern approach layers methods: pretraining on huge unlabeled datasets to learn biology, then fine-tuning on small labeled datasets for specific tasks, and combining text-based speed with structure-based detail when possible.
