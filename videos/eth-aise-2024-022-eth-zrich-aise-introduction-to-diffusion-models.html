First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: How to Turn Noise Into Pictures: The Backward Diffusion Model

The question it answers: If we can make pictures disappear into random noise by adding noise step-by-step, can we run that process backward to turn pure noise into real pictures?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine a photograph slowly fading into pure static, pixel by pixel, as you add random noise. A diffusion model learns to reverse that—starting from complete TV static, it gradually removes the noise to reconstruct a sharp photo.

Diffusion is a natural process where particles spread out randomly. Think of gas molecules in a sealed room: if you break the seal, they wander randomly outward and mix with the air outside. The same idea works for images: if you treat each pixel as a particle and make it do a random walk (by adding random noise each time step), eventually every image becomes pure random static. This forward process is easy—just add noise. The hard part is learning to run it backward.

Mathematically, we describe this with a stochastic differential equation—a rule for how things change when randomness is involved. The forward process says: each tiny change in the image is made up of two things. First, a deterministic drift (a predictable push in some direction). Second, random noise (the Wiener process, which is a fancy way of saying 'Gaussian noise at each step'). The amount of noise we add is controlled by a diffusion coefficient. Start with a real image, apply this forward SDE long enough, and you end up at pure Gaussian noise.

Here's the remarkable part: there exists a reverse SDE. In the 1980s, Anderson proved that if you know the probability distribution of images at every time step, you can write down an SDE that runs backward in time. Start from pure noise and solve this backward SDE, and you arrive at a sample from your original data distribution—a real image. The catch: this backward SDE depends on something we don't know—the probability distribution of images at each time step. But we can train a neural network to learn it.

Instead of learning the full distribution, we train a network to learn the score function: the gradient of the logarithm of the probability. Think of probability as a landscape; the score points uphill. At any noisy image and any time step, the score tells you which direction to move to find images that are 'more probable.' We parameterize this with a neural network that takes two inputs (the noisy image and the time step) and outputs a vector the same shape as the image—the direction to move.

To train this network, we use a loss function derived from the forward process. Here's the key insight: when you apply the forward SDE to a clean image, the noisy image at time t is just the original image plus some Gaussian noise. The amount of noise depends on t. We can compute the true score analytically for this case. So the loss function says: the network should predict the score, which turns out to be equivalent to predicting the noise that was added. During training, we sample random clean images, random time steps, add the corresponding noise, and teach the network to predict that noise. This is simpler and more stable than trying to match distributions directly.

Once trained, generating images is straightforward. Start with pure random noise (sample from a Gaussian). Solve the learned backward SDE from time T to time 0, using your trained neural network as the score function. At each step, the network tells you how to steer the noise toward higher probability. You can use any numerical solver—Euler's method works fine. There's even a deterministic version: an ordinary differential equation (ODE) that produces a single path without randomness but still samples from your learned distribution.

To use diffusion models for inverse problems—like reconstructing a CT image from a sinogram (the scanner measurement)—we interleave two forces. As we reverse the diffusion (denoising), at each time step we also add a small optimization step: we compute the gradient of the measurement loss (how well the current image matches the observed data) and nudge the image toward satisfying that constraint. This hybrid approach marries two ideas: the diffusion model enforces that the result looks like a real CT image, and the optimization enforces that it matches the actual measurement. The result is an image that is both physically realistic and consistent with the observations.

The power of this method is generality. Whether your forward process is adding noise to images, modeling stock price evolution, or describing other physical processes, the same math applies. You learn a score function, reverse the process, and sample from your learned distribution. For inverse problems in science, diffusion models offer a principled way to blend physical constraints with data priors, avoiding overfitting and producing physically plausible solutions. The link to neural differential equations runs deep: the trained model is itself a learnable differential equation that produces realistic samples.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Forward diffusion is simple: repeatedly add noise until an image becomes pure static. It's the reverse we need to learn.
  • The score function is the gradient of log-probability—it points in the direction of 'more likely' images. A neural network learns to approximate it.
  • Training the score network reduces to predicting the noise added at each step, which is tractable and stable.
  • Once trained, generate images by starting from noise and iteratively denoising using the learned score, guided by a differential equation solver.
  • For science: interleave denoising with optimization to satisfy measurement constraints, creating images that are both realistic and consistent with observations.
  • Diffusion models are interpretable as neural differential equations, providing a bridge between deep learning and classical scientific computing.

Worked intuition: Let's trace one example. You have a 64×64 image of a handwritten digit. In the forward process, you add Gaussian noise (scaled by the time step) repeatedly: the image becomes increasingly blurry and grainy until, after T steps, it's indistinguishable from random pixels. To reverse it, your trained score network receives both the noisy image at each step t and the time value. It outputs a 64×64 image (the same shape) predicting the noise. In practice, this is learned as: network predicts noise minus original image divided by noise standard deviation at time t. As you solve the backward equation from t=T down to t=0, at each step the network nudges the image to align with the learned probability of true digits. You never 'condition' on a label—the reverse SDE only depends on the noisy image and time. Yet if your training data was balanced across all digits, the final image will look like one of them, randomly chosen.

Where it breaks down: The method assumes the backward SDE is well-defined, which requires knowing the score function everywhere. In practice, you train only on the times and images your forward process samples; you never see true images that are extremely noisy, so the network's predictions there may be unreliable. Solving the backward ODE in many steps is slow—you're running a neural network hundreds of times per image. For high-resolution images (megapixels), memory and compute explode. For inverse problems, the method works best when the forward operator (the scanner model) is fast to compute gradients through; if it's truly opaque model, the proximal optimization step won't work. Finally, the quality of generated images depends entirely on the quality and diversity of training data; poor or biased data produces poor or biased samples.

Takeaway: Diffusion models reverse a natural process by learning to predict noise at every stage, then run that prediction backward. For science, they blend a learned prior (images that look realistic) with measurement constraints (equations that respect observations), producing solutions that are both physically plausible and data-consistent.
