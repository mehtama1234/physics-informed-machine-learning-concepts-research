PDEs — when a neighborhood rule runs everywhere at once

A partial differential equation says how something changes at each point based on its immediate neighbors. Apply that rule everywhere every tick and something remarkable happens: a purely local instruction produces global shapes. Here is a real run where a heat-equation rule smooths a peak toward zero over time, why the local neighbors are enough to control the whole picture, the one thing that breaks everything, and where this idea reappears.

1 · The problem — you can describe how a thing changes locally, but you want the whole picture

Imagine watching heat spread in a rod, or ink drift through water, or a rumor spread through a town. At each moment you understand the rule perfectly: heat flows from hot to cold between touching points; a rumor travels when a person who knows tells a person who does not. But that is a local rule — it says what happens in the neighborhood of one point. What you really want to know is: what does the whole rod look like after an hour? Which way did the ink swirl? Who knows the rumor by day 10?

For that you need to write the local rule as an equation and step it forward. A partial differential equation is exactly that — a recipe saying how one point relates to its neighbors at each instant. Apply it everywhere in the space, tick by tick, and something extraordinary emerges: the global pattern.

2 · The reframe — write the local rule, then step it forward in time

The key insight is that you already know the rule. You know that heat spreads toward its neighbors' average temperature. You know that ink diffuses. You just need to turn that into an update that runs everywhere.

Here is how: suppose you measure temperature at discrete points along the rod — point 0, point 1, point 2, and so on. At each time step, every point's new temperature is its old temperature plus a small nudge toward the average of its neighbors. That nudge size matters: too big and the whole system oscillates wildly and explodes. Too small and it hardly changes. There is a sweet spot where the system stays stable and smooth — and that sweet spot is called the CFL limit, a stability constraint that every numerical solver must respect.

3 · Seen on a real run — a local neighbor-averaging rule produces exact global decay

We ran the heat equation on a grid using the neighbor-averaging rule. The rule is: each point nudges toward the average of its two neighbors every tick. Mathematically: u_new at point i equals u at point i plus a stability parameter times (left neighbor minus 2 times the point itself plus right neighbor). The stability parameter was 0.4, well below the limit of 0.5 where it blows up.

We started with a sharp peak at the center of the grid. The true mathematical solution to the heat equation says the peak should decay as exp(−pi squared times time), a smooth exponential fall to zero. Let us compare what the numerical rule computed:

This is what a PDE does: turn a neighborhood instruction into a global pattern.

4 · Why local rules give global order

The magic is in the coupling. Every point talks only to its immediate neighbors — this is the local rule. But those neighbors also talk to their neighbors, and those to theirs. So information propagates: what starts as a peak at the center gradually averages with the rest. A single step is tiny and local. But repeat it thousands of times and the peak has nowhere to hide. Every tick it gets pulled down by its surroundings, smoothing and flattening until it is gone.

The same principle works for any diffusive process: heat, chemical concentration, rumors, ideas. The simple average-with-neighbors rule, run everywhere, naturally produces the global smoothing you see in the real world. You never need to code the global pattern — the local rule composes itself into it.

5 · The one weakness — the rule is fragile; too aggressive and it explodes

The stability parameter must stay below 0.5. If you set it to 0.4 the system is calm and smooth. If you bump it to 0.51 the very next thing that happens is wild oscillation and explosion — the values shoot to infinity. This is the CFL limit, and it is a hard constraint.

Why? Taking too large a step breaks the coupling. The system tries to correct too much too fast, overshoots, then overcorrects, spiraling into chaos. In real applications this means your grid size and time step must be chosen carefully, with no margin for error. It is one of the fundamental limitations of numerical methods: the finer your grid the smaller your time steps must be, and very fine grids become prohibitively slow.

6 · The surprise — almost all physics is PDEs, and this is exactly what neural networks are learning to solve

The heat equation is one instance of a vast family. Waves obey a PDE. Fluids obey the Navier-Stokes equations, a PDE. Electromagnetism is Maxwell's equations, four PDEs. Quantum mechanics is the Schrödinger equation. Climate, materials, combustion, chemical reactions — nearly every physical system in science is governed by a partial differential equation saying how quantities change at each point.

For decades physicists and engineers solved these by hand or with classical numerical methods like the one you just saw. Then came an idea: train a neural network not to predict a single output, but to learn the operator — the function-to-function map — that takes an initial state and produces the evolved state at later times. Feed it enough examples of 'input state → output state' and it can apply that learned map to new problems it never saw, much faster than solving the equation numerically each time. This is operator learning, and it is the foundation of modern physics-informed machine learning. The whole field stands on the observation that a neural network can learn to solve what PDEs describe.
