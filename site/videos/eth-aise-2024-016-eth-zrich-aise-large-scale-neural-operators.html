First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Solving Time-Dependent Physics Problems with Neural Operators, and Why Transformers Scale Better

The question it answers: How do you teach a neural network to solve physics problems that unfold over time? And why do Transformers—a completely different architecture based on 'attention'—turn out to be better at learning these patterns when you have lots of data?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you're trying to learn a rule that predicts how a fluid swirls as time passes. You have a snapshot at t=0, and you want to predict what it looks like at t=1 second, then t=2, and beyond. The trick is not to re-run the same prediction K times in sequence (which compounds errors), but to train on all the intermediate states at once, and let the network learn to jump directly from any starting point to any future time.

When solving time-dependent problems—things described by equations where change over time matters, like heat spreading or waves bouncing—the starting point is always an initial condition. A neural operator for time-dependent problems takes two inputs: a time value (t) and an initial state (u₀), and outputs the solution at that time. The challenge is that a neural network is just a fixed transformation learned from examples; it has no built-in clock or sense of time unless you explicitly feed time information into it at every layer, not just at the start.

The first move is 'lead time conditioning': instead of feeding time only at the input, you inject time as additional learned parameters at every normalization layer (the layers that rescale intermediate activations to keep gradients healthy). This is done through small learned networks (MLPs) that take the time value and produce scale and shift factors. Why every layer? Because in a deep network with 20-30 stacked layers, time information fed only at the beginning gets buried and diluted—the later layers lose the sense of what time horizon they're predicting to.

The second move is 'all-to-all training': instead of training only on (initial state → final state at time T), you train on every possible pair of states in your trajectory. If you have K time snapshots, you generate K·(K+1)/2 training samples from a single trajectory—not just one. From the initial state, you can reach any of the K-1 later times. From the first snapshot, you can reach any of the remaining snapshots. This works because time-dependent solutions obey causality: you can only move forward in time, never backward. So all these transitions are valid and already present in your data—you're just reusing them.

The third move is flexible inference: once trained, you're not locked into one-step rollouts. At test time, you can either do a 'direct evaluation' (give the network an initial condition and ask for the answer at a far future time in one shot, which is cheap but may be inaccurate for long times), or 'autoregressive rollout' (step forward one time interval at a time, feeding each predicted state back as input, which is slower but often more accurate), or anything in between (jump 3 steps, then 5 steps, then 2 more—whatever works). The network was trained to handle any lead time, so it's ready for any strategy.

The scaling results show the strategy works: error decreases predictably as you add more training data—a power law, not saturation. The error grows somewhat as you predict farther into the future (predicting t=1.0 is harder than t=0.2 from the initial state), but it grows linearly, not explosively. This suggests the network is genuinely learning the long-term dynamics, not memorizing.

Then the lecture pivots to a deeper question: what architecture scales best with data? Language models (especially after 2020-2021 papers by Kaplan et al.) revealed that Transformers consistently beat LSTMs and RNNs—they didn't saturate as quickly when given more compute and data. The same principle applies here: among neural operator architectures, the one that scales best with data should be preferred. This insight leads to Transformers.

Attention—the core of Transformers—is a mechanism to combine information from all points (or all tokens) at once, in a data-dependent way. Instead of convolving over local neighborhoods (like traditional CNOs), attention computes a weighted sum where the weights are determined by learned query, key, and value transformations. For each location, a 'query' vector asks 'which other locations are relevant to me?', and the 'key' vectors from all other locations answer via dot products, which are then softmaxed to yield attention weights. The 'value' vectors are then combined using those weights. Crucially, the kernel (the attention weights) is nonlinear and data-dependent—it adapts to the input.

This global, learned weighting is effective because (1) information flows from everywhere (not just local neighborhoods), (2) the network learns which connections matter for this specific problem, and (3) it's much more flexible than a fixed convolution kernel. The price is computational: attention is O(n²) in the sequence length, where convolution is O(n). But with enough data, Transformers have proven worth the cost. Practical tricks like multi-head attention (running H independent attention operations and summing them) and layer normalization (scaling each layer's output to unit variance per feature channel) make the architecture stable and efficient to train at scale.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Lead time conditioning: inject time into the network at every layer via learned functions, not just the input, so all layers stay aware of the time horizon being predicted.
  • All-to-all training: reuse causal relationships in trajectory data by training on all pairs of time steps, not just (initial → final). This quadratically expands your effective data size for free.
  • Flexible inference: the trained operator can make predictions at any lead time, and you can mix direct (one-shot) and autoregressive (step-wise) rollouts, or interpolate between them.
  • Scaling with data beats architecture tweaks: consistent power-law error decay with data volume is more important than architectural details. Transformers win because they scale better.
  • Attention as a data-dependent global kernel: each position learns to combine information from all others via a softmax-weighted sum of values, where weights (attention scores) are determined by learned query-key dot products.
  • Non-local and nonlinear: unlike convolutions (which operate on fixed local neighborhoods with fixed weights), attention captures long-range dependencies and adapts its weights to the actual data.
  • Multi-head attention and layer norm: multiple parallel attention operations (heads) running independently, plus careful normalization (scaling each feature channel to unit variance), stabilize training at scale.

Worked intuition: Imagine a 1D diffusion: a hot spot slowly spreading heat along a rod. You observe snapshots at times 0, 1, 2, 3 seconds. In 'one-at-a-time' training, you'd create just 3 training pairs: (t=0→t=1), (t=0→t=2), (t=0→t=3). In 'all-to-all', you also train on (t=1→t=2), (t=1→t=3), and (t=2→t=3)—every valid transition forward. You now have 6 pairs from the same trajectory. The network learns that 'heat spreads the same way regardless of when you start observing.' At test time, to predict what happens at a new time (say t=2.5, never seen during training), you can jump directly: feed the t=0 state with lead time 2.5. Or you can step: predict t=1, feed that to the network with lead time 1.5, get t=2.5. Both work because the network was trained on all time horizons.

Where it breaks down: All-to-all training requires all K snapshots to be available and aligned in time—harder if you have scattered, irregular observational data. The method also assumes causality (no backward time jumps); if the physics is reversible and you need to go backward, you'd have to handle that separately. Transformers' O(n²) attention cost becomes prohibitive for very long sequences (e.g., gigabyte-scale time-series data). For spatial functions, discretizing to a grid and then applying tokens assumes the grid is fine enough; on coarser grids, you lose spatial structure. Extrapolation far beyond training data still fails—the network generalizes better than autoregressive methods over moderate times, but not miracles.

Takeaway: To make neural operators work for time-evolving physics: (1) condition the architecture on time at every layer, (2) reuse causal structure in your data to synthesize more training pairs, and (3) choose an architecture that scales well with data—which increasingly means Transformers. The scaling insight from language models (bigger models + more data = better solutions, predictably) applies to physics problems too.
