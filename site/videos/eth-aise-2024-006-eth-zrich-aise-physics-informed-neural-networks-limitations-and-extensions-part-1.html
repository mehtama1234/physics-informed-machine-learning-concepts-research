First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Why Physics-Informed Neural Networks Are Slow, and How to Fix Them

The question it answers: Physics-informed neural networks promise to solve differential equations using machine learning—but they're often much slower than traditional numerical methods. What are the real bottlenecks, and what practical techniques actually make them competitive?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you're predicting earthquake waves through the earth. A traditional simulator takes one minute. A neural network trying the same thing takes an hour—and then has to retrain from scratch when you move the earthquake to a different location. Why is it so slow, and what can you do about it?

Physics-informed neural networks (PINs) solve differential equations by training a neural network to satisfy both boundary conditions and the physics equation itself. Instead of computing solutions on a fixed grid like traditional methods do, PINs give you a smooth function you can evaluate anywhere. This sounds great, but there's a hidden cost: you have to compute the network's gradients (how its output changes with respect to position and time) millions of times during training, and that takes an enormous amount of computation on a GPU. A seemingly simple 2D wave problem that takes 60 seconds on a traditional method can take an hour on a neural network, making it completely uncompetitive.

The real killer is reusability. In traditional simulation, once you solve for one earthquake location, you can instantly solve for a nearby earthquake—the program just runs again quickly. With PINs, the network was trained for a specific earthquake source location, so if you move the source even slightly, you have to retrain the entire network from scratch. This means for practical problems where you need many scenarios, you're paying the one-hour cost repeatedly. It's not just slow; it's wasteful.

The first major fix is conditioning: instead of training one network per scenario, train a single network that takes the source location (or other parameters) as an extra input alongside the spatial coordinates. During training, you sample over many source locations, boundary conditions, and initial configurations. Once trained, this network can instantly predict the solution for any combination of parameters without retraining. You're no longer throwing away computation; you're building a reusable surrogate model that answers 'what if' questions at the speed of a forward network pass.

A second approach is discretization: instead of having the network output a continuous function, have it output solution values on a fixed grid (like a matrix of pressure values). You replace exact gradients (which are expensive to compute) with finite differences (which are cheap—just arithmetic on neighboring grid points). This feels like stepping backward toward traditional methods, but it's not—you're still using a learnable neural network architecture, just with approximate gradients instead of exact ones. The trade-off is speed: you get 2-4x speedup by using finite differences instead of automatic differentiation.

Here's the mathematical payoff: with discretized PINs, you can use fancy architectures like convolutional networks that exploit grid structure. You add hard boundary constraints by padding the output grid with known values, so the boundary loss term vanishes entirely—no more tuning the tricky Lambda weights that balance boundary versus physics terms. A landmark 2021 paper showed this approach needs no simulation data to train; it only needs the physics equation itself plus the input domain description. Yet it generalizes better than pure data-driven methods when tested on scenarios outside the training distribution, suggesting the physics constraint teaches the network something reliable about the underlying system.

The third speed-up is to replace expensive automatic differentiation with finite difference approximations. By taking differences of the network output at nearby points instead of backpropagating gradients, you reduce training time by another factor of 2-4. The downside is approximation error: you lose the exact gradients, and tuning the 'step size' of your finite difference window becomes important. But for many problems, the speed gain is worth it.

All these fixes address the same root problem: automatic differentiation is computationally expensive at scale. PINs work by imposing the physics equation as a soft constraint during training, which requires computing gradients, which requires autodiff. Each fix sidesteps or cheapens that bottleneck. Conditioning lets one network serve many problems. Discretization lets you use cheap finite differences instead of autodiff. The key insight is that PINs don't need to be 'pure' to be useful—they work even if you approximate the gradients or restrict them to a grid.

But every fix introduces a trade-off. Conditioned networks lose the mesh-free property—they work best for fixed geometries. Discretized PINs depend on a fixed mesh, so you can't query the solution at arbitrary points like you could with the original formulation. Finite differences are approximate, so they accumulate error. There is no universal 'fast PIN' that beats every alternative in every scenario. The practical choice depends on your problem: Is speed or generalization more important? Do you have a fixed geometry? How many scenarios do you need to solve? The extensions give you tools to answer these questions, but you have to pick the right tool.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Automatic differentiation is the bottleneck—computing exact gradients during training makes standard PINs slow because the network's output derivatives must be computed thousands of times
  • Conditioning a PIN on extra inputs (like source location or initial conditions) lets a single trained network serve many scenarios, replacing retraining with fast inference
  • Discretized PINs output solution values on a fixed grid and use finite differences for gradients instead of autodiff, achieving 2-4x speedup at the cost of losing the mesh-free property
  • Physics constraints can teach networks reliability even without simulation data; unsupervised discretized PINs outgeneralize purely data-driven methods on out-of-distribution inputs
  • Every computational speed-up introduces a trade-off: conditioning sacrifices mesh-freedom, discretization fixes the geometry, finite differences trade exact gradients for approximation error

Worked intuition: Picture training a standard PIN to predict seismic waves from an earthquake. The network takes (x, y, z, t) coordinates as input and outputs pressure. To satisfy the wave equation, you need dP/dt and dP/dx and dP/dz—gradients of the output. Computing these via automatic differentiation for thousands of training points is slow. Now imagine instead conditioning the network: add the earthquake source location (xs, ys) as extra inputs. Train on earthquakes scattered across a region. Once done, you query the network with any source location—no retraining. Speed comes from amortizing the training cost across many scenarios. Alternatively, discretize: instead of a continuous function, the network outputs a 3D pressure grid (say, 100×100×100 values). To compute gradients, just subtract neighboring grid values (finite difference)—arithmetic, not autodiff. You lose the ability to query at arbitrary points, but you gain speed and can use convolutional layers. Both strategies work; neither dominates everything.

Where it breaks down: Conditioned PINs work best when the parameter space is bounded (earthquake locations within a region, not anywhere on Earth) and the domain geometry is fixed. Discretized PINs are locked to their training grid resolution and geometry—you can't simulate at higher resolution without retraining. Finite difference approximations accumulate error and require tuning the step size. None of these fixes make PINs faster than traditional methods for simple, forward-only simulations on regular geometries; traditional methods still win there. PINs shine when you need inverse problems (finding parameters from data), when the geometry is irregular, or when you want to solve many coupled problems simultaneously. The extensions fix computational speed but don't address the underlying issue that standard neural networks struggle with high-frequency oscillations—a deeper limitation requiring architectural innovation (which the next lecture covers).

Takeaway: Slow PINs aren't fundamentally broken; they're solving a harder problem than traditional simulators (learning from physics instead of numerical discretization). The practical extensions—conditioning for parameter reuse, discretization for cheap gradients, finite differences for speed—are not Band-Aids. They're recognizing that PINs don't need to be pure to be useful. Pick the variant that matches your problem: Do you need one solution or many? Fixed geometry or arbitrary domains? Speed or exactness? The extensions give you a toolkit; the right choice depends on your constraints.
