First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: How to teach neural networks to predict how things change over time

The question it answers: When you have a movie (or time-series data) of a changing system, how do you build a neural network that can generate a whole trajectory into the future, not just one snapshot? And how do you actually train it without errors piling up?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you train a weather model to predict tomorrow's weather from today's. That's easy. But then at inference, if you want to predict next week's weather, you feed your prediction back in, over and over. Each small mistake grows. You're essentially asking: how do you build a model that can keep going for hundreds of steps without spiraling into nonsense?

First, let's be clear about what the problem actually is. In previous lectures, you learned about operator learning: taking an initial condition (like a snapshot of a fluid flow) and predicting the solution at one specific later time. That's clean: you give one image, you get one image back. But real time-dependent problems are messier. In nature and in computers, time flows forward in discrete steps. When you simulate the Navier-Stokes equations, you don't just compute the final state—you march forward hour by hour, frame by frame, and at each step you save a snapshot. What you actually have is a video: a sequence of images with time baked in. The natural form of scientific data is trajectories, not isolated pairs.

Here's the key insight from causality: in a time-dependent system, you can compose solutions. If I know how the system evolves from time zero to time T, and separately from time T to time 2T, I can chain them together to get from zero to 2T. This is the semigroup property. It means that time gives the system a direction—you can always march forward, but not always backward (only reversible systems go backward). This matters because it suggests we could train on short steps and reuse them, like a numerical solver does. But there's a catch.

The naive approach would be to build K different neural operators, each one predicting the solution at a different time level. Operator one predicts the state at t=0.1, operator two at t=0.2, and so on. This is horrifying: you need hundreds of models. And worse, you're not using the fact that you already know the state at t=0.1—you could use that to predict t=0.2. You'd be throwing away information. So this approach fails immediately.

What people actually do is autoregressive evaluation, also called rollout. You train one operator to predict the next frame given the current frame, and at test time you apply it repeatedly: feed in the initial condition, get frame 1; feed in frame 1, get frame 2; feed in frame 2, get frame 3. Like a language model that generates one word at a time. During training, since each small prediction problem is the same (delta T is fixed and uniform), you can just use supervised learning. This is seductive because it looks like standard next-frame prediction, and it's what 80-90% of papers do. The operator doesn't even need to know about time—it just takes one image and produces the next one, and the time step is implicit.

But there are three serious problems with rollout. First: error accumulation. Each neural network prediction has some error—maybe 1% on a single frame. When you chain 50 predictions together, errors compound. You start with clean initial conditions from summer, predict forward frame by frame, and by frame 50 you're giving predictions for winter. The model was trained on summer weather, so summer errors accumulate into impossible weather. The error isn't random—it's biased, because the model has never seen the regime it's being asked to predict into. You could try to train on longer rollouts to fix this, but then backpropagation through 50 steps hits the exploding/vanishing gradient problem: gradients either shrink to zero or blow up, and learning breaks.

Second: you're limited to discrete time. You can only evaluate at the exact time points in your training data. If your data has frames at t=0, 0.1, 0.2, ..., you can only predict at those points. Real PDEs are continuous in time—there's no reason you shouldn't be able to predict at t=0.157. Third: you're not actually using the trajectory data as richly as you could. In your training set, for each initial condition, you have all K frames. But rollout training only uses the pairs (frame i, frame i+1). You're ignoring the structure that frame 0 → frame K uniquely determines how frames 0 → 1 → 2 → ... → K evolve together.

The solution is time conditioning. Instead of hiding time and learning an implicit time step, you make time explicit as an input to the network. Time becomes another channel, like temperature or pressure. So your operator takes: the initial condition (an image) and the time you want the solution at, and outputs the solution at that time. This sounds simple, but there's a subtlety. If you just concatenate time once at the input and send it through a 20-layer network, by layer 10 the network has effectively forgotten about the time value—it's been processed and mixed away. So instead of giving time once, you condition every layer on time. This is done through conditional normalization. Every layer in the network has a batch-norm or layer-norm step. Normally, normalization rescales the intermediate values to have mean zero and unit variance. But in time-conditioned networks, you also apply a learnable transformation that depends on time. So the normalization at layer 3 is different from the normalization at layer 4 if you're asking for a different time. Mathematically, you're adding time-dependent matrices and biases to the normalization—typically learned by small neural networks. The effect is that time drips through the entire network, modulating how features are normalized at each step.

Here's what you gain: now you can actually train on the full trajectory. Given one initial condition and its trajectory (frames 0 through K), you can create training pairs like (initial condition, t=0.1) → frame at 0.1, (initial condition, t=0.5) → frame at 0.5, and so on. Suddenly you have K times more training data from each trajectory. You're also free from the rollout error accumulation—each prediction is directly from the initial condition, not chained predictions. And you can evaluate at any time, not just grid points. The trade-off is that your network is now more intricate: it has to learn time-conditioned operations at every layer. But the data advantage—having K independent training pairs per trajectory instead of K-1 sequential pairs in rollout—often wins out in practice, especially when data is expensive (which it always is in scientific computing).

Key ideas in plain words

  • Time-dependent problems come naturally as trajectories (videos, sequences)—the data has an intrinsic time direction—but standard operators were built to ignore time and just predict one final snapshot.
  • Rollout (autoregressive evaluation) is the naive fix: train one operator to predict the next frame, then apply it repeatedly. It works but accumulates errors, can't do continuous time, and wastes trajectory information by only using sequential pairs.
  • Error accumulation happens because small errors compound over many steps, and the network drifts into regimes it was never trained on. Backpropagating through 50+ steps also causes vanishing/exploding gradients during training.
  • Time conditioning makes time explicit as an input to the neural operator, so the network learns to output different solutions for different times given the same initial condition.
  • Conditional normalization (applying time-dependent transformation at every layer's normalization) keeps time information alive deep in the network, instead of letting it be forgotten after the first few layers.
  • Time-conditioned networks extract K times more training pairs from each trajectory (one pair per time point, not just sequential pairs), which is a huge data advantage when simulations are expensive.
  • The approach is general: any architecture that uses normalization (convolution, transformers, etc.) can be conditioned on time by modifying how normalization works in each layer.

Worked intuition: Let's make this concrete with a heat equation. Suppose you simulate heat diffusing through a metal rod and save 10 frames. Old approach: train operator_0 on (initial, frame_5), operator_5 on (initial, frame_10), etc.—separate models. Rollout approach: train one model on (frame_i, frame_{i+1}) pairs—so (frame_0, frame_1), (frame_1, frame_2), ..., (frame_9, frame_10)—and at test time, feed the output back in. Problem: frame_1 prediction has 1% error, frame_2 now has error from both steps, by frame_10 the heat distribution is completely wrong. Time-conditioning approach: train one model that takes (initial, time=0.1) and outputs frame_1, (initial, time=0.2) and outputs frame_2, ..., (initial, time=1.0) and outputs frame_10. Now you have 10 independent training examples from one trajectory. At test time, you just ask the network 'given this initial condition, what's the heat at time=0.47?' and it answers directly, no chaining, no error accumulation. The network learns internally that temperature diffuses smoothly over time, and time-conditioned normalization ensures that at each layer, the representation is adapted for the time you're querying.

Where it breaks down: Time conditioning assumes you can condition every layer's normalization on time, which works well for architectures with built-in normalization (convnets with batch-norm, transformers with layer-norm). For architectures without normalization or with atypical designs, the trick needs adaptation. Also, the approach assumes you have the full trajectory for each training example—if your data only gives you pairs (initial, final) without intermediate frames, time conditioning gives back some but not all of the data advantage. On very long trajectories (predicting 1000 steps into the future), even with time conditioning, you're asking the network to extrapolate far beyond its training range, which can break down if the system has transients or instabilities. The method also assumes uniform spacing in time for simplicity—non-uniform sampling requires a bit more care. And like all neural networks, generalization to new initial conditions or parameters depends on training diversity; if your initial conditions are all drawn from one narrow regime, the network won't extrapolate well to others.

Takeaway: Time-dependent neural operators should take time as an explicit input, conditioning every layer on time through normalization, rather than hiding time and using rollout. This avoids error accumulation, enables continuous-time prediction, and multiplies your effective training data by the trajectory length. It's a small architectural change with a large practical payoff.
