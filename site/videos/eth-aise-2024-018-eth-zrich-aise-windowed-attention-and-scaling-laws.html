First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: Why Transformers Need Help to Learn Equations Efficiently

The question it answers: Can we scale Transformers up to solve equations faster and better, or do they just need more data than existing methods?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine reading a page from a book—you don't compare every letter to every other letter. You focus on nearby words first, then connect those clusters together. That's what windowed attention does: it breaks the enormously expensive "look at everything at once" problem into manageable, overlapping local conversations.

When you have a Transformer and you want it to learn to solve a complicated equation (a PDE), the first thing you might try is to feed it the equation data directly. But there's a huge problem: standard Transformers compute 'attention'—a way of comparing every piece of information to every other piece. If you have 1,000 patches (pieces) of space to work with, you need 1,000 × 1,000 = one million comparisons. That's quadratic cost, and in 2D or 3D problems, it becomes nearly impossible. You can patch the data—chop your spatial domain into bigger chunks—to reduce the number of things being compared. Instead of comparing at the pixel level, you compare at the 16×16 patch level. But even that isn't enough. For problems in fluids or heat diffusion, the patches still represent too much averaged information, and you lose the detail you need.

The key insight is that you don't actually need global attention everywhere. Most of the physics is local—what happens at one spot depends mainly on nearby spots, not on a point far away on the other side of the domain. So instead of having every patch attend to every other patch, you divide the spatial domain into windows—larger blocks that contain many patches—and allow attention to happen only inside each window. This cuts the computation dramatically: now instead of quadratic in total patches, it's quadratic only within each window. If you have 4 × 4 patches in each window, the cost is manageable.

But now you face a new problem: if each window acts independently, information can't travel across window boundaries. Two nearby points on opposite sides of a window border have no path to talk to each other. The solution is elegant and simple: shift the windows in alternating layers. In layer 1, windows are aligned one way. In layer 2, you shift them by half a window size, so points that were on opposite sides of a border in layer 1 are now in the same window. In layer 3, you shift again. This way, over multiple layers, every point eventually gets to interact with its neighbors, but through a series of local conversations rather than one global one. This is called 'path length reduction'—information spreads through a chain of local connections rather than disappearing into unattended distance.

Beyond just attention, you need the Transformer's layers to process information at multiple scales simultaneously. Single-scale processing isn't expressive enough. Instead, the architecture (called a Swin Transformer or Scalable Operator Transformer) uses an encoder-decoder structure like a U-Net: the encoder coarsens the spatial resolution and increases the number of channels (features), processing the domain at progressively larger scales. The decoder then expands the resolution back down while reducing channels. At each scale, you do local Transformer processing. This is similar to how convolutional neural operators (CNOs) work—multiscale information helps you capture both large-scale patterns and fine details.

The challenge of scaling large Transformers is more than just architecture. When you increase the size of your model, you can run into problems with training: gradients don't flow as smoothly, and the optimization landscape becomes harder to navigate. The paper and this lecture discuss several small but important technical tricks: using cosine similarity instead of raw dot products for attention (which auto-normalizes), encoding position using relative distances on a log scale rather than absolute positions, and adjusting where layer normalization happens in the block. None of these alone is revolutionary, but together they help the model train more stably as you add more layers.

Despite all this engineering, the experiments show a sobering result: Transformers do not dramatically beat existing methods like Fourier Neural Operators (FNO) or Convolutional Neural Operators (CNO). On elliptic equations (steady-state problems), CNO is more sample-efficient than Transformers—it reaches good accuracy with fewer training examples. On time-dependent problems like the wave equation or Navier-Stokes, Transformers eventually scale better than FNO, but they're comparable to CNO, not orders of magnitude better. Transformers need a lot of data to shine. The smaller 20-million-parameter Transformer sometimes outperforms the 40-million-parameter version because there simply isn't enough training data to feed the larger model.

The real promise comes from a different direction: foundation models. The idea is to take your Transformer (or CNO) and pre-train it on a huge, diverse dataset of many PDEs—Navier-Stokes with different coefficients, reaction-diffusion equations, elliptic problems, time-dependent problems, all mixed together. This 200-million-parameter foundation model learns general patterns about how PDEs behave. Then, when you want to solve a specific new PDE, you don't start training from scratch. Instead, you fine-tune: take the pre-trained model and train it a little bit more on just a few new examples specific to your problem. The initial experiment is striking: where a standard Transformer needed 248 training samples to reach 3% error, a fine-tuned foundation model reaches the same error with 8 samples. This mirrors the success of large language models, which work best when you pre-train on internet-scale diverse data and then adapt to your specific task.

The honest takeaway is that Transformers with windowed attention and hierarchical structure are computationally viable and can work, but they are not a silver bullet. They require careful engineering to scale, they need lots of data to beat existing methods, and the gains are incremental rather than transformative. However, the foundation model approach—combining pre-training on diverse problems with fine-tuning on your specific case—is emerging as the most promising direction. This moves the field closer to how large language models have succeeded: learn broad patterns from scale and diversity, then specialize quickly with little task-specific data. But this is contemporary research, not yet fully understood, and represents the frontier of what's working right now.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Windowed attention: instead of comparing every piece of data to every other (quadratic cost), divide the domain into windows and do attention only within windows, then shift windows across layers so information can travel between them via overlapping connections.
  • Multiscale architecture: use an encoder-decoder structure to process information at coarse and fine scales simultaneously, capturing both large patterns and fine details, similar to existing convolutional neural operators.
  • Technical scaling tricks: cosine similarity, relative positional encoding on log scale, careful placement of layer normalization—small changes that improve training stability for very large models.
  • Empirical reality check: Transformers are not dramatically better than existing methods like CNO on most problems; they're competitive but data-hungry and need enough training examples to justify their added difficulty.
  • Foundation models as the path forward: pre-train on diverse PDEs at large scale, then fine-tune on your specific problem with few samples—this mirrors the success of large language models and shows the most promise for sample efficiency.
  • Data-model balance: there is a delicate equilibrium between how much data you have, how large your model is, and how well it will work. Too much model for too little data and the larger version actually performs worse than a smaller one.
  • Still an open frontier: the relationship between data scale, model size, and generalization in neural operator learning is not yet well understood and represents active contemporary research.

Worked intuition: Picture a 4×4 grid of patches on a map. In layer 1 of your Transformer, you partition this into four 2×2 windows (top-left, top-right, bottom-left, bottom-right), and attention happens only within each window. A patch in the top-left window cannot see into the top-right window. But in layer 2, you shift all windows by half a window width and height, so the boundaries move. Now, the patch that was isolated in the top-left corner of its window may now sit in the middle of a new 2×2 window with patches that include neighbors from what was previously unreachable. After a few layers of shifting, every patch has had a chance to mix with its neighbors through this chain of local windows. The cost at each layer is only 2×2 = 4 comparisons per window, times the number of windows (still manageable), whereas a single global attention layer would do 4×4 = 16 comparisons on every pair, and with thousands of patches that becomes intractable. This is why windowing plus shifting is so clever: it maintains the mixing power of Transformers while cutting computation from quadratic in total size to quadratic in window size.

Where it breaks down: Transformers struggle when you have limited training data—they need orders of magnitude more examples than simpler methods like CNOs to reach the same accuracy on single-problem tasks. They also require careful tuning of architecture details (window size, number of layers, shifting patterns) and training tricks (optimizer choice, normalization placement) to work at scale. On small datasets typical of individual PDE problems, a CNN-based operator is often more sample-efficient. The method also doesn't solve the fundamental issue of training physics-informed neural networks—adding a physics loss term to the objective doesn't automatically make training easier or faster, and gradient propagation through differential operators remains numerically difficult. Finally, windowed Transformers lose the global receptive field that some problems might benefit from, and while alternating shifts help, they can't fully restore full-domain information mixing.

Takeaway: Windowed attention makes Transformers computationally feasible for spatial problems, but the real innovation is recognizing that scale and diversity matter more than a single clever architecture: pre-train a large model on many equations, then fine-tune it quickly on your specific problem, and you get the best of both worlds—expressivity from a huge model and sample efficiency from transfer learning.
