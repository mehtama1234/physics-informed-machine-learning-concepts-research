First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: How to make convolutions work for learning physics: convolutional neural operators

The question it answers: Deep learning works amazingly well for images using convolutions, but when we try to learn solutions to physics problems (PDEs), convolutions fail: they don't preserve the mathematical structure we need. Can we fix convolutions so they work for physics?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine you have a recipe for making bread that works if your oven is exactly 400°F. But if the temperature changes, the recipe breaks—the bread turns out completely different. Now imagine a better recipe that works at ANY temperature, whether your oven is 350°F or 425°F. That's the difference: most neural networks fail when the grid resolution changes, but the method in this lecture works no matter what resolution you use.

The fundamental problem is this: physics is described by PDEs (partial differential equations)—mathematical rules that describe how things like temperature, pressure, or velocity change across space and time. To solve a PDE means to learn the operator—the mathematical machine that takes an input function (like initial pressure everywhere) and outputs an answer (like pressure at a later time everywhere). But this operator lives in infinite-dimensional function spaces, not in discrete grids. When you train a neural network on one grid resolution and test it on another, the whole thing breaks. The convolutions in standard CNNs (convolutional neural networks) were designed for images, and they silently assume the input lives on a particular discrete grid. If you change the grid, the math no longer works.

The key insight is to work with band-limited functions—functions that only contain frequencies up to some cutoff point. In the Fourier transform (a way to decompose any signal into pure frequencies), a band-limited function has zero energy above some frequency W. This matters because of the Nyquist theorem from signal processing: if your spacing between grid points is small enough (the Nyquist condition), then there is a perfect, one-to-one match between the continuous function and the discrete grid points. You can go from continuous to discrete and back without losing information. This is the bridge we'll use.

Here's the first fix for convolutions. Instead of treating the kernel (the filter we slide across the signal) as if it lives on a particular grid, we keep it discrete but act with it continuously. The convolution kernel is a sum of Dirac delta functions at fixed points. When we convolve with a band-limited function on a grid, we upsample the grid points to the continuous function (using sinc interpolation), apply the kernel at the original fixed points, and then resample back to the new grid. This way, the kernel stays fixed but the input functions are rescaled to match it. The operator commutes—you get the same answer whether you compute on a coarse grid, lift to continuous, resample to coarse, apply the kernel, or any other path through the diagram.

The second and trickier fix is for nonlinearities like ReLU. The problem: when you apply ReLU pointwise to a band-limited function, you create new high frequencies that weren't there before. This violates the band-limit assumption. The solution is surprisingly elegant: before applying the nonlinearity, upsample the function to a higher bandwidth (say, double the frequencies). This temporarily creates room for the new frequencies that the nonlinearity will generate. After the nonlinearity, use a low-pass filter to downsample back to the original bandwidth. The high-frequency noise created by the nonlinearity gets quenched. This process is non-local (it involves the entire continuous function, not just local points), but it preserves the structure needed to be a true neural operator.

For polynomial and rational nonlinearities, this is exact—you can compute exactly how much the bandwidth must grow (for squaring, double it; for fourth power, quadruple it). For smooth nonlinearities like ReLU or Swish, it's approximate, but the aliasing error is much smaller than if you didn't upsample. In practice, doubling the bandwidth is usually enough because the coefficients of very high frequencies naturally decay. The cost is real: you must evaluate the function at more grid points during the nonlinearity. But this is the price of keeping the structure.

A bare-bones architecture would just stack convolutions and these special nonlinearities. But from computer vision, we know you need more sophistication. Real systems have multi-scale structure: both coarse features (the outline of a face) and fine details (exact location of eyes). The Convolutional Neural Operator uses a U-Net-style architecture. You progressively downsample (coarsen the grid, add channels) through an encoder, process at the bottleneck, then progressively upsample through a decoder, with skip connections that pass information from the encoder to the decoder. Residual connections (shortcuts around processing blocks) help gradients flow and preserve high-frequency information. Each building block—downsampling, convolution, nonlinearity—is itself a neural operator, so composing them preserves the property.

Why does this work empirically? The Navier-Stokes equations are a great test: they describe fluid flow and are genuinely hard. When you train a standard CNN or FNO at 64×64 resolution and then test at 32×32, both fail in different ways: errors explode because the discrete assumptions break. DeepONet falls apart completely, learning nothing but the mean. FNO does better (4% error) but introduces aliasing artifacts—visible ringing in the solution. A U-Net without the upsampling/downsampling (which violates the structure) also shows aliasing. But the Convolutional Neural Operator, built to be a true neural operator, maintains accuracy and structure across different resolutions. When you look at error spectra, standard methods show corruption in high frequencies, while CNO stays clean near machine precision.

The theoretical foundation is that neural operators can universally approximate operators, just as neural networks approximate functions. If an operator is continuous (small input changes yield small output changes), then a sufficiently large CNO can approximate it to arbitrary accuracy. This works because band-limited functions form a complete space for representing PDEs, and you can apply the universal approximation theorem to finite-dimensional neural networks within that space, then project back. You gain one level of abstraction—from approximating functions to approximating the infinite-dimensional mappings between them—but the principle is identical.

Key ideas in plain words

  • Band-limited functions are the secret: restrict to functions with frequencies up to some cutoff. This creates a one-to-one correspondence between continuous functions and discrete grid points (if grid is fine enough). No aliasing, perfect information transfer.
  • Fix convolutions by keeping the kernel fixed and resampling the input. The kernel lives at fixed locations. When the grid resolution changes, you resample the input function to match the kernel's reference points, apply the convolution, then resample back. The operator commutes—structure is preserved.
  • Fix nonlinearities with upsampling and downsampling. Before applying ReLU or other activations, allow higher frequencies (upsample). The nonlinearity can then create new frequencies safely. After, low-pass filter (downsample) back to the original bandwidth. This way, the created high frequencies don't corrupt the result.
  • True neural operators must satisfy continuous-discrete equivalence: the diagram where you go continuous→discrete→operate→discrete→continuous must commute. Polynomial and rational nonlinearities satisfy this exactly. Smooth nonlinearities (ReLU, Swish) satisfy it approximately but well enough to reduce aliasing dramatically.
  • Multi-scale processing is essential. Use a U-Net architecture: encoder (downsample while adding channels), bottleneck, decoder (upsample while removing channels). Process coarse and fine features at their own scales. Skip connections and residual shortcuts prevent gradient problems and preserve high-frequency information.
  • Empirical proof: train on one grid resolution, test on another. Standard CNNs and FNOs fail (errors explode, aliasing appears). Convolutional Neural Operators maintain accuracy and clean spectra, proving they are true neural operators—they work on any resolution.
  • Universal approximation for operators: any continuous operator between function spaces can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy by a neural operator, given enough parameters. This lifts the function approximation theorem to the operator level.

Worked intuition: Let's work through the Navier-Stokes example concretely. You have a velocity field (how fast fluid is moving at each point) at time zero. A convolution alone preserves the band limit—if your input is band-limited, the output is too (because convolution with a discrete kernel preserves that property). But then you apply ReLU (set negative values to zero). This is a nonlinearity. A smooth curve becoming piecewise linear—this creates high-frequency Fourier components out of nowhere. If you didn't prepare for it, these frequencies alias (wrap around and corrupt the low frequencies). Solution: before ReLU, you upsample from say 4 frequencies to 8 frequencies. This is automatic in the continuous picture—you're just evaluating the sinc-interpolated function at more points. ReLU now acts on these 8-frequency values. Some values become zero, some stay positive. The new signal now has up to 16 frequencies (by the squaring property of frequency growth). You downsample back to 4 frequencies using sinc again (keeping the important low frequencies, throwing away high noise). The network has learned to manage frequency growth on its own. When you change the grid resolution (coarser or finer), this process scales automatically—the sinc interpolation adapts. That's why it's a true neural operator.

Where it breaks down: The method has real limits. First, computational cost: upsampling multiplies grid points by 2, 4, or more. A 1024×1024 image becomes expensive fast. Second, the approximation for non-polynomial nonlinearities (like ReLU) is approximate, not exact. You lose some information when you downsample, and that error accumulates through layers. For highly oscillatory solutions (signals with very sharp features at very high frequencies), the assumption that band-limited functions capture the problem breaks down. Third, the architecture requires significant hyperparameter tuning: what bandwidth multiplier? How many U-Net levels? How many channels? Too few parameters and you can't learn the problem; too many and you overfit and burn compute. Finally, the theory guarantees universal approximation only with infinite layers and parameters; in practice, you're always approximating an approximation.

Takeaway: The insight is not that convolutions are bad—they're actually crucial for preserving locality and efficiency. The insight is that you must be honest about what space you're working in. If you pretend a discrete grid IS a continuous function and treat it that way, you fail when grids change. Instead, explicitly work with band-limited functions where discrete and continuous are equivalent. Keep kernels fixed and resample inputs. Manage frequency growth explicitly during nonlinearities. Build multi-scale architectures to handle different structural scales. When you do this, you get neural operators: networks that work on any resolution, any grid, any discretization—they learn the underlying continuous physics, not the grid.
