First-Principles Walkthrough

What this lecture is really about: What Neural Networks Are and How to Train Them with Data

The question it answers: How can we learn the physics of a system (captured by differential equations) from data using neural networks? What is actually happening inside a neural network, and why are they so hard to train?

Something concrete to hold onto: Imagine neurons in your brain as tiny decision-makers: they receive signals, weigh them, and decide whether to pass a message along. A neural network mimics this by stacking these simple decision units—each one doing a tiny bit of work, but together learning to predict temperature from pressure, or motion from forces.

The story of neural networks starts in 1943 with McCulloch and Pitts, who realized something remarkable: the brain's neurons could be captured in math as simple logical devices. Each biological neuron collects electrical inputs, weighs them through chemical channels, and fires (or doesn't) based on whether a threshold is crossed. They abstracted this into the threshold logic unit—a single number that takes multiple inputs (x1, x2, etc.), multiplies each by a weight (w1, w2), adds a bias term, and then applies a threshold step function. This was not meant to be biologically perfect; it was a massive simplification. But it worked mathematically. The genius move came in 1957 when Rosenblatt realized these weights didn't have to be fixed—they could be learned from data. This learning capacity was the birth of the modern perceptron.

When you stack many of these simple units together in layers, you get a neural network (also called a multi-layer perceptron or MLP). Here's the structure: you take an input (say, a vector of numbers describing a system's state), pass it through a layer that multiplies by weights and adds bias—this is called an affine transformation, borrowing from geometry. Then you apply a scalar nonlinearity (a function applied element-by-element, like ReLU or hyperbolic tangent) that introduces the essential nonlinearity. You repeat this affine-then-nonlinearity pattern layer after layer. The output is your prediction. The whole thing is just a mathematical function: given input, produce output. But it's a parametric function—its behavior depends entirely on the weights and biases. Change those parameters and you have a completely different function.

The activation function (the nonlinearity) is crucial. Early networks used the Heaviside step function—inspired directly by thresholds in biology. But this turns out to be terrible for learning: its gradient (the slope, used for optimization) is zero almost everywhere. Instead, modern networks use smooth approximations like sigmoid (which looks S-shaped) or hyperbolic tangent. The most popular choice today is ReLU—rectified linear unit—which simply keeps positive values and zeros out negative ones. ReLU is fast and works well, but it has a kink (the gradient jumps from zero to one), which is why variants like Leaky ReLU exist. The choice of activation matters somewhat, but the deeper secret is that almost any smooth nonlinear activation function will work. The universal approximation theorem tells us something profound: any continuous function (describing any physics you care about) can be approximated arbitrarily closely using a neural network with just one hidden layer, as long as it has the right weights and bias.

But here's the million-dollar question: how do we find those weights? This is where supervised learning comes in. You don't have access to the ground truth function f(y) that governs your system everywhere. Instead, you probe the system at N sample points y₁, y₂, etc.—imagine running experiments or simulations—and measure the output (with a bit of noise added by measurement error). This creates a labeled dataset: input-output pairs. Your task is to find weights such that when you feed these input points into the neural network, the predicted outputs match the measured outputs as closely as possible. You quantify 'closeness' with a loss function—typically the L2 norm (mean squared error), which sums the squared differences between prediction and truth across all data points. The goal of training is to minimize this loss by adjusting the weights.

This minimization problem is deceptively hard. First, the parameter space is enormous. Even a small neural network has thousands of parameters (weights and biases). Large practical networks have millions or billions. This means you're trying to minimize a function in a space with billions of dimensions. Second, and worse, the loss landscape is hideously non-convex. If it were convex (like a simple valley), you could guarantee finding the global minimum. But neural networks are non-convex because of the layered affine-nonlinearity structure. The composition of nonlinear functions creates a landscape full of valleys, ridges, and saddle points. You might get stuck in a local minimum—a place where every direction uphill looks worse, even though a much better solution exists elsewhere.

To solve this, almost everyone uses gradient descent. The idea is simple: compute the gradient (the direction of steepest descent), take a step down that direction, repeat. In pure form, you'd compute the loss across all N data points, then move your parameters. But this is slow and memory-hungry for large datasets. So in practice, people use stochastic gradient descent (SGD): pick a single data point or a small batch, compute the approximate gradient from that tiny sample, and step. This is a poor approximation of the true gradient, but it's much faster. The trade-off is noisier, more erratic progress—sometimes you move the wrong way. But with enough steps and a properly tuned learning rate (how big each step is), you can still converge to a reasonably good solution, not necessarily the global minimum but a local one that generalizes well.

The learning rate is critical and finicky. Too large and you overshoot and oscillate wildly. Too small and training crawls. This is why training neural networks feels like an art: practitioners learn tricks like warm-up schedules, adaptive learning rates (algorithms like Adam that adjust step size per parameter), and careful initialization of weights. Early in training, you have random, terrible weights, so the loss is huge. As you train, the loss drops—the network learns to fit the data. But there's a danger: the network can memorize the training data perfectly without learning general patterns, a problem called overfitting. You need a validation set to catch this. In the context of physics-informed machine learning, this is particularly important because we care about generalizing beyond the probe points we measured.

Key ideas in plain words

  • A neural network is a parametric function built from layers of affine transformations (multiply-weight-add-bias) followed by scalar nonlinearities (element-wise application of functions like ReLU). By composing many such layers, the network gains the capacity to approximate any continuous function (universal approximation theorem).
  • Training a neural network means finding weights and biases that minimize a loss function—the mismatch between the network's predictions and the measured data. This is solved iteratively using gradient descent, computing which direction decreases loss most steeply and stepping that way.
  • The optimization landscape is non-convex and high-dimensional. Gradient descent often gets trapped in local minima and is sensitive to the learning rate (step size). In practice, stochastic gradient descent uses small batches of data per step, trading computational speed for noisy, erratic progress.
  • Activation functions like ReLU (keep positives, zero negatives) introduce nonlinearity. They are essential: without them, stacking affine layers would collapse into a single affine layer. Different activations exist, but the main requirement is smoothness (or at least non-polynomial behavior) for universal approximation.

Worked intuition: Consider fitting a temperature profile to airflow data over an airfoil (airplane wing shape). You run simulations at different airfoil geometries and measure drag, lift, pressure. You have 100 probe points. A shallow neural network might look like: input (24 airfoil parameters) → 50 hidden neurons with ReLU → 3 output neurons (drag, lift, pressure). The hidden layer computes 50 weighted combinations of the 24 inputs, then applies ReLU (zero out negatives), then a final affine layer maps those 50 ReLU outputs to your 3 quantities. Training starts with random weights. You compute loss: average squared error between predicted and true (drag, lift, pressure). Gradient descent steepens on this landscape: which weight change reduces loss most? Adjust weights slightly in that direction. Repeat thousands of times. Eventually, the network learns which combinations of input geometry matter for each output.

Where it breaks down: The universal approximation theorem guarantees a neural network exists to fit any continuous function, but it's silent on three real obstacles. First, the proof is non-constructive—it doesn't tell you how many neurons you need or what weights to use. Second, finding those weights via gradient descent on non-convex loss is hard: you may converge to a local minimum far from optimal. Third, overfitting: the network may memorize training data without learning the underlying pattern, failing on new probe points. Additionally, this theory assumes access to plenty of labeled data; with only three measurements (as the lecturer mentions), modern neural networks are not reliable. The theory also assumes the true function is continuous, but physics can have discontinuities or bifurcations. Finally, computing gradients for billion-parameter networks is expensive: you need GPUs and distributed computing.

Takeaway: A neural network is a stack of simple affine-nonlinearity pairs (layers) that forms a parametric function. You find the right weights by minimizing a loss function using gradient descent, which is easy to understand but deceptively hard in practice because the landscape is non-convex and high-dimensional. The main miracle is that even with this difficulty, the simple compositional structure—matrix multiply, add bias, apply ReLU, repeat—turns out to be effective enough to learn physics from data. But you'll need a reasonable amount of clean data, careful tuning of hyperparameters (learning rate, network size), and patience to make it work.

Video Study Note

Plain title: Deep learning as learned prediction

Watch for: This lecture sets up neural networks as adjustable prediction machines. Listen for inputs, outputs, loss, training, and why old examples are not enough for trust.

Main Points Discussed

  • A neural network takes an input and produces an output through layers of adjustable numbers.
  • Training means changing those numbers so the output gets closer to known answers.
  • The scientific risk is that a model can fit familiar examples and still fail on a changed case.

First-principles point: A model earns attention only if it predicts a named quantity and is tested away from the examples it learned from.

Learner takeaway: Understand input, output, loss, and test case before worrying about the list of architectures.

Do not worry about this yet: Do not get stuck on every architecture name. The first job is to understand training as error correction.
