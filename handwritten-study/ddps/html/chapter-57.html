Narrative chapter

Close The Missing Scales Without Breaking Energy

Read This Chapter As One Claim

A closure is supposed to tell a coarse solver what the missing small scales do. It should not be a loose neural correction; it should live inside the actual discrete solver, preserve the system's energy bookkeeping, and be judged by rollout behavior.

Everyday problem The coarse model cannot afford every turbulent wiggle, cloud detail, wave scale, or subgrid motion, but those missing scales still push on the resolved state.
Shared promise Learn the missing effect in equation form, after discretization, with compressed subgrid variables that track hidden-scale energy exchange.
Hidden danger A closure can look accurate on snapshots while injecting energy the wrong way, drifting during rollout, or becoming invalid when the solver or grid changes.
First rejection test Reject the closure if it is not embedded in the target solver, does not preserve the needed conservation or dissipation structure, and is not tested by trajectory rollout.

This chapter uses video 60, Benjamin Sanderse's DDPS talk on structure-preserving learning of embedded, discrete closure models. The talk is about what to do when the full simulation has small scales we cannot afford to resolve, but those small scales still push on the large scales we care about.

The first-principles question is this: if a coarse simulation cannot see the small scales, how can a learned closure add their effect without making the coarse solver unstable or physically wrong?

full simulation has many scales
coarse solver misses small scales
learn the missing effect
preserve energy bookkeeping

Why Closure Models Exist

Fluid and climate problems contain many scales. In a wind farm, the farm may span kilometers, blades span meters, and turbulent features near the blade can be much smaller. In clouds, one may care about weather-scale behavior without resolving every small cloud detail.

A closure model is a rule for the effect of what was not resolved. It does not try to reconstruct every missing wiggle. It tries to say how the missing wiggles push, drain, feed, or reshape the resolved large-scale motion.

large scales are resolved
small scales are filtered out
small scales still affect large scales
closure supplies that effect

A Naive Learned Correction Can Look Good Briefly

The talk begins with a simple convection-diffusion-style example. A neural network is trained as a correction inside a coarse time step. On a plot, the result can look much better than the coarse model with no closure.

But that is not the end of the story. A learned correction can match snapshots and still break a conservation law, push energy the wrong way, or become unstable when embedded in the actual time-dependent solver.

train correction offline
coarse result improves
solver later feeds itself
missing structure can break stability

Write The Closure In The Equation Form

The first repair is to put the learned term in a form that matches the equation. If the original equation is in divergence form, write the closure as a divergence of a learned quantity instead of adding an arbitrary learned field.

This simple change can improve accuracy because it tells the network what kind of object it is allowed to be. The model does not have to rediscover conservation structure from data alone.

equation has known form
arbitrary neural term is too free
learn inside the same form
accuracy and meaning improve

Discretize First, Then Learn The Closure

Classical closure modeling often starts in the continuous equation and then discretizes. The talk argues for another route: discretize the full-scale system first, then reduce or filter it, then learn the closure at the discrete level.

This makes the learned closure tailored to the actual code, grid, and time stepping that will use it. The training environment and prediction environment match more closely.

full model on fine grid
filter to coarse grid
learn discrete closure
tailored to the solver

The Price Is Solver Specificity

A closure learned after discretization is not universal in the old sense. If the grid, filter, numerical method, or solver code changes, the closure may need retraining.

That is not necessarily a flaw. In an engineering workflow, a closure that works well inside the exact production solver may be more useful than a general-looking closure that is unstable when actually embedded.

tailored closure
matches one discretization
change solver or grid
retrain or recheck

Energy Is The Stability Ledger

Many physical systems carry a conservation or dissipation law. For incompressible flow, kinetic energy has a known balance. For the Korteweg-de Vries example in the talk, an energy-like quantity is conserved.

Energy bookkeeping is a stability ledger. If a closure injects or removes energy in a way the system cannot support, the simulation can drift or blow up. A structure-preserving closure is designed so this bookkeeping remains controlled.

physical system has energy balance
closure exchanges energy
wrong exchange destabilizes
preserve the ledger

Large-Scale Energy Alone Is Not Conserved

After filtering, the resolved large scales do not necessarily conserve the same energy by themselves. Some energy lives in the unresolved small scales. If we only track the coarse field, we lose part of the energy story.

The talk therefore introduces an extra compressed variable that represents the unresolved-scale energy. It is not the full small-scale field. It is a compact bookkeeping variable that lets the closure system track energy exchange between resolved and unresolved parts.

resolved field energy
missing subgrid energy
learn compressed variable
track exchange between scales

The Closure Becomes An Extended System

Instead of evolving only the coarse resolved variable, the method evolves the resolved variable plus the compressed subgrid variable. That extended state carries both the visible large-scale motion and a compact memory of hidden-scale energy.

The learned operator is then structured so the energy exchange has the right sign and pairing. The design is not just about lower error. It is about making instability impossible or much less likely by construction.

coarse resolved state
compressed hidden-scale state
structured learned exchange
stable embedded closure

The Korteweg-De Vries Example Tests The Idea

The talk uses the Korteweg-de Vries equation as a clean test case because it has wave behavior and an energy conservation structure. A fine-grid simulation is reduced to a much coarser grid, for example from hundreds of points to a few dozen.

The structure-preserving closure gives a coarse solution that tracks the reference better than a standard convolutional neural network closure. More importantly, it also produces meaningful behavior for the subgrid energy variable, which ordinary closures do not provide.

fine KdV waves
coarse grid misses detail
energy-aware closure
coarse and subgrid behavior improve

Derivative Fitting Is Cheap But Can Mislead

One training option is derivative fitting. Use training data to estimate the time derivative and train the network to match that derivative. This is cheap because it can be done offline.

The weakness is that matching the derivative at sampled points does not guarantee the rolled-out solution will stay close. Small derivative errors can accumulate, especially when the learned system is sensitive or chaotic.

estimate derivative from data
fit closure to local slope
rollout accumulates errors
good slope does not ensure good path

Trajectory Fitting Trains The Embedded Solver

The other option is trajectory fitting. Put the learned closure inside the solver, roll the solver forward, and train against the actual trajectory. This is more expensive because the solver must be differentiated through or paired with adjoint equations.

The advantage is direct. The training target is the same thing the user cares about: the path produced by the embedded learned solver. In the talk's experiments, discretize-then-optimize trajectory fitting performs best when a differentiable solver is available.

insert closure in solver
roll forward through time
compare full trajectory
train the embedded behavior

Chaotic Systems Make Training Windows Matter

The Kuramoto-Sivashinsky example in the talk is chaotic. In chaotic systems, two close states separate over time, so error metrics over long windows can behave differently from short windows.

This makes training-window choice important. Longer trajectory windows are not automatically better, because gradients, integrator errors, and sensitivity can make the optimization harder. The chapter's practical lesson is to test training-window length, not assume it.

nearby states separate
short window trains local rollout
long window may complicate gradients
choose by validation time

How This Connects To Design And Topology

Design and topology optimization often use coarse simulations because full resolution is too expensive across thousands of candidates. But a coarse design loop can choose the wrong shape if the unresolved scales feed back on drag, mixing, heat transfer, waves, or loads.

This chapter says a learned closure should not merely look accurate on a plot. It should be embedded in the actual solver, preserve the important conservation or dissipation structure, and be tested by rollout. Otherwise, the optimizer may exploit a closure artifact instead of improving the real design.

many design candidates
coarse solver needs closure
preserve scale energy exchange
avoid optimizing artifacts

The Chapter In One Chain

First, closure models exist because small unresolved scales affect large resolved scales. Second, naive learned corrections can improve snapshots while breaking solver structure. Third, putting the closure in the equation form gives the network the right role. Fourth, discretizing first makes the closure tailored to the actual solver and training data. Fifth, this tailoring improves consistency but requires retraining if the solver changes. Sixth, energy conservation or dissipation acts as a stability ledger. Seventh, filtered large-scale energy alone is incomplete, so a compressed subgrid variable can track hidden-scale energy. Eighth, an extended closure system evolves both resolved and compressed hidden variables. Ninth, derivative fitting is cheap but can drift in rollout. Tenth, trajectory fitting is more expensive but trains the embedded solver behavior that matters for real prediction and design.

The chapter's core idea: do not bolt a learned closure onto a solver as an arbitrary correction. Learn it inside the discrete solver, preserve the energy exchange it must obey, and judge it by the trajectory it produces.

How To Test Whether You Understood This Chapter

Say the problem without closure words

The cheap model leaves out small details, but those details still affect the big motion. The missing effect has to be added without breaking the solver.

Explain why energy matters

Energy is the ledger for whether the learned term is feeding or draining the system in an allowed way. Bad bookkeeping can make a rollout drift or blow up.

Connect it to topology and design

A topology optimizer using a coarse flow, wave, heat, or load solver can exploit a bad closure artifact. Stable design needs a closure that behaves correctly inside the solver it will actually use.

State the honest limit

A discrete embedded closure may be solver-specific. If the grid, filter, time step, or code changes, the closure must be rechecked or retrained.
