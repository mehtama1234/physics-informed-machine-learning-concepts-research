Narrative chapter

Mix AI With Physics Where The Goal Is Clear

Read This Chapter As One Claim

AI helps fluid mechanics when it is placed at a named bottleneck with a clear goal. It should optimize, compress, predict, control, or close a missing term only where physics tells us what state, action, reward, or reduced variable is meaningful.

Everyday problem Fluid simulations can be powerful but too expensive, while pure black-box learning can drift, miss important flow regions, or optimize the wrong reward.
Shared promise Build a computing alloy: let solvers, physics variables, autoencoders, recurrent models, optimization, and reinforcement learning each do the part they are good at.
Hidden danger Bad state choices, unphysical rewards, unweighted compression, weak sampling, or overclaimed PINNs can make the learned piece look useful while failing the fluid task.
First rejection test Reject the AI claim if it cannot name the bottleneck, the physical variables used, the outcome being improved, and the numerical or experimental baseline it beats.

This chapter uses video 51, Petros Koumoutsakos's DDPS talk on artificial intelligence and scientific computing for fluid mechanics. The talk is not a promise that AI replaces simulation. It is a map of places where AI can help scientific computing when the goal is explicit and the physics still guides the choices.

The first-principles question is direct. Fluid mechanics has equations, solvers, experiments, and huge simulations, but they can be expensive or incomplete. Where should learning enter so it improves the outcome instead of merely adding a black box?

fluid problem
expensive or incomplete solver
clear goal or missing term
AI plus physics acts there

Big Simulations Are Powerful But Expensive

The talk starts from the speaker's long history with large scientific computations. Examples include compressible-flow simulations with trillions of elements and blood-flow simulations with trillions of particles. These computations can reveal physics that smaller models cannot.

But the lesson is not "just simulate more." The lesson is that physics-based computation is valuable and costly. If every design, control, or prediction task requires a huge simulation, the workflow becomes too slow. Learning becomes attractive where it reduces the number, cost, or size of those computations without throwing away the physics.

trillion-element flow
trillion-particle blood
high physical detail
heavy compute cost

AI Is About Goals, Not Just Mechanisms

The speaker uses a definition of intelligence that emphasizes achieving goals in the world. That framing matters. In scientific computing, we often start with equations and write code to execute them. In AI-style work, we may ask the computer to find a decision, control, compression, or prediction that achieves a target.

For fluids, the target could be lower drag, better swimming efficiency, a cheaper prediction, a better reduced state, or a closure term that makes a coarse simulation match a trusted fine simulation. The goal must be concrete, otherwise the learning problem is vague.

mechanistic equations
desired outcome
learn action or model
test against the goal

Learning To Optimize: Search Without Gradients

Some fluid design problems are black boxes. You choose inputs, run a solver or experiment, and get a score. The gradients may be unavailable, noisy, or not useful. The talk discusses evolutionary and covariance-matrix adaptation ideas for this setting.

In plain words, keep a probability cloud over candidate designs. Sample designs from that cloud. Keep the better ones. Move and reshape the cloud toward better regions. The covariance matrix tells the search which directions in parameter space look promising.

sample candidate designs
score by objective
keep better samples
reshape search cloud

Learning To Compress: Nonlinear POD

Fluid data are often reduced with proper orthogonal decomposition, also called POD. POD is linear: it finds directions that capture energy in the data. The speaker connects old neural-network work to the idea of making this reduction nonlinear, using what we would now call an autoencoder.

The practical example is wall turbulence. The question was whether flow above a wall could be reconstructed from wall information. A pure data approach was not enough. The useful move was to help the network with physical terms known from the near-wall expansion, then let the neural net learn the remaining correction.

POD gives linear compression
autoencoder gives nonlinear compression
wall physics supplies first terms
network learns the rest

Learning To Predict: Black-Box Forecasts Drift

The talk then turns to recurrent neural networks and LSTMs for predicting the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation. The setup is intentionally clear: train on a block of data, then stop using the equation and let the learned model forecast forward.

The result is a warning. The forecast can look good for a while, then drift away. In chaotic systems, small errors grow. Reducing dissipation makes the forecast worse. The speaker pushes back against overstatements that machine learning has solved chaos prediction. It can help, but pure black-box prediction does not replace the equation.

train on short window
forecast without equation
chaos amplifies error
do not overclaim prediction

Computing Alloys: Combine Solvers And Learning

The speaker calls the useful mixture a computing alloy. Instead of choosing only scientific computing or only AI, combine them. Let equations, numerics, reduced states, and learning each do the part they are good at.

This is an important course-wide idea. A good scientific ML system is usually not one ingredient. It may use a solver for trustworthy local evolution, an autoencoder for compression, a recurrent model for reduced dynamics, and periodic decoding back to the full state.

equations and numerics
compression model
learned reduced dynamics
hybrid workflow

Learned Effective Dynamics

Learned effective dynamics combines the equation-free idea with autoencoders and recurrent networks. The encoder maps the full simulation state into a small latent space. A learned time model advances that small state. The decoder maps it back when needed.

On Kuramoto-Sivashinsky, the autoencoder found a useful eight-dimensional latent space, matching earlier knowledge about effective dynamics. On flow around a cylinder, the method could predict quantities such as drag much faster at lower Reynolds number, but struggled more at higher Reynolds number. That is a real boundary, not a footnote.

full simulation snapshots
encode to latent state
evolve latent dynamics
decode when needed

The Autoencoder Must Care Where Physics Happens

The cylinder example shows that not all points in the field are equally important. Vorticity and strong physics live near the cylinder surface. If compression treats every point equally, it may waste attention and miss important dynamics.

The lesson is simple: compression is a modeling decision. If a flow feature controls drag or future behavior, the compression should preserve it. A weighted autoencoder can do better when it gives more importance to physically active regions.

field has many points
physics concentrated near body
weight important regions
better reduced prediction

Molecules Expose The Training-Set Boundary

The learned-effective-dynamics idea was also applied to alanine dipeptide. The latent coordinate behaved like a free-energy-like projection, with minima matching regions where the molecular simulation spent time.

But the model missed regions that were not visited in training. This is the same lesson from many earlier chapters: a learned model cannot be trusted in places where it has no evidence. Sampling strategy matters as much as architecture.

molecular simulation data
latent free-energy-like space
unvisited region missing
sampling limits the claim

Learning To Control: Reinforcement Learning For Flow

Control is different from prediction. The agent acts, observes a reward, and changes policy to improve long-term reward. For fluids, this is attractive because the thing we care about, such as drag, may be an indirect result of the flow rather than a simple explicit term.

The talk gives fish schooling as a vivid example. If simulated swimmers are simply placed together with flow-structure interaction, they may collide. Reinforcement learning can train a swimmer to position itself in another swimmer's wake. One reward asked it to follow behind and save energy. Another reward optimized efficiency and found a motion that intercepted vortices to gain energy.

agent observes flow state
chooses swimming action
receives energy reward
learns wake use

Reinforcement Learning Needs Scientific Repair

The talk also warns that standard reinforcement-learning code did not simply work on these fluid problems. Experience replay can become unstable when old and current policies differ too much. The group developed a modified remember-and-forget replay idea to stabilize learning.

This is another example of the main theme. Importing an AI method is not enough. The method must be adapted to the scientific problem, the simulation cost, and the physical structure of the state and action spaces.

standard RL algorithm
fluid control instability
repair replay strategy
usable scientific controller

Learning To Close Turbulence

The final major example is turbulence modeling. A coarse grid cannot resolve every fluctuation. After averaging or filtering, the equation needs a closure term that represents the unresolved part. The speaker asks whether multi-agent reinforcement learning can learn that closure.

The key design choice is the state. Using the full velocity field would be too large and unhelpful. Instead, the agents use physically meaningful information such as energy, Fourier components, tensor invariants, and local quantities. The action can tune a turbulence coefficient. The reward can ask the coarse model to match trusted direct simulation statistics.

coarse grid misses fluctuations
agents observe physical state
agents tune closure action
reward matches DNS evidence

Bad States Make Learning Fail

In the question period, the speaker is blunt: physical understanding is still the best guide for choosing useful states and actions. If the reinforcement-learning state is just a raw velocity field, the algorithm may fail. In homogeneous turbulence, tensor invariants and energy information worked better because they match what matters physically.

This is one of the most useful lessons in the talk. Machine learning does not remove the need to know fluid mechanics. It makes the design of state, action, reward, compression, and sampling more important.

raw state too large
physical invariants matter
reward must match physics
learning becomes possible

The PINN Warning

The speaker also comments on physics-informed neural networks. His view is cautious. For some Kuramoto-Sivashinsky settings, neural PDE interpolation can work. For stiffer or longer-time settings, it may fail or cost far more than a finite-difference solver.

The lesson is not that PINNs are useless. The lesson is that one should compare against classical numerical methods honestly, across parameters, seeds, and time horizons. A few successful samples do not prove a robust scientific method.

works on some cases
fails on harder parameters
can cost much more
compare against numerics

How This Connects To Design And Topology

Fluid design and topology optimization often care about outcomes: drag, lift, mixing, cooling, pressure drop, blood-cell capture, swimmer efficiency, or flow stability. This chapter says AI should enter where the outcome is clear and where physics tells us how to represent the problem.

For topology, that may mean using black-box optimization when gradients are unavailable, using learned compression for repeated flow solves, using reinforcement learning for active flow control, or using learned closures when the design solver is too coarse to resolve every turbulent scale. The design claim is stronger when the learned component is tied to physics and tested against the quantity the design actually needs.

design outcome needed
solver too slow or coarse
physics-guided learned component
better design workflow

The Chapter In One Chain

First, large fluid simulations are powerful but expensive. Second, AI is useful when the goal is clear: optimize, compress, predict, control, or close a missing term. Third, covariance-matrix adaptation searches black-box design spaces by learning a probability cloud over better candidates. Fourth, autoencoders can extend POD into nonlinear compression, but wall-flow reconstruction improves when the network is helped with known physics. Fifth, LSTM-style black-box prediction can forecast chaotic dynamics only for a limited time before errors grow. Sixth, computing alloys combine equations, numerical solvers, autoencoders, and recurrent models instead of choosing only AI or only simulation. Seventh, learned effective dynamics can accelerate reduced evolution but must preserve physically important regions. Eighth, reinforcement learning can learn fluid control, as in swimmer schooling and vortex use, but standard algorithms may need scientific repair. Ninth, multi-agent reinforcement learning can learn turbulence closures only when states, actions, and rewards are physically meaningful. Tenth, fluid design and topology should use AI only where the learned piece is tied to an outcome, a physical representation, and a testable claim.

The chapter's core idea: AI is not a replacement for fluid mechanics. It is useful when it is placed at a specific bottleneck, guided by physical variables, and judged by the scientific or design outcome that actually matters.

How To Test Whether You Understood This Chapter

Say the problem without AI words

The solver is costly or incomplete, and the engineer needs a concrete outcome such as lower drag, better control, cheaper prediction, or a better closure.

Explain why the state matters

Learning succeeds only if the model sees the quantities that control the physics. Raw velocity fields, unweighted pixels, or vague rewards can hide the information the decision needs.

Connect it to topology and design

A fluid topology workflow can use AI for black-box search, repeated reduced solves, active control, or turbulence closure, but the learned part must be tied to drag, lift, cooling, mixing, pressure drop, or another real design target.

State the honest limit

Do not claim that AI solved the fluid problem unless it beats relevant numerical baselines, survives harder parameters and longer times, and improves the outcome the design actually uses.
