Find Hidden Frequencies From A Few Samples

Read This Chapter As One Claim

A small set of samples can sometimes reveal the hidden pieces that made them. The catch is that this works only when the signal has a simple enough structure, such as a few modes, poles, or spectral bands, and when the noise has not erased the evidence.

Everyday problem You see only a short list of measured values, but the real question is what hidden frequencies, poles, or spectrum produced those values.
Shared promise Use the structure of shifted samples, moments, and polynomials to recover hidden ingredients without blindly fitting a generic curve.
Hidden danger Noise, faraway poles, missing prior bounds, or unstable root finding can make a recovered feature look more certain than it really is.
First rejection test Reject the recovery if it does not explain what structure was assumed, how noise changes the answer, and which hidden features are actually visible from the samples.

This chapter uses video 45, Lexing Ying's DDPS talk on Prony's method, analytic continuation, and quantum signal processing. The talk is less about neural networks and more about a deep data-driven numerical idea: a small number of samples can contain hidden structure, if you use the right representation.

The first-principles question is simple. Suppose a measured signal is made by adding a few repeating waves together. You do not know how many waves there are. You do not know their frequencies. You do not know their weights. Can the samples tell you the hidden ingredients?

What Prony's Method Is Trying To Do

Prony's method starts with a signal that is a sum of a few exponentials or Fourier modes. Each mode has a frequency and a weight. The samples are just the visible mixture.

The method builds a matrix from shifted copies of the sample sequence. If the signal really has only a few modes, those shifted copies are not independent forever. A hidden linear relation appears. That relation gives a polynomial. The roots of that polynomial reveal the hidden frequencies.

samples in a row
shifted sample rows
rank reveals mode count
roots reveal modes

Why This Is Not Just A Fourier Grid

A standard Fourier transform often asks which frequencies live on a chosen grid. Prony's method is more adaptive. The frequencies do not have to sit exactly on a preset grid. They are recovered through the root-finding step.

That is the power and the danger. The method can find off-grid structure, but root finding can become unstable when the data is noisy. A tiny error in samples can move the recovered roots, especially for hard cases.

stability must be checked

Analytic Continuation From Zero

Analytic continuation asks for values of a function in places where we did not directly measure it. In the talk's setting, the function lives on the complex plane. We may only have noisy values along one line, but we want to recover hidden poles elsewhere.

A pole is a place where the function would blow up. In physical problems, the pole locations and weights can encode spectral information. The difficult part is that the data we have may be far from the poles and noisy.

values known on a line
function lives elsewhere
hidden poles carry structure
recover the spectrum

Why Prior Bounds Are Needed

The talk makes an important practical assumption: the poles live inside known regions. Without some bound like that, a faraway pole might barely affect the measured line, and no method could reliably know it is there.

This is a first-principles lesson about inverse problems. You cannot recover everything from limited data. You need either more measurements, less noise, or prior knowledge about where the answer is allowed to live.

limited measurement line
far pole may be invisible
bound possible region
problem becomes solvable

Conformal Maps Change The Geometry

The analytic-continuation method uses conformal mapping. In plain words, it smoothly warps the complex plane while preserving the local angle structure. The measured line can be turned into a unit circle, and the pole regions move into easier shapes.

Once the data is on a circle, Fourier coefficients become natural. Those coefficients act like moments of the poles. Then a Prony-style shifted-matrix argument can recover the pole locations.

measurement line
warp to unit circle
Fourier moments
Prony recovers poles

Noise Decides What Can Be Recovered

The numerical examples show a repeated pattern. With low noise, the pole locations and weights can be recovered very accurately. As noise grows, the farthest or weakest parts become unreliable first. In the hardest cases, only the central or strongly visible structure remains recoverable.

This is not a failure of one implementation. It is the nature of the problem. Analytic continuation can be severely ill-conditioned. If the data does not carry enough stable information about a feature, no clever method can make that feature certain.

low noise
many poles recovered
high noise
far features disappear

The Quantum Physics Use Case

One motivating application is Green's functions in quantum many-body calculations. Often one has data on an imaginary-frequency grid, while the desired spectral function lives on the real axis. Recovering that spectrum is an analytic-continuation problem.

The talk separates easier molecular-style cases from harder condensed-matter cases. In the harder case, the spectral function can spread over a wider real-axis region, and the measured grid may be short. The same overall strategy remains: map the geometry, extract moments, and recover the spectral structure as well as the noise allows.

Quantum Signal Processing From Zero

The last part of the talk moves to quantum computing. Quantum circuits naturally represent unitary operations. But scientific computing often wants to apply a function to a matrix: exponentials for time evolution, inverse functions for solving systems, filters for selecting eigenvectors, or step-like functions for occupied states.

Quantum signal processing asks how to build a quantum circuit that applies a desired function to the spectrum of a matrix, once the matrix has been placed inside a larger unitary object through block encoding.

matrix encoded in circuit
want function of matrix
approximate by polynomial
circuit applies spectral action

The Phase Angles Are The Design Variables

In quantum signal processing, the target function is approximated by a polynomial. The circuit then needs a sequence of phase angles that realizes that polynomial action.

Existing approaches either use factorization with root finding or direct optimization over the phase angles. Root finding can be unstable for high-degree polynomials. Optimization can be non-convex and may need special assumptions or good starting points.

target polynomial
phase angles needed
root finding can wobble
optimization can be hard

The Prony Trick Avoids The Roots

Ying's key move is to keep the factorization flavor but avoid explicitly computing roots. The observation is subtle but important: in one step, the algorithm needs the characteristic polynomial of hidden roots, not the roots themselves.

Prony's method can recover that polynomial from moments through a null-space calculation. If the polynomial is all that is needed, the algorithm can stop before the unstable root-finding step. That is the same structural idea reused in a different numerical problem.

roots are unstable
only polynomial needed
moments build matrix
null space gives polynomial

What The Quantum Examples Show

The talk tests the phase-construction method on Hamiltonian simulation, eigenstate filtering, matrix inversion, and Fermi-Dirac style step functions. These are standard scientific-computing tasks translated into quantum-circuit language.

The practical result is that the method can compute high-degree phase factors accurately without relying on fragile large-scale root finding. The examples reach small errors in cases with thousands of polynomial degrees.

How This Connects To Earlier Chapters

This chapter is a reminder that data-driven scientific computing is not only neural networks. Sometimes the right tool is an older signal-processing method that knows the structure of the data. Prony's method works because it assumes the signal is made from a small number of hidden pieces and exploits that structure directly.

For topology, materials, and physical design, the lesson is broader. If a measured response is made from a few hidden modes, poles, resonances, relaxation times, or spectral bands, the model should use that structure. A generic black box may fit the samples, but a structured method can reveal the hidden ingredients that matter for design.

measured response
hidden modes or poles

The Chapter In One Chain

First, Prony's method starts from samples of a signal made from a few hidden modes. Second, shifted copies of the samples reveal a rank structure. Third, the null space gives a polynomial whose roots identify the hidden frequencies. Fourth, the method is adaptive because the frequencies do not have to sit on a preset grid. Fifth, root finding makes the method sensitive to noise. Sixth, analytic continuation asks for hidden pole or spectrum information from noisy values measured on a limited set. Seventh, conformal mapping can turn a line of measurements into a circle where Fourier moments are natural. Eighth, Prony-style moment recovery can then find pole structure, but only to the extent the noise allows. Ninth, quantum signal processing asks for stable phase angles that make a quantum circuit apply a chosen function to a matrix spectrum. Tenth, the same Prony idea can recover a needed characteristic polynomial without explicitly finding unstable roots, making high-degree quantum signal-processing constructions more reliable.

The chapter's core idea: samples are not just points. When the hidden structure is a small set of modes, poles, or spectral pieces, the right algebra can recover the ingredients behind the samples. The price is that noise and missing prior information set hard limits on what can be trusted.

How To Test Whether You Understood This Chapter

Say the problem without math words

You hear a short mixed sound and want to know which simple tones made it. That is the same kind of question as finding hidden modes from samples.

Explain why structure matters

Prony-style methods work because they assume the data came from a small number of hidden pieces. Without that structure, a few samples are not enough to recover a detailed world.

Connect it to topology and design

A design response can hide resonances, relaxation times, spectral bands, or vibration modes. A structured method can reveal those ingredients so the design change targets the mechanism, not only the measured curve.

State the honest limit

Analytic continuation and root-based recovery can be fragile. If noise hides a far feature, the method should admit uncertainty instead of presenting a clean but unsupported answer.
