Particles Rewrite The Turbulence

Read This Chapter As One Claim

Dense particles are not just dots carried by a fluid. They push back, collide, cluster, change drag, alter heat exchange, and can either suppress or create turbulence. A useful engineering model has to carry those missing particle-scale statistics upward.

Everyday problem Reactors, plumes, aerosols, and suspensions need large-scale predictions, but every particle wake and collision cannot be resolved.
Shared promise Use particle-resolved data to build closures for drag fluctuations, clustering, exchange, and Reynolds-averaged design equations.
Hidden danger A mean drag law or single-phase turbulence model can miss force spread, particle variance, cluster-induced turbulence, and heat-transfer changes.
First rejection test Reject the closure if it matches only the mean flow while missing dispersion, clustering, variance, phase exchange, or invariance.

This chapter uses video 30, Jesse Capecelatro's DDPS talk on turbulent dispersed two-phase flows. The talk is about gas-solid and gas-particle flows where particles are not decorations inside a fluid. They push back on the fluid, form clusters, shed wakes, exchange heat, collide, and change the turbulence itself.

Examples include particle-laden jets, fluidized beds, catalytic reactors, biomass pyrolysis, plume-surface interaction, droplets and aerosols, and dense suspensions. The common problem is that the small particle-scale physics changes the large flow, but fully resolving every particle surface is too expensive for reactor-scale or engineering-scale prediction.

fluid moves particles
particles create wakes
particles collide and cluster
clusters reshape the flow

Dilute Is Not Dense

The talk separates two regimes. In the dilute regime, particles are sparse and often smaller than the smallest turbulent scales. They may preferentially collect in certain regions and settle faster, but the particle volume fraction is tiny.

The talk focuses on denser gas-solid flows with finite volume fraction, often around one to ten percent. That sounds small, but for heavy particles it can mean order-one mass loading. In plain words, the particles carry enough mass to strongly affect the gas. Now a particle's wake can hit another particle, clusters form, and the carrier turbulence no longer behaves like ordinary single-phase turbulence.

The Old Turbulence Picture Breaks

In ordinary single-phase turbulence, energy is often described as moving from large eddies down to smaller eddies until viscosity dissipates it. Particles disturb that picture. A single particle can shed a small wake. A group of particles can form a large cluster and create a large wake. Energy can appear at small scales and feed back upward, or clustering can suppress turbulence in some ranges.

That is why simply borrowing a single-phase turbulence closure can fail. The terms that matter in particle-laden flow do not exist in single-phase flow: drag exchange, volume fraction effects, particle-fluid slip, particle clustering, collisions, and particle-phase fluctuations.

single-phase cascade
particle wake adds small-scale motion
cluster wake adds large-scale motion
energy budget changes

Why Direct Simulation Is Too Expensive

If we wanted the exact force on a particle, we would resolve the fluid stress on the particle surface. That requires grid spacing smaller than the particle and boundary conditions on every particle surface. Direct numerical simulation can do this for limited cases, but it becomes impractical as particle count grows.

Even simulations with tens or hundreds of millions of tracked particles are not reactor-scale solutions. They are tools for generating data and understanding physics. The engineering model still needs averaging and closure.

resolve every particle surface
compute pressure and viscous stress
many particles become too costly
need averaged model with closures

Drag Is Not One Number

A common practical model uses a drag correlation. It predicts the mean drag from quantities such as average Reynolds number and volume fraction. That can work for the mean, but the talk emphasizes that the spread around the mean can be as large as the mean itself.

The reason is easy to see. Put the same particle in three boxes with the same average velocity and same volume fraction. If its neighbors are arranged differently, its wake environment is different. A standard mean drag law gives the same answer in all three boxes, even though the particle should feel different forces.

same average flow
same volume fraction
different neighbor layout
different drag fluctuation

The Stochastic Drag Idea

The first modeling idea is to keep the mean drag correlation but add a fluctuating drag force. The fluctuation is treated statistically with a Langevin equation, producing an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. In everyday words, the model gives each particle a drag that wanders around the mean with a realistic size and memory time.

The needed statistics come from particle-resolved simulations. The force fluctuations are close to Gaussian, and their autocorrelation decays roughly exponentially, which supports this stochastic model. The time scale is linked to time between collisions, and the standard deviation can be collapsed across volume fractions and Reynolds numbers after normalization.

mean drag law
random force fluctuation
memory time from collisions
particle velocity variance improves

The Heating And Cooling Tests

The model is tested in homogeneous cooling and heating cases. Imagine a periodic box of particles. In the heating case, particles start with little velocity fluctuation and the flow forces them until their velocity variance grows. In the cooling case, particles start with finite fluctuation and relax toward steady behavior.

The stochastic force model predicts both the transient behavior and the steady value much better than a model using only the mean drag. That matters because dispersion and particle velocity variance are not side details. They control mixing, collision rates, heat exchange, and reaction exposure.

cooling: fluctuations decay

Zoom Out To Large Flow

The second half of the talk zooms out. A real fluidized bed or chemical looping reactor has particle collisions, wakes, clustering, and heat transfer at small scales, but the reactor-level model cannot track every detail. The strategy is sequential averaging.

First, locally average the Navier-Stokes equations to get mesoscale equations that still retain volume fraction and interphase exchange. Then Reynolds-average those mesoscale equations to get macroscale equations. This avoids jumping straight from microscopic physics to a small set of overly overloaded closure terms.

particle-scale physics
local volume-filtered equations
Reynolds-averaged equations
closure terms with clearer meaning

Particles Can Kill Or Create Turbulence

The channel-flow example shows three different behaviors as mass loading changes while Reynolds number stays fixed. In a dilute case, the fluid looks like ordinary turbulent channel flow. At moderate mass loading, drag exchange can increase dissipation and re-laminarize the flow. At high mass loading, particles cluster, clusters create large wakes, and turbulence reappears with a different character.

This is a major lesson. Adding particles is not a small correction. It can suppress turbulence, create turbulence, or move energy across scales depending on mass loading, slip velocity, clustering, and particle feedback.

dilute: single-phase-like
moderate: drag damps turbulence
high: clusters create wakes
same Reynolds number, different physics

Cluster-Induced Turbulence

The simplest large-scale case is a periodic box where particles settle under gravity. The fluid is initially at rest. As particles settle, they form dense clusters. Flow moves quickly between clusters and is entrained downward around them. The fluid turbulent kinetic energy is created entirely by coupling with the particles.

For the particles to create turbulence in the fluid, two ingredients are needed: enough mass loading and nonzero mean slip velocity between particle and fluid phases. If there is no meaningful slip, the particles do not inject turbulent kinetic energy into the fluid in this way.

gravity settles particles

Data-Driven Closure Without A Black Box

The final modeling step uses sparse regression to build Reynolds-averaged closure models. The group wanted algebraic expressions, not opaque neural networks. They also wanted invariance: the model should not change just because the coordinate frame is rotated or shifted.

The method starts with tensor data from simulations and a user-built library of physically meaningful tensor bases. Sparse regression chooses a compact combination of those basis tensors. The sparsity penalty helps avoid blindly enslaving the model to every detail in the data. The result is a shorter equation that a turbulence modeler can inspect.

simulation tensor data

sparse regression
compact algebraic closure

What Gets Automated

The user still has to choose possible coefficient forms for the closure. The talk describes early work using gene expression programming to automate part of that symbolic search. Gene expression programming tries combinations of symbolic pieces such as powers, exponentials, and trigonometric functions.

The important balance is this: use symbolic search to reduce manual guessing, but keep sparse regression and tensor-basis structure so the model preserves invariance and remains readable. The goal is not maximum neural-network flexibility. The goal is a useful equation.

Heat Transfer And Radiation Limits

The talk briefly mentions heat transfer in clustered particle flows. Clustering changes the thermal entrance length, meaning the distance needed for fluid and particles to approach thermal equilibrium. If particles were evenly spread, the heat exchange distance would be different than in a clustered flow.

In the Q&A, radiation is called out as missing from the chemical looping reactor example. The answer is direct: radiation is important at high temperature, but dense suspensions make radiation very hard because rays interact with many particle surfaces. This is an open modeling burden, not something solved in the talk.

Why This Matters For Design

Many particle-laden flows are design problems. A reactor designer cares about conversion, mixing, heat transfer, erosion, and pressure drop. A lander designer cares about plume interaction with a dusty surface. A public-health modeler may care about droplet dispersion. In all of these, the design depends on statistics that come from unresolved particle-fluid interaction.

A bad closure can make a design look mixed when it will actually channel, look stable when it will cluster, or look cool enough when hot clustered regions persist. The modeling standard is therefore not just matching mean flow. It must capture fluctuation, dispersion, clustering, and exchange between phases.

The Chapter In One Chain

First, dispersed two-phase flows are hard because particles and fluid move each other. Second, dilute particle flow is not enough to understand dense suspensions. Third, particle wakes, collisions, and clusters break the ordinary single-phase turbulence picture. Fourth, fully resolving every particle surface is too costly for engineering scale. Fifth, mean drag laws miss large force fluctuations caused by neighbor layout. Sixth, stochastic drag adds realistic force variation and improves particle variance and dispersion predictions. Seventh, sequential averaging keeps more physics before building Reynolds-averaged closures. Eighth, particles can damp turbulence or create cluster-induced turbulence depending on mass loading and slip. Ninth, sparse regression with physics-built tensor bases gives readable closure equations. Tenth, design use needs these closures because reactor, plume, heat-transfer, and aerosol predictions depend on unresolved two-phase statistics.

The chapter's core idea: once particles are dense enough, they are part of the turbulence engine. A useful model must carry the missing particle-scale drag, clustering, and exchange statistics into the larger design-scale equations.
