Narrative chapter

Keep The Mechanics Inside The Learned Small Model

This chapter uses video 100, Boris Kramer's DDPS talk on structure-preserving learning of high-dimensional Lagrangian and Hamiltonian systems. The talk connects reduced-order modeling, operator inference, Hamiltonian conservation, Lagrangian mechanics, symplectic projection, soft robotic fish, nonlinear beams, and experimental structural data.

The first-principles question is this: if a large mechanical or wave simulation is too expensive, can we learn a small model without destroying the physical rules that make the motion believable?

The lecture's answer is yes, but only if the small model is forced to keep the right structure. A learned model that fits the training movie can still drift, gain fake energy, or blow up later. A structure-preserving reduced model learns fewer things because it already knows the shape of mechanics: energy, symmetry, damping, second-order motion, and the difference between position and momentum.

Read This Chapter As One Claim

Start with a simple mechanical test. If you push a beam, a wave, or a soft robot, it should move in a way that respects energy, damping, mass, stiffness, position, and velocity. A small learned model can copy the training motion for a short time and still be physically wrong. The mistake may not show up immediately. It may slowly create energy in a damped system, lose the special geometry of a Hamiltonian system, or turn a second-order mechanical system into an arbitrary sequence predictor. This chapter says the learned part must live inside the mechanics. Keep the Hamiltonian or Lagrangian structure when it is known. If friction, contact, or damping is unknown, learn those missing pieces inside the energy and dissipation terms, not as a free black box outside the model.

Data Alone Is Not The Whole Answer

The talk starts with a practical point. We can collect and store more data than before, but physical systems are not arbitrary data tables. They often obey conservation laws, reversibility, symmetries, damping, or geometric constraints.

If a learning method ignores those rules, it has to rediscover them from data. That is expensive and fragile. Worse, it may not rediscover them exactly, and a tiny structural error can become a large long-time prediction error.

Hamiltonian Systems Keep Energy

A Hamiltonian is an energy-like scalar function that governs many conservative systems. In canonical Hamiltonian form, the state separates into position-like and momentum-like pieces, and a special skew-symmetric structure moves the system while conserving the Hamiltonian.

The plain lesson is: the equations are not just any equations. Their algebra is what stops the model from inventing or destroying energy in a conservative problem.

position variables
momentum variables
skew structure
energy conserved

Dissipative Systems Lose Energy In The Right Direction

Not every system conserves energy. Some physical systems dissipate energy. In those cases, the structure says the energy should not increase. It should stay the same or decrease according to the physics.

This is just as important as conservation. A learned model for a damped beam should not slowly create energy until the prediction explodes. The sign and symmetry of the learned operators matter.

damped physical system
energy should decrease
wrong learned sign
prediction can blow up

Reduction Must Respect The Geometry

Before learning the small dynamics, the high-dimensional state is projected into a smaller space. For Hamiltonian systems, the projection cannot be chosen casually. The talk uses proper symplectic decomposition so the reduced coordinates still support Hamiltonian flow.

This is a recurring theme across the course. Compression is not neutral. If the projection destroys the geometry of the original equations, the learned reduced model starts with a broken foundation.

high-dimensional state
symplectic projection
reduced coordinates
Hamiltonian flow remains possible

Operator Inference Learns The Small Operators

Operator inference learns reduced operators from projected trajectory data. Instead of opening the full simulation code and projecting its matrices intrusively, it uses snapshots, time derivatives, inputs, and known terms to fit the small operators.

The important addition in this talk is constraints. The learned matrices are not allowed to be arbitrary. Symmetry, positive definiteness, and Hamiltonian or Lagrangian form are built into the optimization problem.

projected coordinates

structured reduced model

The Cubic Schrodinger Test Shows Conservative Prediction

The Hamiltonian example uses the cubic Schrodinger equation. It has a quadratic part and a nonlinear quartic contribution in the Hamiltonian. The method assumes the nonlinear term is known at the PDE level, evaluates it on data, and learns the reduced linear operators.

The reduced model matches the intrusive symplectic reduced model reasonably well and keeps stable behavior beyond the training interval. It also preserves important invariants such as mass and momentum well, even though those were not directly forced as separate constraints.

Lagrangian Systems Keep Second-Order Mechanics

The second half of the talk focuses on Lagrangian systems. These are natural for mechanical systems with positions, velocities, kinetic energy, potential energy, damping, and external inputs.

The key is that many mechanical systems are second order in time. If we flatten them into an arbitrary first-order black box, we may lose the structure that says how position, velocity, force, mass, stiffness, and damping are supposed to relate.

positions
velocities
kinetic and potential energy
second-order mechanics

A Beam Can Look Fine In Training And Fail Later

The beam example gives the warning. A generic learned second-order model can fit a short training interval well. The training error drops as the reduced dimension grows.

But when run longer, the energy starts growing artificially and the solution blows up. This is the core reason structure preservation matters. The failure is not visible if we only stare at training error.

Sine-Gordon Tests The Nonlinear Wave Case

The Sine-Gordon equation is used as a nonlinear wave test. The Lagrangian operator inference model again knows the nonlinear potential term at the PDE level and learns the reduced linear pieces under Lagrangian constraints.

The result is not perfect, but it stays stable far beyond the training interval and captures the wave structures well. This is the practical value: the small model remains usable in a predictive regime, not only inside the training window.

The Soft Robotic Fish Needs Fast State Estimation

The soft robotic fish application is about control. The robot is a deformable continuum system with fluid-elastomer actuators. The full finite-element model has hundreds of thousands of dimensions, and the controller only measures center-line trajectories.

A real-time controller needs a state estimator. From measured outputs, it must estimate the internal state quickly enough to choose the next actuation. A reduced model is necessary because the full soft-body simulation is far too large for fast control.

soft continuum robot
six actuator inputs
center-line outputs
fast state estimator

Structure Helps In Open And Closed Loop

The robotic fish tests compare dynamic mode decomposition with control, eigensystem realization, and structure-preserving operator inference. The Lagrangian operator inference model tracks the target trajectories best in both open-loop and closed-loop tests.

The reason is not that it is the fanciest black box. It uses the fact that the robot is a mechanical system with second-order structure. That structure gives a better state estimator for the controller.

Known Nonlinear Terms Are Not Always Available

The earlier Hamiltonian and Lagrangian examples often assume that the nonlinear term is known at the PDE level. That is reasonable for simulations where we know the equation. It is less satisfying for experimental data, where the nonlinear contact, friction, or damping may not be known.

This motivates the final extension: keep the mechanical structure, but learn the unknown nonlinear energy and damping pieces with machine-learning function approximators.

experimental data
unknown contact or damping

Neural Terms Are Put Inside The Energy Structure

The extended method augments the Lagrangian model with learned nonlinear potential, damping, and kinetic-energy corrections. Neural networks and polynomial terms parameterize those functions.

The important detail is placement. The neural networks are not allowed to become an unconstrained dynamics model. They sit inside the energy and dissipation structure, with constraints that prevent unphysical negative energy behavior.

The Bolted Beam Example Needs Learned Nonlinear Friction

The last application is an experimental half Brake-Reuss beam. Two high-speed cameras measure vibration with digital image correlation. The beam has a joint with bolts, contact, and friction, so the governing equation of the joint is hard to write exactly.

The structure-preserving learned Lagrangian model predicts the measured frequency-position-amplitude behavior and damping behavior well. This is important because it shows the method is not only a clean simulation test. It can help on real experimental mechanics where the nonlinear joint physics is messy.

The Q And A Clarifies When This Helps

In Q and A, the speaker says high-dimensional molecular dynamics could be a candidate only if the system has the relevant Hamiltonian, gradient, or Lagrangian structure and if the data shows meaningful low-dimensional behavior. Structure helps, but it does not remove the need for reducibility.

The discussion also clarifies the intrusive versus non-intrusive tradeoff. If we own the full solver and can implement a structure-preserving intrusive ROM, that can be excellent. If the code is closed, difficult, changing, or experimental data is all we have, non-intrusive structure-preserving learning becomes valuable.

How This Connects To Design And Topology

Design and topology optimization often run a model far beyond the examples used to train it. A soft robot controller asks new actuation sequences. A structural optimizer changes geometry or joints. A wave-control problem asks the model to predict after the training window.

If the reduced model creates fake energy, loses damping, or breaks second-order mechanics, the optimizer can exploit that defect. It may choose a design that looks good only because the learned model violated physics.

The right lesson is to preserve the structure that the design loop depends on. For conservative systems, keep Hamiltonian structure. For mechanical systems, keep Lagrangian form, mass, stiffness, damping, and positive energy. For experimental nonlinearities, learn the missing terms inside that structure rather than outside it.

The Chapter In One Chain

First, high-dimensional physical simulations need reduced models for prediction, control, design, and uncertainty work. Second, physical data should be combined with known structure rather than treated as arbitrary data. Third, Hamiltonian systems conserve energy through special skew structure. Fourth, dissipative systems should lose energy in the right direction. Fifth, reduction itself must respect geometry, for example through symplectic projection. Sixth, operator inference learns reduced operators from data, but the learning problem must enforce the right constraints. Seventh, the cubic Schrodinger example shows Hamiltonian operator inference preserving stable long-time behavior and invariants. Eighth, Lagrangian systems preserve second-order mechanics through kinetic energy, potential energy, damping, and forcing. Ninth, a generic learned beam model can fit training data yet blow up later from fake energy growth. Tenth, the Sine-Gordon example shows stable nonlinear wave prediction when Lagrangian structure is preserved. Eleventh, the soft robotic fish needs fast reduced state estimation for real-time control. Twelfth, structure-preserving operator inference outperforms DMDc and ERA baselines in the robotic tests. Thirteenth, experimental systems may not reveal their nonlinear contact or damping terms. Fourteenth, neural and polynomial terms can learn those nonlinearities while staying inside constrained energy and dissipation structure. Fifteenth, the bolted beam experiment shows this matters for real vibration data with contact and friction. Sixteenth, design and topology loops need these constraints because an optimizer will exploit fake energy, broken damping, or wrong mechanics if the reduced model allows it.

The chapter's core idea: a reduced mechanical model must be small, fast, and physically structured. Training accuracy is not enough; the model has to conserve, dissipate, and respond like the real class of mechanics when it is pushed into new prediction and control cases.
