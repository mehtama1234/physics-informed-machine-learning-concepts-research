Narrative chapter

Make The Plan Prove The Schedule Can Execute It

This chapter uses video 106, Pinar Caglayan Kucukbakar's DDPS talk on a data-driven process systems engineering approach for supply chain management and enterprise-wide optimization. The talk is not about learning a PDE solution. It is about making decisions in a factory, supply chain, or enterprise system when the real constraints are nested across time scales.

The first-principles problem is this: a high-level plan can look good on paper and still be impossible on the plant floor. Planning may say "make this much product each day." Scheduling then has to assign tasks to equipment, respect capacity, handle storage, obey timing, and actually produce the target. If planning ignores scheduling, the plan can overpromise. This lecture builds a method where the plan is only accepted if the schedule underneath it can really execute it.

Read This Chapter As One Claim

Start with the factory-floor version of truth. A plan is easy to write: make this much product, meet this demand, minimize this cost. Execution is harder. The schedule must decide which unit runs which task, when material moves, where inventory sits, whether storage is full, and whether every demand can be met on time. This chapter says the upper plan should not be allowed to declare success by itself. The lower schedule must prove the plan can happen. Domino does that by sampling possible plans, solving the detailed scheduling problem underneath each one, and using those results to guide a derivative-free search. The method is expensive because every sample includes a real execution check, but that cost is exactly what prevents fake plans that look cheap only because they ignored plant reality.

Process Systems Engineering Starts With Choices

A chemical process turns raw material into final products through processing units. There may be many possible routes, many reactor sizes, many operating temperatures, many pressures, and many safety limits. Every one of those choices changes profit, yield, cost, emissions, and risk.

Because it is unsafe and expensive to physically try every alternative, engineers write mathematical models. These models include balances for mass, energy, and momentum, plus process limits and decision variables. Once a goal is attached, such as minimize cost or maximize yield, the model becomes an optimization problem.

Classic Optimization Wants Derivatives

The talk gives a short optimization lesson. Many deterministic optimization methods need derivative information. In everyday words, they need to know which direction improves the objective and which constraints become tight.

That becomes difficult when the system is represented by a complex simulator, a large set of differential equations, a multi-scale model, or data instead of closed-form equations. If the derivative is unavailable, unreliable, or too expensive, the optimization method needs another way to search.

Supply Chains Have Several Clocks

Supply chain management has decisions at different time scales. Long-term structure decides where facilities are and how the network is arranged. Medium-term planning decides production targets and inventory. Short-term scheduling decides which tasks happen on which units at which times.

A sequential approach is tempting: choose the network, choose production goals, then create the schedule. The problem is that these layers share variables. A production target chosen at the planning layer can be impossible at the scheduling layer.

The Planning Mistake Is Overpromising

The lecture's simple planning example has product demand over a week. The planning layer may choose daily production amounts to meet demand. But if it makes that choice without checking the scheduling layer, it can ask the plant to produce more than its equipment can actually handle in a day.

The result is unmet demand. The plan was mathematically attractive at its own level, but it was not executable. This is the central everyday lesson: a plan is not real until the schedule can carry it.

Bilevel Optimization Keeps The Layers Honest

Bilevel optimization represents this hierarchy directly. The upper level chooses planning decisions and tries to minimize planning cost. The lower level solves the scheduling problem underneath those planning decisions. The two levels share production targets and other linking variables.

The key rule is strict: the upper plan is feasible only if the lower scheduling problem is solved to global optimality. That is how the formulation prevents a fake plan. It embeds the schedule as a condition the plan must pass.

The Price Is Hardness

The lecture is clear that bilevel problems are hard. Even a linear program constrained by another linear program can become strongly NP-hard. When the lower level has discrete decisions, such as task assignments and unit choices, common simplifications no longer apply cleanly.

Scheduling also creates multi-follower structure. If the plan covers seven days, the method may need to solve a scheduling problem for each day and link those results through inventory. That is not one leader and one follower. It is one planning layer with many scheduling problems underneath.

Domino Samples The Upper Decisions

The proposed Domino framework uses a data-driven strategy. First, sample the upper-level decisions, such as production targets. For each sampled planning choice, fix that choice in the lower scheduling problem. Then solve the lower problem deterministically to global optimality using an appropriate solver.

After the lower solve, Domino can evaluate the planning cost and constraint violations. Those input-output pairs become data for a derivative-free optimizer. The optimizer chooses more samples, checks convergence, and finally checks feasibility and lower-level global optimality.

fix in schedule
solve schedule globally
learn feasible cost surface

Nomad And Argonaut Search Differently

The talk uses two styles of derivative-free optimization. Nomad is more sample-based. It explores by evaluating points and moving through the space without requiring gradients. Argonaut is more model-based. It builds surrogate models for objectives and constraints, then optimizes those surrogates to propose new samples.

The tradeoff is familiar. A model-based method can be more sample efficient, but its surrogate can mislead it on harder problems. A sample-based method may need many more evaluations, but it can be more robust in some difficult mixed-integer nonlinear cases.

The first integrated planning and scheduling example uses a state-task network with feeds, processing units, tasks, and two products over seven planning days. The lower scheduling layer handles allocation, storage, task duration, sequence, time horizon, and material balances. The important linking constraint says that by the final event point, scheduled production must meet or exceed the production target chosen by planning.

Cyclic Planning Prevents A Fake Week Boundary

The lecture includes cyclic planning. A week does not truly end the process. Inventory left at the end of this week may become the starting inventory for next week. If the model ignores that loop, it can produce plans that look good only because the time window was cut artificially.

Cyclic planning forces the week to connect back to the next week. That makes the inventory decision more realistic. A plan that empties the plant at the end may not be acceptable if next week's first demand needs starting inventory.

Uncertain Demand Requires Scenarios

The deterministic examples use one known demand curve. The stochastic part asks what happens when demand is uncertain. The talk creates many possible demand samples, then groups demand into low, medium, and high regions over parts of the week.

Because infinite scenarios cannot be tested, the study samples nine representative scenarios. The same production targets must work across those scenarios. If the worst case is high demand through much of the week, the chosen plan may carry extra inventory in lower-demand scenarios. That is the cost of protecting feasibility under uncertainty.

Sampling Time Becomes The Bottleneck

Domino's cost depends heavily on how expensive it is to evaluate one sample. One sample means choosing an upper-level plan and solving the lower-level schedule. As the number of scheduling event points grows, the lower problem has more variables and constraints.

The lecture reports that sample time can rise from tens of seconds to over one hundred seconds per sample in harder cases. That matters because sample-based optimizers such as Nomad may need many samples. Parallel execution helps, but the lower-level solve remains a central bottleneck.

Crude Oil Scheduling Adds Real Nonlinearity

The crude oil example makes the scheduling problem harder. Vessels bring crude oil to storage tanks. Storage tanks feed charging tanks. Blending occurs because crude compositions differ. Charging tanks feed the distillation unit.

The nonlinear part is not artificial decoration. Component balances from blending create nonlinear relationships. That turns the lower scheduling problem into a mixed-integer nonlinear problem. Domino still uses the same idea: sample the plan, solve the lower problem globally, and keep only plans whose schedules can meet demand.

The Q And A Gives The Practical Boundary

In Q&A, the speaker says the number of samples depends on problem scale. Smaller cases may use hundreds or a few thousand samples. More complex cases may need many thousands if the computation can afford it. That is why speeding up sample collection is a continuing research direction.

The discussion also connects this to expensive physical simulation. If one sample requires a week-long high-fidelity simulation, the optimizer must become more sample efficient or use a trusted surrogate. The speaker suggests model-based derivative-free optimization, Kriging or radial basis surrogates, and physics-informed or hybrid machine-learning surrogates as possible ways to reduce the pain.

How This Connects To Design And Topology

Design and topology optimization often has the same nested structure. A top-level design chooses geometry or material layout. A lower-level physics solver checks stress, flow, heat, reaction, manufacturing limits, or scheduling feasibility. If the top-level optimizer ignores the lower-level reality, it can choose a design that is mathematically attractive but physically impossible.

This lecture adds an important non-PDE lesson to the course. Sometimes the central object is not a field, manifold, or neural operator. Sometimes the central object is a decision hierarchy. The first-principles question becomes: who is allowed to say a design is feasible? The answer should be: the lower-level solver that represents the real execution constraints.

For topology, this matters when design choices must pass manufacturing schedules, supply availability, inventory constraints, uncertainty scenarios, or downstream operations. A part shape may be optimal in mechanics but impossible to produce at the required rate. A thermal layout may be excellent but require a material supply chain that cannot meet demand. Domino's lesson is to couple the top design choice to the lower execution proof.

Eighth, the resulting objective and constraint values become data for derivative-free optimization. Ninth, Nomad searches by samples while Argonaut builds surrogate models. Tenth, integrated planning and scheduling examples show demand satisfaction when schedules are globally optimal. Eleventh, cyclic planning avoids pretending the week ends cleanly. The chapter's core idea: do not trust a beautiful high-level plan until the lower-level execution problem has proved it can actually happen.
