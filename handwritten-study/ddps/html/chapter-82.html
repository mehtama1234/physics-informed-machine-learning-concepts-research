Design The Microstructure By Following The Hot Spots

Read This Chapter As One Claim

Start with a material that looks like one solid block from the outside. Inside, it has pores, cracks, grains, binder regions, and damage sites. Under shock, one tiny pore can collapse, concentrate heat, start chemistry, and help push the whole sample toward detonation. This chapter is about connecting that hidden internal topology to the large-scale safety decision, so design does not ignore the small sites where the event begins.

Everyday problem The same energetic material can be safer or more sensitive depending on pore size, orientation, porosity, cracks, and grain arrangement.
Shared promise Use mesoscale simulations and learned surrogates to connect pore collapse, hot spot ignition, growth rates, and macro sensitivity curves.
Hidden danger A synthetic microstructure can match statistics while hiding a few critical defects that dominate shock response.
First rejection test Ask which pores drive hot spot temperature and whether the macro sensitivity changes when their size, orientation, or location changes.

This chapter uses video 85, Udaykumar's DDPS talk on data-driven multiscale simulations for materials-by-design of energetic materials. The talk is about how a material's tiny pores and cracks can decide whether a shock stays safe or becomes a detonation.

The first-principles question is this: if the dangerous event starts at tiny defects but the final outcome is a large-scale explosion, how do we connect the small scale to the big decision?

The lecture's answer is to build a multiscale bridge. Simulate pore collapse and hotspot growth at the mesoscale. Turn that information into burn-rate closure for a macroscale hydrocode. Then use generated microstructures and learned dynamics to ask how changing pores, grain sizes, and orientations changes sensitivity.

Sensitivity Depends On Microstructure

The talk begins with experimental sensitivity curves for pressed HMX. In a James-curve-style picture, one side of the curve means the material detonates under a given shock pressure and pulse width; the other side means it does not.

Two samples made of the same energetic material can have different criticality envelopes because their microstructures differ. Porosity, pore size, cracks, and void orientation change how easily hot spots form. So the material name is not enough. The arrangement of defects matters.

same chemical material
different porosity
different shock threshold
microstructure matters

A Hot Spot Begins With A Collapsing Pore

Under strong shock loading, pores can collapse quickly. The collapse can jet material, focus energy, and create a local high-temperature region. That local region is a hot spot.

If the hot spot is strong enough, chemical reaction starts there. If the reaction grows and couples back to the shock, the system can transition from shock loading to detonation. The whole large-scale danger can begin with a small defect doing the wrong thing.

shock hits pore
pore collapses
hot spot forms
reaction can grow

Traditional Burn Models Need Closure

Large hydrocode simulations cannot resolve every pore in a full material sample. They use burn models, such as ignition-and-growth models, to represent how unresolved hot spots create reaction at the macroscale.

Those burn models have rates and parameters. Traditionally, many of those parameters are fit to experiments. The data-driven route in this talk asks whether mesoscale simulations can supply those rates more directly.

macro hydrocode
cannot resolve pores
needs burn-rate closure
use mesoscale evidence

The Structure-Property-Performance Chain

The lecture frames the work as a structure-property-performance linkage. Structure means the microstructure: pores, cracks, crystals, binder, grain size, orientation, and porosity. Property means mesoscale response: pore collapse, hotspot temperature, hotspot area, and reaction rates. Performance means macroscale sensitivity: whether the material detonates under a loading condition.

This chain is the design logic. If we want materials by design, we need to know which structural changes move the performance in the desired direction.

structure
mesoscale properties
macro performance
design decision

Scimitar Supplies The Direct Simulations

The simulations use Scimitar 3D, an in-house code for immersed moving interfaces, transport, adaptive refinement, and compressible multiphase or multimaterial dynamics. It can go from image-based microstructures to simulations by representing material interfaces with level sets on a fixed Cartesian grid.

For this chapter, the important point is not the code name. The important point is that the data-driven models are trained on direct numerical simulations of pore collapse and hotspot evolution, not only on final experimental labels.

MESSAGE Uses Single-Pore Collapse Surrogates

MESSAGE is the first bridge described in the talk. It runs many single-pore collapse simulations while changing shock pressure, pulse width, pore size, shape, and porosity. From each simulation, it extracts ignition and growth rates for a hot spot.

Those rates are placed into a Gaussian-process-style surrogate surface. Later, when the macro code needs a burn rate at a cell, it can ask the surrogate: given this pressure and this microstructure statistic, what ignition or growth rate should be used?

single-pore DNS
vary pressure and pore size
learn rate surface
feed macro burn model

Ignition And Growth Are Measured From Reaction History

In the single-pore simulations, the model tracks how much material has reacted over time. The early steep part gives an ignition rate. The later steadier part gives a growth rate.

At the macro scale, those pore-level rates are multiplied by porosity or otherwise aggregated so the hydrocode gets the reaction rate it needs. In plain terms, the small simulation tells the big simulation how fast hidden hot spots are likely to start and grow.

track reacted mass
early ignition slope
later growth slope
macro reaction rate

MESSAGE Can Predict Sensitivity Curves

The talk shows MESSAGE predicting a James curve for class 5 HMX and comparing it with experiments. The match is good enough to show that mesoscale pore-collapse data can be telescoped into a macroscale sensitivity prediction.

This is the first major success of the chain: from microstructure statistics, to pore-collapse simulations, to surrogate burn rates, to a macro criticality curve.

MatterIQ Generates Microstructures For Design

The design part needs many possible microstructures, but experiments may provide only a few images. MatterIQ uses generative models to synthesize microstructures that statistically resemble real ones.

A GAN-style generator can create many samples with similar pore-size, aspect-ratio, and orientation distributions. Then latent variables can be adjusted to change grain size, void orientation, size mixtures, and spatial patterns. This turns microstructure from a fixed input into a design variable.

one real image
GAN microstructure generator
adjust latent variables
design new structures

Latent Knobs Can Paint A Microstructure

The lecture shows microstructures with small grains in one region and large grains in another, and even a patterned demonstration where large and small crystals are placed in specified regions. The point is not the novelty of the picture. The point is control.

If latent variables can steer crystal size, void size, or orientation, then a designer can ask targeted questions: what if defects are aligned this way, what if large grains are concentrated there, or what if damage is added in a controlled pattern?

PARK Learns Hotspot Dynamics From Full DNS

PARK is the second major model in the talk. It stands for a physics-aware recurrent convolution approach. Instead of using only single-pore collapse, PARK learns from full DNS simulations of microstructures.

The state it learns includes hotspot temperature, pressure, and the evolving microstructure. That matters because the voids collapse and turn into hot regions over time. PARK is trained to predict this time evolution, so it acts as a fast surrogate for the expensive DNS.

full microstructure DNS
temperature field
pressure and evolving voids
fast hotspot prediction

PARK Is Physics-Aware By Choosing The Right State

In the Q&A, there is a useful challenge: where exactly is the physics in PARK? It is not a PINN that directly enforces the full governing equations in the loss.

The answer is that PARK uses expert physical judgment to choose the state that must be learned: temperature, pressure, and microstructure evolution. It learns the coupling among shock, void collapse, and hotspot growth because those are the mesoscale ingredients the macro model needs. This is weaker than enforcing every equation, but stronger than learning arbitrary pictures.

not full equation enforcement
choose physical state variables
learn their coupled evolution
produce macro closure

PARK Must Generalize Beyond The Training Window

The lecture tests whether PARK can predict after the time interval used in training. This is important because a fast surrogate is useless if it only repeats the part of the DNS it has already seen.

PARK is also tested on outlier microstructures and unseen loading pressures. These tests ask whether it has learned hotspot physics rather than memorized examples. The reported results show useful generalization for the cases tested.

train on DNS windows
predict later time
try outlier structures
try unseen pressure

Saliency Maps Identify The Critical Pores

PARK is also used for interpretation. A saliency map asks how sensitive hotspot temperature is to the microstructure. In simple terms, it highlights which pores mattered most for creating hot spots.

The result is physically meaningful. Most pores do not become critical. The pores that do become critical tend to have particular morphology and orientation. The talk notes that orientation relative to the shock direction has a strong effect, and smaller pores can be more sensitive through surface-area effects.

The Low-Pressure Region Is Harder Physics

The Q&A adds an important boundary. At lower pressures, pore collapse may stop being mostly hydrodynamic jetting. Strength, shear bands, sideways pinching, and chemical kinetics can become more important.

That means errors in the sensitivity curve near the threshold may not be only machine-learning errors. They may reflect missing physics in the underlying simulations or closure. The right response is not simply to tune the network. It is to improve the physics where the mechanism changes.

Why This Matters For Design And Topology

This talk is directly about topology in the material sense. The topology of pores, cracks, grains, binder regions, and damage sites changes how shock energy localizes. The design object is not only a shape on the outside. It is the internal arrangement of defects and phases.

The lesson for broader design is that topology decisions can be dangerous if they ignore the initiating mechanism. Here, the initiating mechanism is hotspot formation at pores. For other materials, it may be crack growth, phase transformation, local buckling, thermal runaway, or flow through a connected pore network.

A useful materials-by-design workflow must therefore connect generated structures, high-fidelity local physics, fast surrogates, interpretable saliency, and macro performance. Otherwise we may design a microstructure that looks statistically right but fails because a small set of hidden sites controls the outcome.

The chapter's core idea: materials-by-design needs a bridge from internal topology to macro consequence. In energetic materials, that bridge runs through pore collapse, hotspot ignition, growth rates, and sensitivity curves. The learned model is useful only if it preserves that physical chain and helps identify which tiny sites control the big outcome.
