Narrative chapter

Keep Newton's Law, Learn The Flexible Energy

Read This Chapter As One Claim

Start with a rope, ribbon, Slinky, soft robot, or cut sheet. It can bend, twist, stretch, touch itself, wrinkle, snap, and settle into more than one shape. The physics already tells us something precious: forces come from energy, and motion follows Newton's law. This chapter says not to throw that away. Learn the missing energy rule or the compressed design code, but keep the mechanics that make the simulation and inverse design inspectable.

Everyday problem Flexible structures have many possible shapes, contacts, and instabilities, so full simulation and direct design search can be too expensive.
Shared promise Keep known mechanics, learn the missing constitutive energy or kirigami latent code, and use the smaller model for faster simulation or inverse design.
Hidden danger A direct black-box rollout can look accurate for one step but drift badly once its outputs are differentiated into forces and rolled forward.
First rejection test Shift, rotate, reflect, change length, alter boundary conditions, or roll out longer; reject the model if it violates symmetry, energy logic, or changed-case behavior.

This chapter uses video 87, Khalid Jawed's DDPS talk on reduced-order modeling and inverse design of flexible structures by machine learning. The talk is about flexible objects: rods, ribbons, plates, shells, soft robots, Slinkies, bacterial flagella, kirigami sheets, wearable patches, and grippers.

The first-principles question is simple: if a flexible object is expensive to simulate in full detail, and if we also want to design it backward from a desired shape, what should the machine learn and what should physics still control?

The lecture's answer is not to replace mechanics with a black box. Keep the motion law. Keep the idea that forces come from energy. Use machine learning only where the simple model does not know the right energy or where the design space is too awkward to search directly.

flexible object
known motion law
learn missing energy
simulate or design faster

The Real Problem Is Shape Change

A rigid object is easy to describe compared with a flexible one. A table moves mostly as one piece. A rope, wire, hair, spring, ribbon, or soft robot can bend in many places at once. A small contact point can change the whole motion. A slight twist can decide whether a knot tightens or loosens. A small material mismatch can make a flat sheet jump into a wrinkled shape.

That is why the lecture keeps returning to energy. Energy is a way to score how much the object dislikes its current shape. If a rod is bent, twisted, or stretched, it stores energy. The force is the push that tries to lower that energy. In plain words: the object moves toward shapes that cost less.

bending changes direction
twisting changes orientation
stretching changes length
energy creates force

Different Thin Objects Need Different Simplifications

The talk first sorts thin objects by their size. A beam is long compared with its height and width, and it usually bends in a plane. A rod is also long, but it can bend, twist, and stretch in three dimensions. A ribbon is long and narrow, but its width still matters. A plate is wide and thin, usually starting flat. A shell is also thin, but its natural shape may already be curved in three dimensions.

This sorting matters because the wrong simplification gives the wrong answer. A very narrow strip may behave like a rod. A wider ribbon may not. If we pretend every thin object is just a centerline, we may miss the side-to-side deformation that controls buckling and force.

beam: mostly planar bending
rod: bend, twist, stretch
ribbon: length and width matter
plate or shell: surface matters

Discrete Elastic Rods Turn A Curve Into Computable Pieces

The physics baseline is the discrete elastic rod model. A continuous rod can be imagined as a smooth centerline plus a twist angle along that line. To compute with it, the line is broken into nodes and small segments. The unknowns are the node positions and the twist angles.

The rod energy has three everyday parts. Bending says the rod pays a cost when neighboring segments change direction. Twisting says it pays a cost when the material frame rotates around the centerline. Stretching says it pays a cost when a segment is longer or shorter than its preferred length.

Once the energy is written down, the force is not guessed. The force is the negative gradient of the energy, meaning it points in the direction that lowers the energy fastest. Then Newton's law moves every degree of freedom: mass times acceleration equals the total force.

nodes and twist angles
bending, twisting, stretching energy
force lowers energy
Newton moves the rod

Contact Is Not Just A Detail

Flexible structures touch themselves and the world. A knot touches itself. A soft robot touches a slope. Flagella touch nearby filaments. Contact changes the motion because two parts cannot pass through each other, and friction changes how easily they slide.

The ideal contact rule is harsh: no overlap is allowed. But a harsh step rule is difficult for fast numerical solvers because it is not smooth. The talk explains a smoother contact energy. As two rod pieces get too close, the energy rises. Because that energy is smooth, the simulation can take derivatives and solve faster.

two rod pieces approach
overlap should be impossible
smooth barrier energy
fast contact solve

The Same Mechanics Explains Robots, Knots, And Flagella

Jawed shows this physics before adding machine learning because it proves what should not be thrown away. A soft robot with shape-memory-alloy limbs can be simulated on slopes and bumpy surfaces. The simulation may not copy every experimental detail, but it captures the shape of the behavior and can run faster than real time for some settings.

Knots show why contact and friction are not optional. Different overhand knots can suddenly snap or buckle at different points. Bacterial flagella show the same idea in a biological setting. Several rotating helical filaments interact through fluid forces, contact, and friction. Their bundling behavior changes when friction changes.

soft robot on terrain
knots with contact
flagella in fluid
mechanics still matters

The Machine Should Learn The Constitutive Law, Not The Whole Universe

The important modeling decision comes next. If Newton's law is already known, learning the entire governing equation from scratch is wasteful and risky. The better question is narrower: what energy should this simplified object have so that it behaves like the detailed object?

A constitutive law is the rule that says how a material stores energy when it is bent, twisted, stretched, compressed, or sheared. In everyday words, it is the object's stiffness rule. The lecture uses machine learning to learn that stiffness rule while keeping the outer mechanics intact.

do not learn all motion
keep mass times acceleration
learn stiffness energy
get reduced model

The Slinky Example Makes The Idea Concrete

A full Slinky is a three-dimensional coil. Simulating every wire detail is expensive. But if we only care about the large motion of the Slinky, we would like a cheaper model that behaves like a beam or centerline model.

The reduced model breaks the Slinky into small groups of rings. The model describes each local group with relative coordinates instead of absolute positions. That choice is important. If the whole Slinky is shifted or rotated in space, the internal energy should not change. Relative coordinates help enforce that common-sense rule.

full 3D coil
local ring triplets
relative coordinates
beam-like reduced motion

Symmetry Is A First-Principles Constraint

The Slinky energy should also respect reflection. If a left-right mirrored version is physically the same kind of state, the learned energy should not change just because the coordinate description is mirrored. The talk handles this by feeding reflected versions through the network and combining the resulting energies.

This is a useful pattern across the course. Do not ask the data to rediscover a rule that we already know. If translation, rotation, or reflection should not change the energy, build that into the learning setup.

shift should not matter
rotation should not matter
reflection should not matter
energy respects symmetry

The Payoff Is Speed With A Reasonable Safety Rail

The learned Slinky model runs far faster than the full discrete elastic rod simulation in the example, around tens of times faster. It also generalizes beyond the exact training rollout: longer time, changed length, changed boundary condition, lower density, and lower elasticity are tested.

The deeper lesson is not that the neural network is magically robust. It is more modest and more useful. Because the network learns energy inside a physical shell, the model is less free to invent impossible dynamics than a direct next-step predictor.

full model is costly
learn local energy
run reduced simulation
test changed cases

The Bad Baselines Teach The Boundary

The talk compares the energy-learning approach with more direct neural choices: mapping coordinates to the next coordinates, mapping coordinates to forces, or mapping coordinates to energy without enough structure. These simpler black-box choices can drift or fail when their outputs are differentiated inside a simulator.

This is a big warning. In scientific computing, a small prediction error may become a large force error after taking derivatives. A model that looks fine for one-step prediction can behave poorly when it is placed inside a long physical rollout.

predict next state directly
small error appears
derivatives amplify it
rollout drifts away

Ribbons Show Why A Simple Rod Can Be Too Simple

The second reduction example is a ribbon. A ribbon is not just a rod with a prettier name. It has enough width that plate-like behavior can matter. A full plate simulation can capture that behavior, but it is expensive. A classical rod simulation is cheaper, but it can be wrong when the ribbon gets wider.

The experiment clamps a ribbon, compresses it into a bent shape, then pushes it sideways. The ribbon can form U, US, and S shapes. At a certain point it goes through a pitchfork bifurcation, which means the symmetric shape loses stability and two mirrored alternatives become possible. The reaction force reaches a maximum near that switch.

clamped ribbon
side displacement
U, US, or S shape
bifurcation changes force

The Learned Ribbon Energy Fills The Gap

The lecture then uses a neural-ODE-style reduced model. The governing motion law stays. The neural network represents the elastic energy that a rod-like reduced model should feel if it is trying to imitate a wider ribbon or plate.

For narrow ribbons, the classical rod model, the learned model, plate simulation, and experiment agree better. For wider ribbons, the classical rod model misses important behavior, while the learned rod-like energy moves closer to the plate and experiment. It is not presented as perfect; it is presented as a way to keep the cheap coordinates while learning the missing material behavior.

cheap rod-like coordinates
plate data teaches energy

better wide-ribbon behavior

Inverse Design Starts From A Flat Sheet Problem

The final part asks the backward question. Instead of asking what shape a structure will take, ask how to fabricate a flat structure so that it becomes a desired three-dimensional shape after release.

The key physical trick is strain mismatch. A simple one-dimensional example is a bimetallic strip. Two materials expand differently when heated, so the joined strip bends. In two dimensions, a stretched bottom layer can be glued to an unstretched top layer. When released, the layers disagree about their preferred size, and the sheet bends or wrinkles.

bottom layer stretched
top layer unstretched
glue and release
sheet bends or wrinkles

Radial Symmetry Does Not Guarantee A Hemisphere

A beginner might expect a round, radially symmetric setup to make a smooth hemispherical cap. But beyond a critical pre-stretch, the lower-energy shape may be wrinkled. The talk describes different wrinkle modes: no wrinkle, two wrinkles, three wrinkles, four wrinkles, and so on.

This is another first-principles lesson. The shape is not chosen by visual symmetry alone. It is chosen by energy. If a wrinkled shape costs less energy than a smooth cap, the sheet can wrinkle even when the setup looks symmetric.

symmetric disk
pre-stretch increases
energy curves cross
wrinkle mode wins

Kirigami Gives The Designer A Handle

To make inverse design possible, the talk introduces kirigami: cutting patterns into the top layer. Cuts change where the sheet can stretch, fold, and relax. That gives the designer control over the final three-dimensional shape.

A lotus-like cut pattern can produce different shapes as pre-stretch changes. At some pre-stretches it may look like a hemispherical cap. At others it can take a taco-like mode. At still higher pre-stretch it may return toward a cap. The important point is that the cuts and the pre-stretch work together.

choose cut pattern
choose pre-stretch
release sheet
shape is controlled

The Autoencoder Shrinks The Design Search

If the target is always a simple hemisphere, theory and hand analysis may help. But if the target is a flower, peanut, face-like patch, or body-fitting health monitor, the cut pattern is too large to search pixel by pixel.

The lecture uses a variational autoencoder to compress many possible kirigami images into a small set of latent numbers. The training images are synthetic random cut patterns, generated in a systematic way from a quadrant and its symmetries. After training, the design search does not change every pixel directly. It searches a small latent code plus physical parameters such as pre-stretch and structure size.

many cut-pattern images
encoder compresses
six latent numbers
optimizer searches code

The Target Shape Is Judged By Height

To compare a designed shape with the desired shape, the talk uses a structural similarity measure based on the heights of the nodes. A score of one means the shapes match perfectly. A score near zero means they are very different.

In the examples, the optimization can find multiple designs for the same target shape. That is not a bug. Flexible inverse design often has many answers: different cut patterns and parameters can land on similar final shapes.

target 3D shape
decode cut pattern
simulate or test release
compare heights

Bistability Adds A Second Use Case

The talk then adds a tri-layer structure: a kirigami layer, a substrate layer, and another kirigami layer. This can make the structure bistable. Bistable means it has two stable shapes. It needs power to switch between them, but it does not need constant power to remain in either one.

That matters for soft robotic grippers and reconfigurable robots. A gripper can snap between open and closed. A robot can change shape for walking, swimming, or carrying. The same inverse-design idea can be adapted by tuning a few parameters, even when the bilayer solution does not transfer directly to the tri-layer case.

tri-layer sheet

grip or reconfigure

Why This Matters For Design And Topology

Topology design is about arranging material, voids, cuts, pores, fibers, channels, or layers so that the final object does what we want. This lecture connects directly to that problem because kirigami cuts are a topology choice. A cut pattern changes the path of stress and strain. A learned latent code becomes a smaller map of possible topologies that the optimizer can search.

The flexible-structure examples also explain why topology cannot be judged only by appearance. The best pattern is the one that gives the right energy landscape. For a ribbon, the energy landscape decides when it bifurcates. For a bilayer sheet, it decides whether a cap or wrinkle wins. For a gripper, it decides whether the object has one stable shape or two.

The bigger lesson is that design should preserve the physics we trust. If the object moves by Newton's law, keep Newton's law. If forces come from energy, keep that relationship. If symmetries should hold, build them in. Then let learning handle the part that is genuinely hard: the reduced energy, the cut-pattern code, or the search through many possible designs.

topology changes material layout
energy decides shape
learning shrinks search
physics keeps it inspectable
