Narrative chapter

Make Fast Scientific Models Say How Much To Trust Them

This chapter uses video 139, Michael Shields's DDPS talk on the nexus of machine learning, physics-based modeling, and uncertainty quantification. The talk is about a problem that appears after scientific machine learning starts to work. Once a surrogate model predicts fast, how do we know how much to trust the prediction?

The first-principles problem is simple. A classical simulation already has uncertainty: uncertain inputs, uncertain parameters, numerical error, model-form error, and measurement error. When we replace or augment that simulation with a machine-learning surrogate, we add new uncertainties: architecture choice, training data, discretization choices, loss choices, optimization choices, and millions of learned weights. A fast answer without uncertainty can be fast in the wrong direction.

Read This Chapter As One Claim

Start from an ordinary decision. You want to choose a shape, a material, a control setting, or a safety margin, but the full physics simulation is slow. A learned surrogate gives an answer quickly. The hidden question is whether the answer is solid enough to act on. This chapter says speed alone is not trust. A scientific model must also show where its answer may be weak, because the expensive mistake often happens exactly where the shortcut is least sure.

Everyday problemYou need a fast answer, but you also need to know whether that answer is safe to use.

The Everyday Version

Imagine a shortcut road that saves an hour. It is useful only if you know when it is flooded, under construction, or unreliable at night. A surrogate model is the shortcut road. It can save enormous compute, but it must also say when the shortcut is risky.

This is why the talk emphasizes uncertainty quantification, or UQ, for scientific machine learning. The goal is not to make the model nervous for no reason. The goal is to attach the right amount of doubt to the prediction, so a scientist or engineer can decide whether to trust it, refine it, run the expensive solver, or gather more data.

Why Foundation Models Make UQ Urgent

Scientific foundation models are large models trained to work across broad physics tasks. They are meant to generalize across regimes, equations, boundary conditions, geometries, and downstream tasks. That promise makes UQ more important, not less. The broader the model's intended use, the more ways it can be uncertain.

The talk points to verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification as missing infrastructure for foundation models. Traditional modeling has VVUQ practices. Foundation models do not yet have mature analogs. Trust will require uncertainty estimates that are interpretable, meaningful, and tied to the physical task.

foundation model claims broad use
broad use creates many failure routes
traditional VVUQ tools do not directly transfer
interpretable uncertainty supports trust

Where Uncertainty Enters

The talk breaks a foundation model into components. There is tokenization, which in scientific machine learning often means discretization in space and time. There is the learning objective, such as a regression loss or a physics-informed loss. There is the model architecture: neural operator, transformer, graph model, encoder-decoder, or something else. There is training: pretraining, fine-tuning, data curation, learning schedules, and heterogeneous simulation or experimental data.

Every one of these choices injects uncertainty. The grid can be wrong for the phenomenon. The loss can prioritize the wrong quantity. The architecture can be poorly matched. The parameters can be underdetermined. The training data can be biased. The key engineering move is to break the giant model into these sources rather than treating uncertainty as one vague output bar.

discretization or tokenization
loss and learning objective
architecture and parameterization
training data and learned weights

Bayesian Neural Networks

A Bayesian neural network treats weights as uncertain rather than fixed. Instead of one best set of parameters, it tries to represent a posterior distribution over parameters given the data. That posterior can then be pushed through the network to produce predictive uncertainty.

This lets the model split uncertainty into two broad kinds. Aleatory uncertainty is irreducible noise or variability in the system. Epistemic uncertainty is lack of knowledge from limited data or uncertain parameters. This split matters because the response is different. More data can reduce epistemic uncertainty. More data cannot remove true randomness or variability.

weights become distributions
posterior reflects data and prior
aleatory uncertainty is system variability
epistemic uncertainty is missing knowledge

The Cost Ladder

The problem is scale. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo can sample from a posterior with high fidelity, but it is expensive. It is a gold-standard type method for smaller Bayesian neural networks, not something you can naively apply to billions of parameters. Variational inference is cheaper because it assumes a simpler posterior form and optimizes that approximation. Monte Carlo dropout is cheaper still, but it makes even stronger assumptions.

So the choice is a ladder. At the top, expensive methods give better posterior fidelity. At the bottom, cheap methods scale better but may give less reliable uncertainty. The talk does not pretend this tradeoff disappears. It tries to find combinations that keep useful uncertainty without destroying the surrogate's speed advantage.

HMC: strong posterior sampling, high cost
variational inference: cheaper, assumed posterior
dropout: cheaper, stronger assumptions
surrogate UQ must not erase speed savings

Learning Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

The talk briefly discusses accelerating Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with Hamiltonian neural networks. HMC evolves a Hamiltonian system to propose posterior samples. A neural network can learn parts of that Hamiltonian evolution, using automatic differentiation to compute needed gradients.

This works nicely for low-dimensional problems and has been pushed to hundreds of dimensions, but that is still tiny compared with foundation models. The point is not that this solves large foundation-model UQ. The point is that high-quality Bayesian sampling is possible in principle, but scaling it requires new structure.

HMC samples by evolving Hamiltonian dynamics
neural network can learn the evolution
works best at lower dimension
foundation scale needs further reduction

Testing Cheaper Bayesian Approximations

The microstructure stress example compares HMC, Bayes by Backprop, and Monte Carlo dropout on a Bayesian U-Net-style problem. The input is a microstructure, and the output is the mean and variance of a stress field under uniaxial tension. The model size is modest by foundation-model standards, but large enough to see differences.

The result is that Bayes by Backprop tracks HMC uncertainty much better than Monte Carlo dropout in this case. That does not make variational inference perfect. It means the cheap approximation can be useful if checked against a stronger method on problems where that stronger method is still feasible.

input is a material microstructure
output is stress mean and variance
HMC provides a stronger comparison
Bayes by Backprop tracks HMC better than dropout

Initialization And Ensembles

Variational inference can depend strongly on initialization. The talk shows a simple function where different initializations lead to different uncertainty behavior. To address that, the group uses pseudo-Bayesian model averaging: train several variational models, assign weights based on predictive quality, and combine them.

This is a model-form uncertainty idea. Instead of trusting one trained approximation, let several plausible approximations contribute. The price is ensemble cost. The gain is a more diverse and often better-calibrated uncertainty picture.

VI can depend on initialization
train several Bayesian approximations
weight models by predictive quality
ensemble gives broader uncertainty picture

The VI-HMC Sensitivity Idea

The central scaling idea is VI-HMC. The hypothesis is that not every parameter in a huge model matters equally for uncertainty. Train first with variational inference. Use gradients and a variance contribution estimate to rank parameters by sensitivity. Freeze the low-sensitivity parameters. Then apply HMC only to the subset that most affects predictive uncertainty.

This is not exact full HMC. It is a compromise. But it can move uncertainty estimates closer to HMC quality while reducing the sampling dimension by orders of magnitude. In the hypersonic DeepONet example, a much smaller subset of parameters accounts for most of the uncertainty. In the Bayesian Fourier neural operator building example, the talk reports reducing millions of parameters to thousands of influential ones for UQ purposes.

start with variational inference
rank parameters by uncertainty sensitivity
freeze low-impact parameters
sample influential subset with HMC

Examples: Hypersonics And Structures

The hypersonic case predicts pressure spectra at sensors on a nose cone from incoming-flow energy-spectrum parameters. The model is a Bayesian DeepONet. Sensitivity ranking identifies a much smaller parameter subset that captures most of the output uncertainty, allowing HMC on that reduced subset.

The structural dynamics case uses a Bayesian Fourier neural operator for a six-story shear building under stochastic ground acceleration with nonlinear floor behavior. The full model has millions of parameters. The sensitivity screen reduces the UQ-active subset to thousands of parameters. This is still hard, but it is a different problem than sampling all weights blindly.

hypersonic DeepONet predicts wall pressure spectra
sensitivity picks uncertainty-active parameters
Bayesian FNO models nonlinear building response
millions of weights reduce to thousands for UQ

Dimension Reduction Still Matters

The talk connects this to a long line of dimension-reduction work. High-dimensional inputs and outputs can be mapped to lower-dimensional latent spaces, a surrogate can be built there, and the result can be lifted back. This includes manifold polynomial chaos expansions, principal geodesic analysis, and neural operators bookended by autoencoders.

The reason is practical. If you can compress the physical state before learning or before doing UQ, you reduce the burden on the model. Dimension reduction and Bayesian sampling are not separate worlds. They can be combined so the uncertainty problem lives in a smaller, more meaningful space.

high-dimensional field is compressed
surrogate works in latent space
uncertainty is quantified on compressed variables
decoder returns physical predictions

Discretization Is Also Uncertain

Scientific data do not arrive like clean text tokens. Inputs and outputs may be on different meshes, different resolutions, and different fidelity levels. If an operator learner requires a fixed input grid, it can confuse discretization with physics. That creates uncertainty in the "tokenization" step.

The resolution-independent neural operator, or RINO, addresses this by projecting arbitrary input samples onto a shared representation before applying the operator. A physics-informed version uses dictionary learning and physics constraints. The larger point is that foundation models will need ways to combine computational data, experimental data, resolutions, and fidelities without pretending they are all the same.

scientific inputs may use different meshes
fixed-grid operators can be too rigid
projection creates a shared embedding
multifidelity foundation data become more usable

The Q And A Boundaries

The Q and A clarifies the limits. Parameter reduction is not guaranteed. It depends on architecture, overparameterization, data richness, and physical problem structure. Some problems may have a small uncertainty-active subset. Others may not. HMC itself carries assumptions and practical challenges, including smooth posteriors, gradients, initialization, and high cost.

There is also a practical surrogate-model question: if UQ is too expensive, it defeats the reason for building the surrogate. The speaker's answer is honest. The field is not fully there for very large models. The goal is to make UQ an add-on that remains computationally tractable, not a second problem more expensive than the original simulation.

sensitivity reduction depends on the problem
HMC assumptions can fail or become expensive
ensembles add cost
UQ must stay cheaper than losing the surrogate benefit

The Design And Topology Connection

This chapter matters for design and topology because design choices should not be made from a fast but uncalibrated surrogate. A topology optimizer may remove a load path, change a flow channel, alter a material microstructure, or modify a building response. If the surrogate is uncertain in exactly that region, the optimizer can confidently choose a bad design.

UQ changes the design loop. Instead of asking only "what design has the best predicted score?" the loop can ask "what design has a strong score and acceptable uncertainty?" It can also decide where to run the expensive solver next, where to collect data, or where a lower-fidelity prediction is too risky.

topology changes can leave training support
surrogate may be uncertain near new designs
optimizer needs score plus uncertainty
expensive solves can target risky regions

How This Connects To Earlier Chapters

This chapter connects directly to Chapters 131 and 132. Those chapters discuss scientific foundation models and the need for generalization. This chapter adds the missing trust layer: if a model generalizes, it should say how uncertain that generalization is. It connects to Chapter 134 because assimilation and digital twins also need uncertainty-aware state estimates. It connects to operator-learning chapters because DeepONets, FNOs, and neural operators are the surrogate models receiving UQ treatment here.

The new lesson is that uncertainty is not one number bolted onto the end. It comes from the structure of the whole model-building pipeline.

The Boundary Of The Claim

The claim is not that scalable UQ for foundation models is solved. It is not. Bayesian sampling over billions of parameters remains out of reach. Variational methods and dropout make assumptions. Ensembles add cost. Discretization and training-objective uncertainties are still open research problems.

The honest claim is that there is a path. Break the model into components. Use Bayesian tools where possible. Compare cheap approximations to stronger samplers on tractable problems. Focus expensive sampling on uncertainty-sensitive parameters. Use dimension reduction and resolution-independent embeddings to control the size of the problem.

foundation-scale Bayesian sampling is not solved
cheap UQ methods make assumptions
discretization and objective uncertainty remain open
component-wise reduction is the path forward

What You Should Remember

The heart of the lecture is that scientific machine learning must earn trust, not only speed. A surrogate model should not only produce a field, a spectrum, or a design score. It should also expose the uncertainty created by its data, architecture, parameters, discretization, objective, and training.

If you remember one sentence, remember this: a fast scientific model becomes useful for decisions only when it can also say where its own answer is fragile.
