Carry Water Hazard Uncertainty Through The Whole Chain

Read This Chapter As One Claim

Start with a coastal town asking a simple question: if a rare water event happens, what should we build, strengthen, evacuate, or insure? The answer cannot come from one pretty wave picture. A wave starts far away, changes shape near land, pushes water and debris through streets, loads a structure, and then becomes damage, repair cost, downtime, or loss of life. This chapter is about carrying uncertainty through that whole path so the final decision is honest.

Everyday problem A coast faces rare flooding, moving debris, and expensive infrastructure choices, but the event is uncertain before it even reaches land.
Shared promise Use the right model at each scale, then pass the uncertainty from ocean wave to local force to structural damage to public decision.
Hidden danger If one link is treated as certain, the final risk number can look precise while hiding the biggest unknowns.
First rejection test If changing the event, debris load, material property, or failure threshold changes the decision, the workflow must show that uncertainty instead of burying it.

This chapter uses video 67, Ajay Harish's DDPS talk on uncertainty quantification and deep learning for water-hazard prediction. The talk is about tsunamis, storm surge, debris, coastal structures, and the hard practical question of turning large fluid simulations into useful risk decisions.

The first-principles question is this: when a rare water event hits a coast, how do we move from ocean-scale wave motion to building-scale damage while keeping track of uncertainty at every step?

decision under uncertainty

The Main Risk Is Not Only Water Height

A tsunami or storm surge is dangerous because it moves water, but the damage is not only a question of wave height. The moving water can carry ships, containers, cars, timber, and other debris. A slow-moving heavy object can still hit with large momentum.

That is why the talk keeps returning to infrastructure. A coastal building, port, evacuation structure, oil rig, or critical facility needs to survive the water force and the debris force. The model has to help answer whether the structure crosses a damage threshold.

wave water moves
debris is carried
impact adds load
structure may fail

Tsunamis And Storm Surge Start Differently

Tsunamis often start from seafloor motion during an earthquake, or from a landslide pushing a large water mass. Storm surge comes from hurricane pressure and wind pushing water toward land. The causes differ, but the engineering question near the coast becomes similar: what water depth, velocity, and force reach the built environment?

This matters because the full hazard cannot be represented by one number. Event size, location, bathymetry, topography, wind, pressure, debris, and structure properties all feed the final risk.

earthquake or landslide
hurricane wind and pressure
coastal water motion
building load question

Use Shallow Water For The Ocean Scale

In the deep ocean, tsunami wavelengths are very long compared with water depth. The wave may be hard to notice offshore, then grows as it reaches shallower water. Because the horizontal scale is so large, shallow-water equations are a practical model for ocean-scale propagation.

These equations track the water column height and depth-averaged velocity. They are much cheaper than full 3D fluid simulation across the whole ocean. That is the first model-scale choice: use the cheaper model where it is the right level of detail.

long wavelength offshore
track water height
track averaged velocity
simulate ocean basin cheaply

The Coast Needs Finer Resolution

Near land, the question changes. We care about inundation, streets, ports, local channels, and water entering dry cells. The model must handle cells changing from dry to wet as water moves inland.

The talk shows adaptive mesh refinement: coarse grid far offshore, much finer grid near Crescent City or Honolulu. In the example, the open ocean grid can be roughly hundred-kilometer scale, while the coastal grid can be meter-scale. The mesh follows the decision need.

coarse grid offshore
wave approaches coast
wet and dry cells change
fine grid where damage matters

3D CFD Is Needed For Local Forces

Shallow-water models can estimate inundation and depth, but they are not enough to compute detailed force on a building. For that, the talk connects the 2D shallow-water solution to a 3D CFD model such as OpenFOAM.

This is a coupling problem. Depth-averaged velocity from the shallow-water model has to become a 3D boundary condition for the local fluid model. The local CFD then gives time histories of pressure and force on structures.

2D wave model
depth-averaged velocity
3D CFD boundary
force time series

Then The Structure Carries Its Own Uncertainty

Once the water force is known, the structural model still has uncertain inputs. Material stiffness, geometry, boundary conditions, and damage state may vary. The talk starts with simple examples such as uncertainty in Young's modulus, then propagates that through structural response.

This is where structural UQ is cheaper than repeated CFD. The fluid simulation may take days, while structural finite element runs may be fast enough for sampling, reliability, sensitivity, or surrogate analysis.

force from CFD
uncertain material
structural response
damage probability

Reliability Asks Whether A Threshold Is Crossed

For many engineering decisions, the exact displacement is less important than whether a limit is crossed. Does the structure exceed a damage threshold? Does downtime become unacceptable? Does repair cost become too high?

Brute-force Monte Carlo can require huge numbers of simulations for rare failure probabilities. That is why HydroUQ includes methods such as Latin hypercube sampling, importance sampling, subset simulation, surrogate models, and global sensitivity analysis.

define failure threshold
sample uncertain inputs
estimate crossing probability
support design decision

Surrogates Are Needed Where Runs Are Too Slow

The talk is practical about cost. Structural engineers want quick answers. CFD engineers may accept a week-long simulation. A design workflow cannot wait for a week every time it changes a parameter.

So the model chain uses surrogate models where repeated high-fidelity runs are too expensive. HydroUQ includes neural networks and Gaussian processes, and the talk points toward design of experiments so that each expensive run teaches the surrogate as much as possible.

full CFD is slow
many decisions need repeats
train surrogate
query risk faster

Deep Learning Also Compresses The Simulation Data

In the Q and A, Harish describes graph neural networks trained on shallow-water and CFD simulations. They act as surrogates, but also as a compression mechanism. Massive simulation output can be terabytes; a trained graph model can be much smaller and still reproduce the fields well enough for visualization or post-processing.

This is a different use of deep learning from classification. The problem is not "label this image." The problem is "do not force every user to download huge field data when a compact learned representation can carry the usable information."

huge CFD fields
train graph model
store compact representation
visualize or query locally

Sampling Should Become More Physics-Aware

The talk mentions Latin hypercube sampling for choosing simulation cases. In the discussion, the host points out that physics-based sampling could choose the next expensive simulation by looking at where the current model has the largest physics error.

That is an important open direction. Random space-filling samples are useful, but expensive CFD runs should eventually be placed where they reduce decision uncertainty the most.

Latin hypercube covers space
CFD runs are expensive
physics error indicates need
sample where it helps most

The Human Decision Layer Is Part Of The Model

The Q and A also asks how these tools reach city planners, governments, insurers, and communities. That matters because a hazard model is not finished when it produces a field plot. It is finished only when it helps choose evacuation structures, premiums, retrofits, zoning, or resilience investments.

The speaker notes that this requires communication across engineering, climate science, geophysics, insurance, city planning, and community recovery. The model scale is local, but the decision impact is social.

simulate hazard
estimate damage and downtime
communicate risk
improve resilience decision

Why This Also Matters For Design

Design and topology optimization for coastal infrastructure cannot rely only on nominal loads. A vertical evacuation building, port structure, sea wall, or critical facility must be designed against uncertain water levels, uncertain debris, uncertain structure properties, and uncertain future events.

This chapter gives a design workflow: use the right model fidelity at each scale, propagate uncertainty through the coupled chain, use surrogates where repeated runs are too expensive, and judge designs by risk thresholds rather than only by one deterministic response.

choose coastal design
propagate hazard uncertainty
test thresholds
select resilient option

The Chapter In One Chain

First, tsunamis and storm surges threaten coastal structures through water motion and debris impact. Second, the event source can be earthquake, landslide, hurricane pressure, or wind. Third, shallow-water equations are used for ocean-scale wave propagation because full 3D CFD is too expensive at that scale. Fourth, adaptive mesh refinement puts resolution near the coast where inundation matters. Fifth, shallow-water outputs become boundary conditions for local 3D CFD. Sixth, CFD produces force time series on structures. Seventh, structural uncertainty then affects displacement, damage, repair cost, and downtime. Eighth, reliability analysis asks whether response crosses a threshold. Ninth, surrogate models are needed because repeated CFD is too slow for design and UQ. Tenth, graph neural networks can also compress massive simulation output. Eleventh, future sampling should become more physics-aware so expensive runs are placed where they reduce uncertainty most. Twelfth, design and city planning need the full chain, from hazard source to community consequence, not only a pretty simulation.

The chapter's core idea: water-hazard prediction is a chain of coupled approximations. The uncertainty has to travel through the chain before the design decision can be trusted.

How To Test Whether You Understood This Chapter

Explain why height is not enough A water hazard is not only "how high did the water get." Moving water has speed, direction, duration, and debris. A building can survive depth but fail when a container, car, or log is carried into it.
Explain the model handoff Shallow-water equations are useful across the ocean because the wave is huge and full 3D detail is too expensive. Near a building, the question becomes local pressure and force, so the shallow-water result has to feed a local 3D fluid model.
Explain why reliability matters The decision is often about crossing a threshold: too much displacement, too much damage, too much repair cost, or too much downtime. That is why the workflow estimates risk, not only one best-guess displacement.
Connect this to topology and design A coastal structure's shape should not be optimized for one clean load case. The design has to be tested against uncertain water levels, debris impacts, material variation, and future events, then judged by whether it still protects the decision that matters.
