Do Not Call It Foundational Until It Generalizes

This chapter uses video 136, Youngsoo Choi's DDPS talk on defining foundation models for computational science. The talk is not mainly about showing one more fast surrogate. It is about vocabulary discipline. Many scientific machine learning papers call their model a foundation model. The speaker asks what that should actually mean.

The first-principles problem is this: in ordinary AI, a foundation model earns the name because it is trained broadly and can be adapted to many tasks. In computational science, many models are trained on one dataset, one geometry, one equation family, or one mesh regime. They may predict fast, but if changing the boundary condition, geometry, initial condition, or task forces retraining from scratch, the word foundational is doing too much work.

Read This Chapter As One Claim

The chapter is asking for honesty in naming. A fast surrogate for one equation is useful, but it is not automatically a foundation model. In computational science, the label should mean that the model can serve as a reusable base across meaningful scientific changes: new domains, boundary conditions, tasks, geometries, source terms, scales, and physical regimes. DDFM is proposed as one route because it learns local scientific building blocks and assembles them through a physics solve, closer to how finite elements earn reuse.

The Everyday Version

Imagine someone calls a wrench a universal repair kit. If the wrench only fits one bolt in one machine, the label is misleading. It may still be useful, but it is not universal. A real repair kit works across many situations because it has reusable structure.

This is the lecture's attitude toward foundation models. A model trained on one fluid dataset and reused only after days of retraining may be a good surrogate. It may be valuable. But calling it foundational hides the real limitation. The name should be reserved for models that serve as reusable bases across scientific problems.

The Definition

The proposed definition has five central pieces. A computational-science foundation model should be data-driven. It should be trained on a broad distribution of scientific application types or physical systems. It should generalize across scientific problems, computational domains, tasks, and physical conditions. It should avoid retraining from scratch or structural modification when adapting. It should serve as a reusable basis.

The definition is stricter than "large model trained on scientific data." It asks what the model can do when the situation changes. Can it handle new boundary conditions? New parameters? Forward and inverse tasks? Different equations? Different geometries? Uncertainty quantification? Larger scales? If the answer is mostly no, the model may not deserve the foundation label.

data-driven model
broad scientific training distribution
wide generalization across tasks and domains
reusable basis without starting over

Desirable Scientific Traits

The talk then adds four desirable traits from computational science. The model should be scientifically consistent, meaning it should respect physical laws such as conservation, symmetry, or invariance where those laws matter. It should have mathematical rigor where possible: stability, convergence, well-posedness, or error analysis. It should extrapolate robustly beyond the exact training region. It should improve as better and broader data are added.

These traits come from the history of numerical methods. Finite elements and finite volumes are not called foundational because they are fashionable. They are foundational because they provide reusable, analyzable procedures across many problems. A computational-science foundation model should aspire to the same level of responsibility, not just the AI meaning of scale.

respect physical laws
support analysis where possible
extrapolate beyond the training region
improve with better data

Why This Is Harder Than Language

Computational science data are not like text tokens. A single simulation sample can contain millions, billions, or more values. A high-dimensional turbulence simulation in a tokamak setting may be gigabytes or terabytes for one data point. That makes storage, batching, transfer learning, and fine-tuning expensive before the model even starts to reason.

The data are also heterogeneous. One problem may be scalar, another vector-valued, another tensor-valued. Some are two-dimensional, some three-dimensional, some five-dimensional. Meshes and geometries change. Boundary conditions change. Long-time dynamics and multiscale structure make stability difficult. Scientific users also need trust and interpretability, because a visually plausible answer is not enough.

one sample can be enormous
physics data types are heterogeneous
mesh and geometry matter
trust requires more than visual plausibility

The DDFM Proposal

The talk proposes data-driven finite element methods, or DDFM, as one possible framework that could move toward the definition. The idea borrows the structure of finite elements. A traditional finite element method breaks a domain into elements, uses polynomial basis functions inside each element, assembles the global system, and solves the governing equations.

DDFM keeps the element-and-assembly logic but replaces the generic polynomial basis with a data-driven basis. The data-driven basis is trained locally, on small domains, components, or elements. Then many such learned elements are assembled into a larger domain, and the governing equations are still solved. In simple words: learn better local building blocks, but keep the global physics solve.

finite elements use generic polynomial bases
DDFM trains local data-driven bases
learned elements assemble into a global domain
governing equations still enforce physics

Why Local Training Helps

The local element idea attacks the huge-data problem. Instead of generating training data over one gigantic global geometry, generate diverse data on small reusable units: a strut, a joint, a porous-media cell, a reference element, or a small subdomain. Small local data are cheaper to generate, store, and train on. They are also reusable when the same kind of local structure appears in a larger domain.

This is the analogy to language tokens. A language model does not memorize every possible book as one huge object. It works with smaller pieces and learns how they combine. DDFM tries to do something similar for computation: break a domain into local pieces, learn useful local representations, and assemble them into a global physical calculation.

global simulations are huge
local element data are manageable
learned bases become reusable pieces
assembly builds the full physical problem

How The Basis Can Be Learned

The framework does not prescribe one learning method. A data-driven basis could come from compression, such as singular value decomposition, tensor decomposition, or autoencoders. It could come from sparse or symbolic regression, where local data identify a functional form or even a local governing equation. It could use implicit neural representations or neural operators to build mesh-free input-output maps. It could also mix learned bases with classical polynomial bases.

This flexibility is why the talk calls DDFM a framework rather than a single algorithm. The stable part is the idea: local learned representation plus global assembly plus governing physics. The specific basis technology can change as better AI tools appear.

compression builds compact local modes
symbolic regression can identify local laws
neural operators can provide mesh-free maps
hybrids can enrich classical bases

Example: Lattice Structures

The first demonstration uses lattice-type structures made from repeated components, such as struts and joints. Train data-driven bases on those components. Assemble them into a larger lattice. Then solve for displacement or stress under loads and boundary conditions.

The reported comparison is sharp. A monolithic finite element solve may involve millions of degrees of freedom. The DDFM representation reduces this to a few thousand degrees of freedom, giving large speedups with less than one percent relative error in the shown examples. Because the forward solve is fast, the same framework can also support inverse design: reduce weight while still carrying the load.

train on repeated lattice components
assemble a larger structure
solve far fewer degrees of freedom
use speed for inverse design

Example: Porous Media And Burgers Flow

The porous-media Navier-Stokes example shows nonlinear physics and different obstacle shapes. Each obstacle-cell type gets its own data-driven element. The global porous domain is assembled from those elements, and the method solves the flow with a reported speedup and low error compared with the traditional finite element method.

The time-dependent Burgers example shows that DDFM is not limited to static problems. A data-driven basis trained on a smaller domain is assembled into a larger spatial domain and used for time evolution. The talk also tests different source functions, including source behavior not identical to the training sources, and reports accurate results.

porous media uses obstacle-specific elements
nonlinear Navier-Stokes can be assembled
Burgers tests time-dependent behavior
different source functions probe generalization

Cross-PDE Demonstration

The talk also presents a more ambitious test: combine data from two different PDE types, such as a parabolic Poisson-like problem and a hyperbolic Burgers problem, into one data-driven basis. Then use that basis to solve either problem. The claim is not that all PDEs are solved by one basis now. The claim is that the framework can start to demonstrate cross-domain generalization.

This is important because the definition requires breadth. A model that only works on one equation with one source type does not become foundational by being fast. The cross-PDE test is preliminary evidence that local learned bases can carry reusable structure across different equation behavior.

train with multiple PDE data sources
build one local data-driven basis
solve parabolic-style tasks
solve hyperbolic-style tasks

The Foundation Claim Is Not Finished

The speaker is careful. DDFM is not presented as a completed foundation model. It is presented as a route toward one. The framework shows local training, global assembly, spatial extrapolation, forward and inverse tasks, nonlinear tests, time-dependent tests, and some cross-PDE behavior. But a lot remains open before it satisfies the full definition at scale.

The open work includes scalable solvers and preconditioners for the new block-dense system structure, mathematical analysis, error indicators, adaptive refinement, non-conforming assembly, user-friendly software, and broader demonstrations across domains. This caution is part of the lesson. A foundation-model claim should come with a checklist and evidence, not only a name.

preconditioners are still needed
new mathematical analysis is needed
adaptive refinement must be developed
usable software matters for reuse

The Q And A Boundaries

The Q and A clarifies the limits. A question about gas versus liquid flow brings out the domain issue: a model can be foundational within a clearly stated domain, but the domain must be named. A question about substructuring points to the old challenge: if a macro element must be as general as the full finite element method, it can become just as complex. Error estimation and adaptivity are therefore central, not optional.

Other questions ask whether DDFM is tied to finite elements. The answer is no. The name follows finite-element procedure, but finite difference, finite volume, spectral, or other discretizations could be used inside the framework. A question about large language models gets a similar boundary: computational science can borrow ideas from language models, but scientific data and physics constraints are different enough that off-the-shelf language models are not the answer.

foundation scope must be stated
error estimation controls macro-element risk
DDFM is not limited to FEM discretization
LLM ideas transfer, not LLMs directly

The Design And Topology Connection

This chapter is directly tied to design and topology. Lattice optimization is a topology problem: which members stay, which disappear, and how load paths survive. Porous media is also topology: obstacle shapes and connected channels control flow. DDFM tries to reuse local learned pieces across many assembled geometries, which is exactly what design loops need.

The larger design point is that a reusable computational-science foundation model should let a domain scientist change the geometry, boundary conditions, loads, source terms, or task without rebuilding the whole machine-learning pipeline. If the model can only handle the original topology, it is a local surrogate. If it can assemble reusable learned elements into new topologies while still enforcing physics, it starts moving toward foundational behavior.

lattice design changes load paths
porous design changes channels and obstacles
learned elements can be reused in new topologies
physics solve keeps the design accountable

How This Connects To Earlier Chapters

This chapter is the natural follow-up to Chapter 131. That chapter showed why PDE foundation models are attractive for sample efficiency. This chapter asks what standard they must meet before earning the name. It also connects to Chapter 129 because both keep a trusted physics structure while learning representations. It connects to reduced-order-model chapters because DDFM is a local compression and assembly strategy. It connects to symbolic-regression chapters because one possible DDFM basis could come from discovered local laws.

The new lesson is a naming discipline. The course has many fast surrogates. Not all of them are foundation models. A foundation model should be broad, reusable, scientifically consistent, and adaptable without rebuilding from scratch.

The Boundary Of The Claim

The claim is not that DDFM has already solved computational-science foundation models. It has preliminary demonstrations and a plausible structure. But the full standard is much larger: broad physics, geometry changes, mesh changes, forward and inverse tasks, uncertainty, scale-up, theory, software, adaptivity, and reliable error control.

The honest claim is this: if computational science wants to use the phrase foundation model rigorously, it must require broad generalization and reuse. DDFM is proposed as one route because it learns local data-driven bases, assembles them like a numerical method, and still solves physics globally.

preliminary success is not final proof

What You Should Remember

The heart of the lecture is that "foundation model" should be an earned label. A fast model is not enough. A large model is not enough. A model trained on scientific data is not enough. The model must generalize across meaningful scientific changes and serve as a reusable basis.

If you remember one sentence, remember this: in computational science, foundational means reusable across physics-aware tasks, not merely impressive on one dataset.
