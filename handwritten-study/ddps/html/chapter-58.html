Narrative chapter

Train The Controller Through The Model

Read This Chapter As One Claim

A learned controller should be trained through the world it must live in. The policy, model dynamics, objectives, constraints, uncertainty, and safety signals should be part of one differentiable rollout before the controller is trusted online.

Everyday problem Classical online control can be too slow for buildings, power grids, or embedded devices, while a detached black-box policy may ignore constraints or safety.
Shared promise Train an explicit policy offline by differentiating through the model, objective, and constraint graph, then deploy a fast forward pass at runtime.
Hidden danger A policy can look fast while violating actuator limits, state limits, comfort, safety boundaries, uncertainty cases, or the physical priors engineers already know.
First rejection test Reject the controller if constraints, Lyapunov or barrier checks, uncertainty sampling, and closed-loop rollout are not tested before deployment.

This chapter uses video 61, Jan Drgona's DDPS talk on differentiable programming for modeling and control of dynamical systems. The talk is about a practical control problem: we want the safety and structure of model-based control, but we also want the flexibility and speed of learned policies.

The first-principles question is this: if a controller must obey objectives, dynamics, and safety constraints, can we train the controller by differentiating through the whole closed-loop model instead of solving a hard optimization problem from scratch every time?

system must be controlled
online optimization is costly
differentiate through model rollout
precompute a usable policy

Two Control Worlds Meet

Model predictive control solves an optimization problem online. It uses a model of the system, predicts future behavior, and chooses actions that satisfy objectives and constraints. It is trusted in many industrial settings because constraints and performance can be stated directly.

Reinforcement learning learns by interaction. It is flexible when the equations are unknown or too hard to write, but it can need a lot of data and is harder to certify for safety. The talk aims for the middle: use differentiable models and automatic differentiation to train policies while keeping model-based objectives and constraints visible.

model predictive control has structure
reinforcement learning has flexibility
differentiable graph connects them
policy learns with constraints

Online Optimization Can Be Too Slow

In classical model predictive control, every new measurement or boundary condition can require solving a new optimization problem. That is acceptable when the system is small enough and enough compute is available.

For power networks, building systems, embedded devices, or fast controllers, solving a nonlinear constrained problem online may be too slow. The alternative is to learn an explicit map from the current situation to the control action, so runtime is just a forward pass.

new condition arrives
classical solver recomputes
runtime may be too slow
learn parametric solution map

Parametric Programming Means Solve A Family

A normal optimization solve fixes one case and finds one answer. Parametric programming asks for a rule that solves many related cases: different initial states, references, constraints, disturbances, or boundary conditions.

The learned policy is that rule. It maps parameters into actions. Training is expensive offline, but deployment can be fast because the policy has already learned how the answer changes across the family of cases.

many related control cases
parameters describe each case
policy maps case to action
fast online response

The Whole Problem Becomes A Differentiable Graph

The key move is to write the model, policy, objective, and constraints as differentiable components. The policy proposes actions. The model rolls the system forward. The objective measures tracking, energy use, cost, comfort, or other goals. The constraints penalize unsafe or infeasible behavior.

Because every part is differentiable, one scalar loss can be backpropagated through the whole graph. That produces gradients for training the policy directly.

policy chooses action
model rolls future state
objectives and constraints score rollout
backprop trains policy

Constraints Are Not Afterthoughts

The talk frames constraints as part of the loss through penalty or augmented Lagrangian ideas. This is similar in spirit to physics-informed training, but the residuals are not only PDE residuals. They can be optimization and control constraints.

This matters because controllers do not only need low prediction error. They must obey actuator limits, state limits, tracking requirements, safety regions, and operating constraints.

tracking objective
control effort objective
state and action constraints
one differentiable loss

Domain Priors Shrink The Search

The learned components do not have to be unrestricted black boxes. The talk shows structured linear maps that constrain singular values, sparsity, eigenvalue behavior, or other matrix properties.

The plain idea is to tell the model what an engineer already knows. If a building's temperature dynamics are dissipative, the learned model should not freely invent unstable heat behavior. If a network has a topology, the architecture should reflect that topology.

engineering knowledge
structured model component
smaller search space
more plausible learned dynamics

Building Control Is The First Example

A building thermal model predicts room temperatures from weather, occupancy, heating, cooling, and building envelope behavior. A detailed physics model can take months to build for one building.

The talk uses limited real time-series data and domain priors to learn a more robust model than a simple linear model. The learned model can then be placed inside a control graph to satisfy comfort and energy constraints.

room temperature data
building physics priors
learn thermal dynamics
control comfort and energy

Power Systems Are The Second Example

The power-system example uses network measurements such as voltages and frequencies. Classical control or dispatch can require solving expensive differential-algebraic and optimization problems.

By learning a differentiable surrogate model and training a policy offline, the method can produce near-real-time control actions with large speedups. The solution may be approximate, but it can be close enough to satisfy constraints and operate within the communication and sampling limits of the grid.

network state measurements
differentiable surrogate dynamics
offline policy training
fast online dispatch

Small Devices Need Precomputed Policies

The lab-device example is a ball floating in a tube controlled by a small Raspberry Pi system. A full online model predictive control solve can be too heavy for such limited hardware.

The differentiable-programming route trains the policy offline, then deploys only the light policy. That changes the runtime problem from solving an optimization problem to evaluating a learned control map.

small embedded controller
online solve too heavy
train policy offline
deploy light forward pass

Safety Needs Certificates

The talk discusses Lyapunov functions and control barrier functions. A Lyapunov function is like an energy landscape that proves the system moves toward a stable point. A barrier function indicates whether the system is approaching an unsafe boundary.

These tools can be learned, designed, or used as corrective layers. They turn safety from a vague hope into an explicit signal: keep moving toward the safe set, and trigger correction before crossing the boundary.

Lyapunov function checks convergence
barrier function checks safety boundary
policy may need correction
certificate guides safe action

Uncertainty Expands The Sampling Problem

Uncertainty can be added by sampling more dimensions: not only initial states and references, but disturbances and uncertain parameters. This can train a policy to handle variation.

The cost is more offline work. The open question is where to sample densely. The talk suggests that constraint boundaries and decision boundaries may need more samples than easy interior regions.

sample states and tasks
sample disturbances
focus near boundaries
train robust policy offline

How This Connects To Design And Topology

Design and topology optimization often creates systems that later need control: buildings with new layouts, power networks with new devices, thermal systems with new material paths, or robots with new bodies.

This chapter says the design loop should not stop at a passive simulation. If the final system will be controlled, then the design should be evaluated with the controller in the loop. Differentiable programming makes that possible because model, policy, objectives, and constraints can all sit in one graph.

design changes system
controller changes behavior
differentiate through both
optimize design with control reality

The Chapter In One Chain

First, model predictive control is trusted because it uses explicit models and constraints, but online optimization can be too slow. Second, reinforcement learning is flexible but can demand too much data and weak safety guarantees. Third, differentiable programming puts policy, dynamics, objectives, and constraints into one trainable graph. Fourth, parametric programming learns one policy over many related control cases. Fifth, constraints become part of the differentiable training loss. Sixth, domain priors make learned dynamics more plausible. Seventh, building control uses limited data and physics priors to learn usable thermal models. Eighth, power-system control can gain large runtime speedups by pretraining policies offline. Ninth, Lyapunov and barrier functions add safety certificates and correction signals. Tenth, design and topology loops should include controlled rollout when the final system will be operated by a controller.

The chapter's core idea: do not train a controller as a detached black box. Train it through the model, objectives, and constraints it must live with, then deploy only after safety and uncertainty checks are explicit.

How To Test Whether You Understood This Chapter

Say the problem without control words

You want a fast decision rule, but it must be trained while seeing the future consequences and the rules it is not allowed to break.

Explain why differentiability matters

If the policy, model, objective, and constraints form one differentiable graph, training can directly improve the closed-loop rollout instead of tuning pieces separately.

Connect it to topology and design

A design that will be actively controlled should be optimized with the controller in the loop. Otherwise the design may look good passively but fail under real operating actions and constraints.

State the honest limit

A fast learned policy still needs safety certificates, uncertainty sampling, boundary-case testing, and fallback behavior before it can replace online optimization in a real system.
