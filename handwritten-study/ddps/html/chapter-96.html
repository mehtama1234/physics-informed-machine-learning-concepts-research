Narrative chapter

Read Hidden Heart Waves From Motion

This chapter uses video 99, Jan Christoph's DDPS talk on predicting heart rhythm disorders from spatio-temporal imaging data using AI. The talk connects cardiac electrophysiology, mechanical contraction, optical mapping, ultrasound, spiral and scroll waves, phase singularities, U-Nets, diffusion models, and simulation-to-experiment transfer.

The first-principles question is this: if dangerous electrical waves move inside the heart, but we can only measure part of the heart, can motion and imaging data reveal the hidden electrical pattern?

The lecture's answer is cautiously yes, in controlled research settings. Electrical activation causes calcium release. Calcium causes contraction. The heart's motion is therefore not random motion. It is a delayed mechanical trace of an electrical wave. AI can learn that trace and infer electrical maps that are hard to measure directly.

Read This Chapter As One Claim

Start with the body-level problem. The dangerous thing in fibrillation is not just that the voltage is high or low. It is that electrical waves can rotate, split, hide inside the wall, and keep the heart from pumping as one coordinated muscle. Doctors can measure some electrical activity, but the measurements are sparse, invasive, or mostly surface-level. The key physical clue is that electrical activation does not stay invisible. It triggers calcium, calcium makes muscle contract, and contraction creates motion that ultrasound or imaging can see. This chapter says motion can be a delayed shadow of hidden electrical waves. AI is useful only if it learns that electromechanical shadow well enough to recover the organizing structure: spirals, scrolls, filaments, scars, and reentry paths, not just a nice-looking voltage image.

The Heart Is An Electromechanical Pump

Cardiac muscle cells are electrically excitable. Their membrane voltage rises and falls in an action potential. That electrical event triggers calcium release inside the cell, and calcium drives contraction.

At the whole-heart scale, electrical excitation normally travels through a conduction system and then through billions of muscle cells. The wave coordinates contraction so the heart pumps blood rather than twitching incoherently.

Fibrillation Is A Wave Problem

Atrial fibrillation affects the atria and can become chronic. Ventricular fibrillation affects the main pumping chambers and can be immediately life-threatening because the heart no longer pumps effectively.

The talk describes fibrillation as disorganized electrical waves. A normal beat is like one organized wave. A dangerous rhythm can involve rotating spiral or scroll waves, breakup, and spatio-temporal chaos.

normal organized wave
spiral or scroll wave

chaotic pumping failure

Reaction-Diffusion Models Give The First Picture

Cardiac wave models often use reaction-diffusion equations. The reaction part describes local excitability: a cell fires, becomes refractory, and then recovers. The diffusion part describes excitation spreading to neighboring cells.

The fire-front analogy is useful. Dry grass is excitable tissue. The flame front is the action-potential wave. Burned grass is refractory tissue that cannot immediately burn again. A healthy beat should pass once and die out at the boundary.

Clinical Electrical Mapping Is Powerful But Limited

Catheter mapping can measure electrical activity inside a patient. But it is invasive, point-by-point, time-consuming, and works best when the abnormal rhythm is stable enough to map.

Chaotic rhythms are harder. If the wave pattern changes quickly, a sparse catheter path may not capture the full picture. The talk uses this limitation to motivate AI methods that infer more complete maps from limited data.

Optical Mapping Shows The Waves In The Lab

In ex vivo experiments, the lab can keep isolated hearts alive for hours using perfusion. Multiple high-speed cameras image the heart surface while fluorescent dyes report voltage changes. Ultrasound can also record three-dimensional motion.

This setup creates rare paired data: electrical activity and mechanical deformation measured together. That pairing is exactly what machine learning needs if the goal is to learn how motion reveals hidden electrical activity.

isolated perfused heart

Spiral Waves Have Centers That Can Drive Arrhythmia

A spiral wave has an open end around which the wave rotates. The center is often tracked as a phase singularity. In a clinical ablation mindset, locating organizing centers matters because removing or interrupting the source can sometimes stop the abnormal rhythm.

But phase singularities are fragile in real data. Noise, sparse measurements, motion artifacts, and the fact that a surface view is only a projection of a 3D wave can create unstable or misleading detections.

U-Nets Can Recover Phase Maps From Sparse Snapshots

The first AI application uses U-Net-style encoder-decoder networks. The input is a short sequence of voltage measurements. The output is a phase map or the phase-singularity locations.

The striking result is that only a few snapshots can be enough. The network learns common wave patterns from many examples and can infer a useful phase map even when less than ten percent of the spatial information is available.

Simulation Training Can Transfer To Experiments

The talk reports that networks trained on simulation data can still perform reasonably on optical-mapping experiments from pig and rabbit hearts. That is not automatic, because simulated 2D dynamics and experimental 3D surface projections are not the same.

This matters because medical data is hard to collect. If simulation can teach the network the basic wave grammar, then experimental data can be used more strategically to validate, adapt, or fine-tune the model.

Surface Electrical Data Cannot See The Full 3D Wave

Voltage-sensitive optical imaging mostly measures the surface. Light from deeper tissue does not escape clearly enough to reconstruct the full interior. So the 3D scroll wave inside the wall is hidden.

The lab tested whether neural networks could infer a 3D wave pattern from 2D surface views in toy simulations. Surface layers are reconstructed best. Deeper layers become uncertain and blurrier. This is an important boundary: surface data contains clues, but not unlimited information.

Diffusion Models Improve When Given More Time

The group also tested diffusion models for reconstructing 3D wave patterns from surface information. With only a few frames, diffusion models performed similarly to U-Nets. With more frames covering more of a spiral period, they improved.

The reason is physical. The timing of waves reaching the surface carries information about what happened inside. More temporal context gives the model more clues about propagation speed, rotation, and hidden geometry.

The Second Strategy Uses Motion Instead Of Electrical Surface Only

The major pivot in the talk is to use mechanics. The heart visibly moves. Ultrasound and MRI can measure motion. If electrical activation causes contraction, then the motion field should contain information about the electrical wave that caused it.

The key question becomes: is the mapping from mechanics back to electrophysiology unique enough to be useful? The talk shows evidence that activation wavefronts align with characteristic strain and strain-rate patterns, with a small electromechanical delay.

electrical activation

A Physics-Based Synchronization Method Came First

Before deep learning, the group used a data-assimilation idea. One system is the measured heart. The second is a virtual copy. Sensors observe mechanics in the measured system, and controllers push the virtual electrical model until its mechanics synchronize with the observed mechanics.

This physics-based method can reconstruct complex wave topology with useful accuracy, but it is limited and computationally involved. The speaker says deep learning later worked much better for this inverse electromechanics task.

measured mechanical heart

Autoencoders Map Deformation To Electrical Waves

The deep-learning approach trains encoder-decoder networks on pairs: mechanical deformation patterns as input and electrical excitation patterns as target. In simulations, the networks can reconstruct turbulent 3D electrical wave patterns from tiny mechanical deformations.

The talk shows that filament structures computed from the reconstructed wave overlap well with the ground truth. That is the important test: not only does the image look similar, but the organizing structure of the arrhythmia is recovered.

The Patient Motivation Is Faster Activation Mapping

In a clinical ventricular tachycardia procedure, a catheter may spend a long time building an electrical activation map. If ultrasound motion could be converted into an activation map quickly, the physician could get a useful view much faster.

The talk does not claim this is already clinically solved. The patient data test did not work when a model trained only on simulation was applied directly. The current path is to generate better experimental training data from isolated hearts, then bridge toward patient data through adaptation and validation.

Training Data Must Cover Geometry, Scar, And Wave Types

The group generated many simulation cases by varying heart geometry, fiber architecture, electrical models, mechanical laws, initial conditions, stimulation sites, scars, heterogeneity, and model parameters. This matters because the model must not learn one ideal heart.

Scar tissue is especially important because many patients have myocardial infarcts or heterogeneous tissue. A method that only works in clean homogeneous hearts would miss the cases where mapping is often most needed.

The Q And A Clarifies The Hard Parts

The Q and A raises practical concerns. Patient-specific anatomy may matter, but ultrasound or MRI can provide at least global geometry. Sparse catheter layouts are constrained by device designs. Phase singularities can be unstable and should not be overinterpreted as the whole story of fibrillation.

Another open question is interpretability. The networks work, but the physical meaning of the latent space is not yet clear. The speaker describes this as an interesting direction, especially because electromechanical motion has global elastic effects, not just local wavefront motion.

How This Connects To Design And Topology

This lecture is a strong example of topology in a real physical system. The clinically important object is not only voltage magnitude. It is the wave organization: spirals, scrolls, filaments, reentry paths, breakup, and scars that anchor or block waves.

For design and topology thinking, the heart is a warning that the hidden state can be topological. A small surface measurement may not reveal the interior wave. A motion field may carry indirect information. A useful model must reconstruct the organizing structure, not just produce a pretty voltage picture.

The same lesson applies to sensor placement, actuator placement, and material-layout problems. If the decision depends on a hidden wave, vortex, crack front, thermal channel, or reentrant path, we need measurements and models that recover that hidden structure with uncertainty and clinical or engineering validation.

The Chapter In One Chain

First, the heart is an electromechanical pump: voltage triggers calcium, calcium triggers contraction, and coordinated waves create useful pumping. Second, fibrillation is a wave disorder where organized propagation becomes rotating, breaking, or chaotic. Third, reaction-diffusion models explain why excitable tissue can support waves, refractory wakes, spirals, and scrolls. Fourth, catheter mapping is clinically useful but invasive, sparse, slow, and hard for chaotic rhythms. Fifth, optical mapping and ultrasound in isolated hearts provide rare paired electrical and mechanical data. Sixth, phase singularities mark spiral centers but are fragile in noisy, sparse, or projected data. Seventh, U-Nets can infer phase maps and spiral centers from sparse voltage snapshots. Eighth, simulation-trained networks can partially transfer to pig and rabbit experiments. Ninth, surface electrical imaging cannot fully see hidden 3D waves, and uncertainty grows with depth. Tenth, diffusion models improve when given more time context. Eleventh, motion is a second source of information because electrical activation creates delayed strain patterns. Twelfth, a physics-based synchronization method can infer electrical waves from mechanics by driving a virtual heart model. Thirteenth, autoencoders can map deformation fields to electrical wave fields and recover filament structure in simulations. Fourteenth, patient translation aims at faster activation mapping from ultrasound motion, but simulation-only transfer is not enough. Fifteenth, training data must cover anatomy, fibers, scars, wave types, and heterogeneity. Sixteenth, design and topology should treat hidden organizing wave structure as the real object to recover before trusting an intervention.

The chapter's core idea: the heart's motion is a delayed shadow of its electrical waves. AI can use that shadow to infer hidden rhythm structure, but the method must respect anatomy, scars, sparse measurements, uncertainty, and the difference between lab validation and patient use.
