Turn Ground Wiggles Into Earthquake Decisions

Read This Chapter As One Claim

Machine learning matters here only if it turns continuous noisy ground motion into a better earthquake catalog. The catalog is the decision artifact: it says what happened, where it happened, how large it was, and what active structures it reveals.

Everyday problem Earthquake waves are hidden inside nonstop sensor noise from people, oceans, weather, instruments, and overlapping events.
Shared promise Denoise, pick P and S arrivals, associate picks into events, locate and size them, then build a deeper catalog.
Hidden danger Every serial threshold can throw away weak events, overlapping context, or uncertainty needed for later decisions.
First rejection test Reject the workflow if it only improves a model score and does not improve catalog completeness, false-event control, location quality, or decision evidence.

This chapter uses video 40, Greg Beroza's DDPS talk on complete machine-learning-based earthquake monitoring workflows. The talk is about a full scientific pipeline. The input is continuous ground motion from many sensors. The output is an earthquake catalog: when each earthquake happened, where it happened, how large it was, and what sequence it belongs to.

The first-principles problem is simple to state and hard to solve. The ground is always moving. Most of the recorded motion is noise from people, oceans, weather, instruments, and local disturbances. Hidden inside that stream are earthquake waves. The monitoring system must find the real events, ignore false signals, separate overlapping events, and do this fast enough to matter.

Why Earthquakes Are A Data Problem

Earthquake sensors record all the time because we do not know when earthquakes will happen. Networks can have one sensor or tens of thousands. They often sample around one hundred times per second. Over years, this becomes a huge archive.

Earthquakes also follow a power-law pattern: for every step down in magnitude, there are many more smaller events. The small events are easy to miss, but they carry a lot of information. If machine learning can find many more of them in the same raw data, the picture of the active fault system becomes much sharper.

sensors run all day

The Catalog Is The Scientific Product

A catalog is a list of discrete earthquakes inferred from continuous recordings. A standard catalog may include only the clearer events. A deep catalog uses better machine-learning processing to recover many more smaller events from the same data.

This matters because a catalog is not only a database. It is the map scientists and regulators use to understand what faults are active, how sequences migrate, whether injected fluids are changing the system, and whether a larger event may be more likely in one region than another.

detected events
locations and magnitudes
fault and hazard interpretation

The Old Trigger Is Too Crude

A traditional detector often uses a short-term average divided by a long-term average. If the short-term energy jumps enough, the system says a wave arrived. This can work for a clear sudden event.

But many real cases are harder. Small events may be buried in noise. Events may emerge gradually. Aftershock sequences and swarms may overlap in time. A crude threshold can miss weak events, split one event badly, or fire on noise.

weak events missed

Step One: Clean The Signal

The first machine-learning piece is deep denoising. The model sees a seismogram in time-frequency form and learns masks that separate earthquake signal from noise. Training examples are made by combining known earthquake signals with real noise at many signal-to-noise levels.

The goal is not to make the trace pretty. The goal is to recover the first shape of the earthquake wave so later steps can pick arrivals accurately and avoid false detections. The model also learns that some traces are all noise and should not be forced into a fake signal.

known signal plus real noise

Step Two: Pick P And S Arrivals

Earthquake waves arrive in phases. The P wave is usually first. The S wave comes later and is often harder to pick because other scattered waves may interfere. Picking means estimating the arrival time of each phase at each station.

PhaseNet treats this as a classification and timing problem. Given a three-component seismogram, it predicts P-wave probability, S-wave probability, and noise. It was trained on Northern California data but generalizes broadly. Its picks are close to expert analyst picks, especially compared with older automatic methods.

three-component waveform

Step Three: Associate Arrivals Into Events

Association asks which arrivals belong to the same earthquake. This is hard because the number of earthquakes is unknown. Small events may appear on only a few stations. Noise can create false picks. Earthquake swarms can overlap so that arrivals from different events are tangled together.

The talk describes a Gaussian-mixture-style associator. Instead of treating each pick as a hard fact, it treats arrival times probabilistically and clusters them into possible events. It can use both P and S waves and some amplitude information. This helps in dense aftershock sequences such as Ridgecrest, where many events happen close together in time.

many phase picks

Step Four: Locate And Size The Earthquake

Once arrivals are associated, the system estimates the earthquake location and magnitude. The basic idea is triangulation through wave travel times. If a wave reaches nearby stations earlier and farther stations later, the pattern says where the event likely started.

Depth is harder than map location because most stations sit at the surface. The talk's Q&A notes that nearby events can be located very precisely relative to each other, sometimes at meter-level horizontal precision and tens of meters in depth, but absolute accuracy is harder.

The Serial Workflow Loses Information

A normal workflow does one step, thresholds the result, then passes only the survivors to the next step. Denoise, pick, associate, locate. This modular approach is useful and easier to build, but every threshold can throw information away.

That is why the talk also discusses more integrated models. Earthquake Transformer combines waveform context with picking and detection. EQNet goes further by using the network context: nearby stations should see related arrivals with consistent time moveout. In the long run, Beroza expects end-to-end or all-in-one workflows to win, although modular workflows are easier to deploy first.

QuakeFlow Makes It Operational

QuakeFlow packages the workflow for cloud processing. It can process archives from the past and can also support streaming, near-real-time monitoring. Most of the heavy work is parallel because different waveform chunks and stations can be processed independently.

The talk gives practical examples. For Puerto Rico, a few years of data from dozens of stations could be processed cheaply in the cloud and produced roughly seven times more earthquakes than the prior catalog. For Hawaii, a short processing run found over ten times more earthquakes than the standard catalog, revealing deep and shallow structures tied to volcanic activity.

Deep Catalogs Change What Scientists Can See

When a catalog has many more small events, faults appear that were nearly invisible before. In Central Italy, the deep catalog had much lower magnitude completeness and roughly an order of magnitude more information. In Oklahoma and Kansas, hundreds of thousands of detected events revealed fault fabrics, conjugate angles, barriers to fluid flow, and structures that hosted larger events.

This is the scientific payoff. The machine-learning method is not the end product. The end product is a sharper view of the earth's active structures and how seismicity moves through them.

Induced Seismicity Needs Real-Time Detail

The Arkansas example shows why this matters for decisions. Earthquakes related to disposal wells and hydraulic fracturing can evolve in space and time. A deeper catalog revealed that a sequence previously treated as one process was actually more complicated, with activity tied to different wells and migration over time.

If regulators had that information in real time, they might have made different decisions about which wells to shut in. The point is not that the catalog predicts everything. The point is that better monitoring changes the decision state. It shows which structures are active and how activity is spreading.

injection changes pressure

Forecasting Is Still Careful

The talk is careful about prediction. Deep catalogs can show where many small earthquakes illuminate a fault before a larger event. In Oklahoma and Kansas, many magnitude-four-and-larger events occurred on structures that had already been lit up by smaller earthquakes. But most lit-up structures did not host a damaging event.

So the result is not "we can predict earthquakes." It is a probability gain. The background chance is low, but the chance is higher on certain active structures. That kind of information can still matter for risk decisions, especially when human activity is changing the system.

small events illuminate structures

Open Science Matters Here

PhaseNet and related tools are available and widely used. The talk mentions an emerging ecosystem of seismology data sets and toolboxes. This matters because earthquake monitoring is a public science problem. Different regions, volcanoes, induced sequences, and even Mars can benefit from shared tools and shared labels.

It also means catalogs will no longer be one-size-fits-all. There may be different catalogs for different purposes: authoritative public reporting, dense scientific analysis, induced-seismicity regulation, volcanic monitoring, or specialized research.

How This Connects To Earlier Chapters

This chapter is a real end-to-end scientific workflow. It uses deep denoising, classification, sequence context, association, cloud processing, and catalog analysis. It also echoes the scientific-contract theme: the model is useful only if the workflow output supports the scientific and operational decisions.

For topology, materials, and other physical design areas, the lesson is similar. Do not only build a good single predictor. Build the full chain from raw measurement to decision artifact, and watch where each threshold loses information.

The Chapter In One Chain

First, earthquake monitoring starts with continuous noisy ground-motion records. Second, the goal is a catalog of discrete events with times, locations, magnitudes, and sequence context. Third, small earthquakes are numerous and scientifically valuable, but standard catalogs miss many of them. Fourth, deep denoising separates signal from real noise. Fifth, PhaseNet picks P and S arrival times more accurately than older automatic pickers. Sixth, association groups arrivals into events even when events overlap. Seventh, location and magnitude estimation turn those events into catalog entries. Eighth, serial workflows are practical but lose information at thresholds, so integrated models such as Earthquake Transformer and EQNet use more context. Ninth, QuakeFlow makes archive and near-real-time processing practical at scale. Tenth, deep catalogs reveal faults, migration, induced-seismicity processes, and probability gains that can support better scientific and regulatory decisions.

The chapter's core idea: machine learning is valuable here because it turns the same continuous noisy sensor data into a much sharper earthquake catalog. The catalog, not the neural network, is the thing that changes what scientists and decision-makers can see.
