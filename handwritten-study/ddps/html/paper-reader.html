Paper Reader Worksheet

This page turns the whole handwritten course into a reading protocol. Use it when you open a new scientific machine-learning paper and do not want to drown in method names. Fill these boxes in ordinary words before trusting the paper's claim.

claim
physical burden
evidence
rejection test

1. What Is The Paper Claiming?

Write the claim without adjectives. Avoid words like robust, scalable, accurate, general, or foundation until the paper proves them.

Good sentence: The model predicts pressure fields for new geometries faster than the full solver.

Weak sentence: The paper proposes a powerful AI framework for physics.

2. What Physical Pain Created The Method?

Name the real problem. Is the full solver too slow? Are observations sparse? Is the geometry changing? Is the state hidden? Is the system chaotic? Is uncertainty the decision blocker?

If there is no physical pain, the method may be an algorithm looking for a reason to exist.

3. What Burden Does Learning Carry?

Learning might carry the equation residual, a reduced coordinate system, a field-to-field operator, a sensor-to-state map, a controller, a prior distribution, a posterior update, or an uncertainty estimate.

Do not let the paper hide behind "we use a neural network." Ask what job the network is actually doing.

4. What Evidence Supports The Claim?

Separate evidence types. Training data is not validation. A pretty plot is not a stress test. A benchmark is not proof of physical transfer unless the benchmark changes the thing the paper claims to handle.

Look for changed geometry, changed parameters, changed grid, changed boundary condition, longer rollout, noisier data, unseen regime, or real experimental data.

5. What Is The Honest Baseline?

A method should be compared to the right alternative. Sometimes the baseline is the full solver. Sometimes it is a classical ROM, Kalman filter, adjoint method, Gaussian process, POD model, finite-volume solver, or simpler neural network.

If the baseline is weak, the win may be smaller than the paper makes it sound.

6. What Physical Rule Must Not Break?

Write the rule in plain words. Mass should not vanish. Energy should not grow without cause. A fracture should stay connected if the simulator needs connectivity. A controller should not violate safety. A probability estimate should cover the true outcome at the promised rate.

This is the rule you check before believing speed or accuracy numbers.

7. What Changed Case Would Expose The Shortcut?

Every serious claim needs a changed-case test. Change the geometry, resolution, Reynolds number, material, initial condition, sensor layout, noise level, forcing, time horizon, or regime. The right change depends on the paper's promise.

If the paper claims generalization but only tests nearby samples, write "generalization not proved."

8. What Uncertainty Is Missing?

Ask which doubt sources are included: data noise, parameter uncertainty, model-form error, architecture choice, discretization, training randomness, out-of-distribution inputs, and future forcing.

A single error bar is not enough if it ignores the uncertainty that controls the decision.

9. What Decision Would This Support?

Name the action. Design a wing. Place a sensor. Inject CO2. Control a PDE. Choose a material. Trust a digital twin. Run the full solver only when needed.

If no decision changes, the method may still be interesting, but its practical value is less clear.

10. Final Reader Verdict

End with one of these sentences.

Believe locallyThe paper proves the claim only for the tested regime.
Promising but unprovedThe idea is useful, but the changed-case evidence is missing.
Reject for nowThe method breaks the physical rule or lacks a fair baseline.
Trust for actionThe evidence covers the decision, uncertainty, and failure boundary.
