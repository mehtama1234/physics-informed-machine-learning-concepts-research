Narrative chapter

Build The Right Latent Language For Physics

This chapter uses video 129, Johannes Brandstetter's DDPS talk on why the next wave of scientific breakthroughs may happen in latent space. The talk is not mainly about one architecture. It is about representation. Text models became powerful only after people learned how to turn text into useful tokens. Scientific simulations still do not have an equally settled token language.

The first-principles question is simple: what should a machine learning model actually see when the object is a flow around a Formula 1 car, a million particles in a hopper, a changing mesh, a fluid-particle mixture, a molecule, a black-hole merger, or a five-dimensional fusion simulation? If the input is represented badly, the model spends its capacity fighting the representation instead of learning the physics.

Read This Chapter As One Claim

The most important choice may happen before the neural network starts predicting. You must decide what language the physical object will be written in. A mesh, particle cloud, molecule, field, CAD shape, and time sequence do not all want the same tokens. A good latent language keeps the facts the science cannot lose: geometry, local activity, atom identity, contact, coupling, scale, time, and the output the decision needs. A bad latent language can make a powerful model learn the wrong thing efficiently.

The Everyday Version

Imagine trying to describe a factory machine to someone over the phone. You could read every bolt coordinate, every scratch, and every vibration sample. That would be complete, but almost useless. A better description compresses the machine into the parts that matter: which pieces move, which forces interact, where heat builds up, and what state the process is in.

A latent space is that kind of compressed description. It is not just a smaller file. It is a working language. The hard part is making the language keep the facts the science needs: geometry, conservation, contact, time, scale, material identity, and the difference between real structure and disposable detail.

Why Engineering Data Is Different

In ordinary images, nearby pixels are often a reasonable starting point. In language, tokens are words or word pieces. In engineering simulations, the situation is stranger. A car-flow simulation may have a billion mesh cells. A particle simulation may have hundreds of thousands of moving grains. A multiphysics problem may have particles, fluid, solid geometry, and forces all interacting at once.

The ratio between data size and useful information can be very different from images or weather grids. Some regions have many mesh cells because the physics changes sharply there. Other regions are coarse because little happens. Geometry is not a background detail. It can decide the solution. Sometimes geometry itself changes over time as a material bends, fills, or breaks.

mesh density changes with physics
geometry controls the solution
particles and fields interact
tokenization must respect all of this

The Old Numerical Split

The speaker frames numerical simulation as two linked tasks. First, approximate the spatial physics: gradients, fluxes, accelerations, diffusion, convection, and other operators. Second, update the system forward in time. Many early scientific machine learning models copied this split. They used a neural network to predict a right-hand side, such as an acceleration or derivative, and then fed it into a time-stepper.

That can work, but the talk shows why it can become fragile. If the model predicts one small interaction wrong, the time update carries that error forward. The next prediction starts from a slightly wrong state, then the error compounds. In particle systems with complex geometry, contact, and surfaces, this can produce failure quickly.

network predicts acceleration or derivative
numerical update advances time
small interaction error is carried forward
rollout can become unstable

The Shift To State Prediction

The next community move was to predict the next state more directly. Weather models such as GraphCast and related systems showed that neural networks can learn powerful step-to-step simulators at large scale. This is a major success, but the speaker argues that many engineering problems still demand a more careful representation because geometry, mesh structure, particles, and multiphysics interactions are central.

So the question becomes not only "what network predicts the next state?" It becomes "what compact state should be predicted at all?" This is where latent-space modeling enters.

The Encoder And Decoder Become Separate Tools

The talk draws inspiration from stable diffusion. The important lesson is not the ability to make images. The important lesson is the separation of representation from generation. An encoder compresses a scene into a latent state. A decoder reconstructs from that latent state. The heavy modeling happens in the latent space, where the data is smaller and better organized.

For physics, this separation is attractive. You can spend effort building an encoder that knows how to read particles, meshes, fields, geometry, or multiple modalities. Then you can run temporal or generative models on the latent state. The decoder can return whatever output is needed: a field, a surface quantity, a trajectory, a force, or a diagnostic.

encoder reads raw scientific object
latent state stores useful information
model evolves or refines latent state
decoder returns requested physical output

Subsampling Is A Scientific Choice

The speaker argues that many simulation datasets can be heavily subsampled, sometimes drastically, because the raw number of points is much larger than the number of important degrees of freedom. But subsampling is not solved. Random sampling from an adaptive mesh may already preserve more samples where the mesh is fine, because fine mesh usually marks regions where the solution changes rapidly. Still, the right amount of subsampling is an open research question.

This is a first-principles warning. Compression is useful only if it throws away the right things. Throw away redundant points and you gain speed. Throw away the feature that controls the physics and the latent state becomes misleading.

raw simulation may be huge
many points may be redundant
sampling should keep active regions
too much compression loses science

Local Then Global Aggregation

A practical latent encoder cannot let every point talk to every other point immediately. Very large simulations would become too expensive. The talk describes a two-stage picture. First, aggregate local information into representative nodes or local champions. Then let those compressed tokens interact globally. This resembles how a simulation domain might be distributed across GPUs: local neighborhoods are processed first, and global interaction happens after compression.

The point is to create tokens that are not arbitrary pixels. They carry local physical context before entering the global latent model. A good token knows something about its nearby geometry, field values, particles, or mesh region.

subsample raw points
aggregate local neighborhoods
create compact latent tokens
let global interactions happen later

Rethinking Time

The talk questions whether always predicting the next time step is the right mental model. Autoregressive rollout can accumulate errors. Video diffusion suggests another view: model a chunk of time together and iteratively refine it. Instead of saying "next, next, next," the model can see past and future positions inside a time window and make them consistent.

This is why the speaker connects latent modeling to spacetime thinking. A trajectory can be treated less like a frame-by-frame movie and more like a whole path that must be aligned. For physics, that may mean generating or refining a time block in latent space so the whole evolution is coherent.

autoregressive rollout predicts one step
errors accumulate over steps
latent time block sees a window
iterative refinement aligns the trajectory

Black-Hole Data Shows Why Compression Matters

The talk mentions black-hole merger simulations from general relativity. One simulation can produce enormous data, with adaptive meshes and difficult derivative information near singular regions. A neural-field representation can compress the simulation and still allow physical quantities to be reconstructed with automatic differentiation.

The broad lesson is that compression can be more than storage. If the latent or neural-field representation is differentiable and faithful, it can become a working object for science. You can query it, differentiate it, and extract physics from it without carrying every raw mesh value around.

Particles Can Sometimes Become Fields

The speaker describes a particle hopper problem with hundreds of thousands of grains. Earlier approaches tried to model each particle acceleration and then update each particle. The newer view treats the particles as samples from fields, such as an occupancy field and a displacement field. The neural network predicts those fields instead of tracking every particle interaction directly.

This works when the bulk behavior matters more than the identity of each particle. If seven grains are added to three hundred thousand, the overall flow may barely change. In that setting, a field representation can be natural and much easier for the network.

many particles move together
individual identity may not matter
occupancy field says where mass is
displacement field says how it moves

But Molecules Need Identity

Molecular dynamics is different. In molecular modeling, atoms are not disposable grains in a bulk field. Their identities matter. A carbon atom and a hydrogen atom cannot be blurred into the same anonymous mass. A graph neural network naturally keeps atom identities because each atom is a node. A naive latent field might destroy that identity and break the physics.

The talk describes an ID-based trick. Each atom carries an ID or type information into the encoder, and the decoder is asked to reconstruct positions based on the requested IDs. This keeps some of the identity-preserving bias while still using a latent representation for trajectory modeling. The lesson is important: the representation must match what the physical system cannot afford to forget.

atoms are not anonymous mass
identity affects forces and chemistry
encoder receives atom IDs
decoder returns positions for those IDs

Multiphysics Can Meet In Latent Space

The fluid-particle example shows another reason latent spaces are attractive. Particles and fluids are different kinds of objects. A particle system may be encoded one way. A fluid field may be encoded another way. Once both are in latent form, their tokens can interact. The model can learn the coupling in a common representational space.

The speaker extends this idea to multiscale problems in the Q&A. Different scales can be treated like different modalities. Each scale can have its own encoder, and the latent tokens from those scales can interact. That is a concrete way to think about multiscale learning without forcing every scale into one raw grid.

particles get one encoding
fluid gets another encoding
latent tokens interact
decoder returns coupled behavior

The Mesh And Design Connection

This chapter connects strongly to geometry and topology design. In CFD, creating the mesh can be as painful as running the simulation. The mesh encodes the car, wing, duct, hole, surface, gap, and wake region. If a surrogate requires a perfect simulation mesh every time, it has not removed the full design bottleneck.

A latent-space approach can decouple what you encode from what you decode. The input might be a simulation mesh, a CAD geometry, a coarse point cloud, or a language-described shape. The output might be surface pressure, drag and lift coefficients, or a volume field around the object. If this works, a design loop can explore shapes without rebuilding the same heavy representation every time.

design geometry may arrive as CAD or mesh
encoder builds common latent state
decoder answers surface or volume questions
design loop avoids repeated meshing pain

Fusion Shows The High-Dimensional End

The talk closes with nuclear fusion turbulence, where gyrokinetic models can involve several spatial and velocity dimensions. The raw state is enormous. In such problems, the case for latent modeling becomes stronger because direct modeling of the full object is too large, while the scientific need for prediction and understanding remains urgent.

The harder the simulation object becomes, the more important the representation becomes. The latent space is not a convenience layer. It may be the only place where the model can afford to reason.

The Boundary Of The Claim

The speaker is careful that this is a personal view, not a solved recipe. The right scientific tokenizer is not known. The right subsampling level is not known in general. Good latent encoders are problem-dependent and can be difficult to build. Out-of-distribution behavior must be studied again in this new representation. Autoregressive time modeling may not be enough, but the best replacement is still an active research question.

So the claim is not "latent space solves all scientific simulation." The claim is: for many engineering and physics problems, the raw representation is the bottleneck. If we build faithful encoders and decoders, then latent space becomes a place where geometry, fields, particles, time, and multiple physics can interact at a tractable scale.

not a solved universal tokenizer
compression can lose key physics
OOD behavior must be retested
faithful latent states can unlock modeling

What You Should Remember

The chapter's main lesson is that representation is a scientific decision. Before asking which model is biggest, ask what the model is allowed to see. A physics model may need tokens for geometry, local fields, particles, atom IDs, scales, time chunks, and interactions. A bad representation makes the learning problem harder than it needs to be.

Across the DDPS course, this chapter ties together reduced models, neural operators, generative modeling, simulation surrogates, topology, and design. It argues that the next useful breakthroughs may come from learning the right latent language for scientific objects, then doing the prediction, refinement, coupling, and design work inside that language.
